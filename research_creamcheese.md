## 2. BRAND HISTORIES (with receipts)

### 2.1 PHILADELPHIA

**Invention — 1872, Chester, New York (not Philadelphia).**
- "William Lawrence, a dairyman from Chester, New York, attempted to make Neufchâtel" in 1872 and ended up with something richer — Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_Cream_Cheese
- VinePair: "William Lawrence, a New York dairy farmer" had "originally purchased an operating Neufchâtel factory in Chester, N.Y." and "added more cream to his traditional Neufchâtel recipe" — https://vinepair.com/cocktail-chatter/philadelphia-cream-cheese-not-invented-in-philadelphia/
- ⚠️ Date friction to acknowledge in script: The Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia (scholarly) says Lawrence "opened one of these factories in Orange County, New York, in 1862" and dates the cream cheese itself to 1875 — https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/essays/philadelphia-cream-cheese/. Safest on-screen line: "invented in the early 1870s in Chester, New York."

**The name was pure marketing.**
- "In 1880, he joined forces with distributor Alvah Reynolds (1830–1925), who came up with a brand that he felt would associate Lawrence's product with high-quality foods: Philadelphia Cream Cheese." — Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia, https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/essays/philadelphia-cream-cheese/
- "At the time, Philadelphia and its surrounding area had a reputation for high-quality dairy farms and creamier cheese products, so they decided to use the name 'Philadelphia' on the foil-wrapped blocks." — Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_Cream_Cheese

**Phenix Cheese Company → Kraft, 1928.**
- "Reynolds himself manufactured Philadelphia Cream Cheese from 1882 until 1903, then sold the brand to the Phenix Cheese Company of New York." — https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/essays/philadelphia-cream-cheese/
- "In 1928, Phenix merged with Kraft to form the Kraft-Phenix Cheese Company." — Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_Cream_Cheese
- "In 1928 Kraft merged with Phenix Cheese Corporation, the maker of Philadelphia Brand cream cheese. The newly formed Kraft-Phenix Cheese Corporation had captured 40 percent of the nation's cheese market by 1930 and boasted operations in Canada, Australia, Britain, and Germany." — Encyclopedia.com (International Directory of Company Histories), https://www.encyclopedia.com/books/politics-and-business-magazines/kraft-general-foods-inc-0

**Kraft's Canadian roots — the founder was born in Ontario.**
- From the Canada Heirloom Series (archived by Library and Archives Canada): "When James Lewis Kraft was born in 1875 in Stevensville, Ontario, near Fort Erie, one did not anticipate that he would drastically change the manufacture of cheese." And: "In 1920, Kraft entered the Canadian market by purchasing MacLaren's Imperial Cheese Co. Ltd. It began selling in Canada, on a national scale, process cheese in tins as well as loaves. One year later a factory was opened in downtown Montreal." — http://epe.lac-bac.gc.ca/100/205/301/ic/cdc/heirloom_series/volume4/350-351.html
- ⚠️ Birth-year friction: Wikipedia gives December 11, 1874 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_L._Kraft). Use "born in Stevensville, Ontario, in the mid-1870s" or pick one and footnote.

**Where Philadelphia is made for Canada today: Montreal.**
- Kraft Heinz Canada president Simon Laroche, on the 2026 $250-million plant investment: "From Kraft Dinner to Philadelphia Cream Cheese and Kraft Peanut Butter, our Mont Royal facility plays a vital role in bringing these household staples to tables across the country." The article adds "The facility employs more than 1,000 workers" and "The project builds on more than 120 years of Kraft Heinz history in Canada." — Food Processing, https://www.foodprocessing.com/business-of-food-beverage/capital-spending/news/55365941/kraft-heinz-canada-commits-to-significant-upgrades-to-mont-royal-plant-in-montreal (original release: Businesswire, https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260319488425/en/Kraft-Heinz-to-Invest-$250-Million-in-Montreals-Mont-Royal-Factory-to-Strengthen-Canadian-Manufacturing-Capacity)
- Note: **Ingleside, Ontario was Kraft's natural-cheese plant (Cracker Barrel, P'tit Québec), not the Philadelphia plant, and it was sold to Parmalat in the 2018–19 deal** — see The Globe and Mail, "Kraft Heinz to sell Canadian natural cheese operations to Parmalat for $1.62-billion," https://www.theglobeandmail.com/business/article-kraft-heinz-to-sell-natural-cheese-operations-in-canada-for-16/. Don't say Philly was made in Ingleside.

**The angel ads — a genuinely Canadian campaign.**
- "In 1994 JWT launched a campaign in Canada featuring angels who humorously find ways to make 'Philly' part of their angelic lives." Slogan: "When it's Philadelphia, it's Heaven." "There were five spots between 1994 and 1997: Heaven, Crumbs, Empty, Midnight and Cloud." — Postkiwi (ad-history blog), https://www.postkiwi.com/2005/philadelphia-cream-cheese-angels/
- The angel was Canadian actress Linda Kash: "It was in 1994 that she would inhabit her best-known Canadian role as the Kraft Philadelphia Cream Cheese Angel… These commercials became one of the most successful advertising campaigns in Canadian history, and Kash continues to play her Angel role more than 15 years after its debut." — The Canadian Encyclopedia, https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/linda-kash
- Bonus interview for clip licensing/context: CBC Radio q, "Philly Cream Cheese Angel Linda Kash remembers her Seinfeld audition," https://www.cbc.ca/radio/q/tuesday-march-9-2021-gabriel-byrne-linda-kash-1.5940691/philly-cream-cheese-angel-linda-kash-remembers-her-seinfeld-audition-1.5942171

### 2.2 CHEEZ WHIZ

- **Invention/launch:** "Cheez Whiz was first developed as a pre-prepared cheese sauce for the traditional British dish Welsh rarebit, by a team at Kraft (U.S.) including Edwin Traisman." And: "It was developed by a team led by food scientist Edwin Traisman (1915–2007)." "It was introduced in the United Kingdom in 1952, the same year it was first marketed in the United States." — Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheez_Whiz. Most popular histories give the US national launch as July 1953 (e.g., Back Then History, https://www.backthenhistory.com/articles/the-history-of-cheez-whiz). Safest line: "developed 1950–52, on shelves everywhere by 1953."
- **Traisman trivia:** he also later "would develop the way McDonald's flash froze its French fries" (as summarized from Today I Found Out, https://www.todayifoundout.com/index.php/2015/01/cheese-cheez-whiz/) — attribute loosely ("food-history sites love to point out…").
- **Canadian production is domestic — verified:** "Canadian Cheez Whiz is manufactured at the Kraft processing plant in Mount Royal, Quebec." — Tasting Table, https://www.tastingtable.com/2104125/is-spray-cheez-whiz-banned-canada/
- **Canadian formulation differs from the US jar:** "American Cheez Whiz contains no actual cheese. Instead, it is made with whey, canola oil, milk protein concentrate, and maltodextrin, among other ingredients. Canadian Cheez Whiz is made with modified milk ingredients, water, vegetable oil, and cheese, as well as other additions." — same Tasting Table URL. (Frame as ingredient/regulatory difference only — no health commentary.)
- **Place in Canadian culture:** "Canadian Cheez Whiz is typically served on toast or even on celery sticks." — same Tasting Table URL. Chatelaine has also covered Cheez Whiz as a nostalgia food ("The Best Way To Eat Cheez Whiz And Not Hate It," https://chatelaine.com/food/trends/cheez-whiz/ — page blocks robots; writer should pull the quote manually).
- **Myth to debunk on camera:** "Have you ever heard that Cheez Whiz is banned in Canada? … it's been available to both Americans and Canadians this whole time." What Canada never had is the spray can: "what Canadian stores lack is Cheez Whiz in a can – or any kind of spray cheese, for that matter." — same Tasting Table URL.

## PART 1 — THE CORE LEGAL ENGINE: what may be called "cream cheese," verbatim

All of the following standards are quoted verbatim from **Canadian Food Compositional Standards, Volume 7 – Dairy Products** (single-page document, navigate to Volume 7): https://inspection.canada.ca/en/about-cfia/acts-and-regulations/list-acts-and-regulations/documents-incorporated-reference/canadian-food-compositional-standards-0

### 1.1 Cream Cheese (s. 7.4.7; formerly B.08.035)

> "**7.4.7 Cream Cheese** is the product made by coagulating cream with the aid of bacteria to form a curd and forming the curd into a homogeneous mass after draining the whey; (a) contains (i) not more than 55% moisture, and (ii) not less than 30% milk fat; and (b) may contain (i) cream added to adjust the milk fat content, and (ii) salt."

**The two magic numbers: minimum 30% milk fat, maximum 55% moisture.** And note how short the permitted-ingredients list is: cream, and salt. (Stabilizer gums and preservatives appear on real labels because they are separately permitted as food additives — see 2.3 below.)

### 1.2 Cream Cheese with (naming the added ingredients) (s. 7.4.8; formerly B.08.037)

> "**7.4.8 Cream Cheese with (naming the added ingredients)** is the product made by coagulating cream with the aid of bacteria to form a curd and forming the curd into a homogeneous mass after draining the whey; (a) contains the named added ingredients which is one or more of the following ingredients in amounts sufficient to differentiate the product from cream cheese but not in amounts so large as to change the basic nature of the product (i) cheese other than cream cheese, (ii) chocolate, condiments, flavouring preparations, seasonings or spices, (iii) fruits, nuts, pickles, relishes or vegetables, (iv) prepared meat or preserved meat as set out in Volume 13, or (v) prepared fish or meat, or preserved fish or meat as set out in Volume 18; (b) contains (i) not more than 60% moisture, and (ii) not less than 26% milk fat; and (c) may contain (i) cream added to adjust the milk fat content, and (ii) salt."

### 1.3 Cream Cheese Spread (s. 7.4.9; formerly B.08.038)

> "**7.4.9 Cream Cheese Spread** is the product made by coagulating cream with the aid of bacteria to form a curd and forming the curd into a homogeneous mass after draining the whey; (a) contains (i) added milk and milk products, (ii) not less than 51% cream cheese, (iii) not more than 60% moisture, and (iv) not less than 24% milk fat; and (b) may contain (i) cream added to adjust the milk fat content, and (ii) salt, vinegar and sweetening agents."

**Key on-screen fact: a "cream cheese spread" must still be at least 51% actual cream cheese.**

### 1.4 Cream Cheese Spread with (naming the added ingredients) (s. 7.4.10; formerly B.08.039)

> "**7.4.10 Cream Cheese Spread with (naming the added ingredients)** is the product made by coagulating cream with the aid of bacteria to form a curd and forming the curd into a homogeneous mass after draining the whey; (a) contains the named added ingredients … (b) contains (i) added milk and milk products, (ii) not more than 60% moisture, and (ii) [sic — numbering as published] not less than 24% milk fat; and (c) may contain (i) cream added to adjust the milk fat content, (ii) salt, vinegar and sweetening agents."

*(The doubled "(ii)" is in the CFIA text as published — a quotable curiosity, if you want it.)*

### 1.5 Processed Cheese Spread — Cheez Whiz territory (s. 7.4.15; formerly B.08.041.3)

> "**7.4.15 Processed Cheese Spread** (1) is the product made by comminuting and mixing one or more varieties of cheese, other than cream cheese, cottage cheese or whey cheese, into a homogeneous mass with the aid of heat; (a) contains (i) added milk or milk products, (ii) not less than 51% cheese, (iii) not more than 60% moisture, and (iv) not less than 20% milk fat; and (b) may contain (i) water added to adjust the moisture content, (ii) added milk fat, and (iii) salt, vinegar and sweetening agents."

Also in the family (same URL): **7.4.13 Processed Cheese Food** ("not less than 51% cheese… not more than 46% moisture, and… not less than 23% milk fat"); **7.4.14 Processed Cheese Food with (naming the added ingredients)** ("not more than 46% moisture, and… not less than 22% milk fat"); **7.4.16 Processed Cheese Spread with (naming the added ingredients)** ("not more than 60% moisture, and… not less than 20% milk fat"). A labelling note in Volume 7's interpretation section:

> "The word 'process' may be used in the place of the word 'processed' in the following common names: processed (naming the variety) cheese, processed (naming the variety) cheese with (naming the added ingredients), processed cheese food, processed cheese food with (naming the added ingredients), processed cheese spread, and processed cheese spread with (naming the added ingredients)."

### 1.6 Cold-Pack Cheese — MacLaren's territory (ss. 7.4.17–7.4.20; formerly B.08.041.5–B.08.041.8)

> "**7.4.17 Cold-Pack (naming the variety) Cheese** (1) subject to paragraph (a), is the product made by comminuting and mixing the named variety or varieties of cheese, other than cream cheese, cottage cheese or whey cheese, into a homogeneous mass **without the aid of heat**; (a) in the case of cold-pack cheddar cheese, is the product made by comminuting and mixing, into a homogeneous mass without the aid of heat, one or more of the following (i) cheddar cheese, (ii) stirred curd cheese, (iii) granular curd cheese, or (iv) washed curd cheese; (b) contains, where it is made from (i) one variety of cheese, not more moisture and not less milk fat than the maximum moisture content and minimum fat content permitted for that variety…" *(emphasis added)*

So "cold-pack cheddar cheese" must match cheddar's own numbers, which are:

> "**7.4.3 Cheddar Cheese** … (c) contains … (i) not more than 39% moisture, and (ii) not less than 31% milk fat"

And the softer cousins:

> "**7.4.19 Cold-Pack Cheese Food** (1) is the product made by comminuting and mixing one or more varieties of cheese … without the aid of heat; (a) contains (i) added milk or milk products, (ii) not less than 51% cheese, (iii) not more than 46% moisture, and (iv) not less than 23% milk fat…"

**7.4.20 Cold-Pack Cheese Food with (naming the added ingredients)**: "not more than 46% moisture, and… not less than 22% milk fat."

## PART 2 — WHAT "CREAM CHEESE PRODUCT" MEANS LEGALLY (non-standardized names)

### 2.1 The rules

**Rule 1 — if you use the standardized name, you must meet the standard (FDR B.01.042):**

> "If a standard for a food is set out in the Food Compositional Standards Document: (a) the food must contain only the ingredients set out in the standard for the food; (b) the food must contain each ingredient in a quantity within any limits set out in the standard for that ingredient; (c) the food must be labelled and advertised in accordance with any requirements set out in the standard; and (d) the food must comply with any other requirements set out in the standard in relation to the composition, strength, potency, purity, quality or other property of the food."
> — FDR s. B.01.042 (as amended by SOR/2024-244, s. 13), https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/C.R.C.,_c._870/section-B.01.042.html

**Rule 2 — the companion rule for substances required/permitted elsewhere in the FDR (FDR B.01.043):**

> "Despite paragraph B.01.042(a), if a substance — other than a supplemental ingredient — is required or permitted by a provision of these Regulations in a food for which a standard is set out in the Food Compositional Standards Document, that food must contain the required substance and may contain the permitted substance."
> — FDR s. B.01.043, https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/C.R.C.,_c._870/section-B.01.043.html

**Rule 3 — the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations trade rule (SFCR s. 201):**

> "A food, whether prepackaged or not, that is sent or conveyed from one province to another or that is imported or exported, and whose label bears a common name printed in boldface type, but not in italics, in the Standards of Identity Document must meet any standard that applies in respect of that common name."
> — SFCR s. 201, https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-2018-108/section-201.html
> *(Note for the record: the task brief's guess of "SFCR s. 262" is wrong — s. 262 is about prepackaged fish. The naming rule is s. 201.)*

**Rule 4 — the Act itself (Food and Drugs Act, s. 6):**

> "Where a standard for a food has been prescribed, no person shall (a) import into Canada, (b) send, convey or receive for conveyance from one province to another, or (c) have in possession for the purpose of sending or conveying from one province to another any article that is intended for sale and that is likely to be mistaken for that food unless the article complies with the prescribed standard."
> and, under the marginal note "Labelling, etc., of food that is imported or moved interprovincially":
> "Where a standard for a food has been prescribed, no person shall label, package, sell or advertise any article that (a) has been imported into Canada, (b) has been sent or conveyed from one province to another, or (c) is intended to be sent or conveyed from one province to another in such a manner that it is likely to be mistaken for that food unless the article complies with the prescribed standard."
> — Food and Drugs Act, s. 6, https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/F-27/section-6.html

**CFIA guidance (Industry Labelling Tool, common name page):**

> "A modified common name of a standardized food may not be used to describe a food that does not meet that standard, unless the following conditions are met" — the conditions being that it is clear to consumers the food doesn't meet the standard and how it differs.
> — CFIA, Common name, https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/common-name

And the current FDR definition of "common name" (para. (a)):

> "the name of the food printed in boldface type, but not in italics, in a section of the Food Compositional Standards Document"
> — FDR s. B.01.001(1) "common name", https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/C.R.C.,_c._870/section-B.01.001.html

### 2.2 The perfect real-world exhibit: Kraft's own two Philadelphias

> — https://www.kraftheinz.com/en-CA/philadelphia/products/00068100011258-original-brick-cream-cheese

- **"Original Cream Cheese Product"** (Philadelphia soft tub, Canada) — ingredients, verbatim:
> "MILK, CREAM, MODIFIED MILK INGREDIENTS, SALT, BACTERIAL CULTURE, LACTIC ACID, GUAR GUM AND/OR CAROB BEAN GUM, POTASSIUM SORBATE."
> — https://www.kraftheinz.com/en-CA/philadelphia/products/00068100895971-original-cream-cheese-product

Kraft Heinz Canada also lists **"Whipped Original Cream Cheese Product"** (https://www.kraftheinz.com/en-CA/philadelphia/products/00068100896510-whipped-original-cream-cheese-product) and **"Light Cream Cheese Product"** (https://www.kraftheinz.com/en-CA/philadelphia/products/00068100011173-light-cream-cheese-spread). **The legal mechanics, stated neutrally:** the cream cheese standard (7.4.7) permits, beyond the cream-derived curd, only "cream added to adjust the milk fat content" and "salt" as ingredients. A formulation containing other dairy ingredients (e.g. "modified milk ingredients") or falling outside the 30%-fat/55%-moisture box is not the standardized food, and under B.01.042 / SFCR 201 / FDA s. 6 cannot be sold under the bare name "cream cheese" — which is how names like **"cream cheese product"** arise. "Cream cheese product" is not a defined standard anywhere in the FCSD; it is a non-standardized food name. (The episode can show the two Philadelphia packages side by side and let the names speak.)

### 2.3 Why the brick still has gums in it (what the law permits)

— Health Canada, List of Permitted Emulsifying, Gelling, Stabilizing or Thickening Agents, https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/food-safety/food-additives/lists-permitted/4-emulsifying-gelling-stabilizing-thickening-agents.html

### 3.1 The rule and the compliance date

> "B.01.350 (1) Except as otherwise provided in this section, the principal display panel of a prepackaged product must carry a symbol that is set out in Schedule K.1 if (a) the product, as offered for sale, contains a nutrient that is set out in column 1 of the table to this section; and (b) the amount of the nutrient, calculated as a percentage of the daily value, meets or exceeds the applicable threshold set out in columns 2 to 7 of that table.
> (2) For the purposes of subsection (1), the percentage of the daily value for the nutrient is calculated on the basis of the amount of the nutrient, by weight, **per serving of stated size or per reference amount, whichever is greater**."

> — CFIA, Food labelling implementation plan, https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/implementation-plan

### 3.2 Which threshold column applies

From the table to B.01.350 (same Justice URL), the column headings are, verbatim:
- Column 2: "Prepackaged product with a reference amount greater than 30 g or 30 mL, unless the product is described in column 4" → threshold "15% of the daily value…"
- Column 3: "Prepackaged product with a reference amount of 30 g or 30 mL or less" → threshold "10% of the daily value…"
- Column 4: "Prepackaged main dish with a reference amount of 200 g or more" → "30% of the daily value…"

### 3.3 The reference amount for cream cheese and cheese spreads

Health Canada's Table of Reference Amounts for Food (incorporated by reference), section "D. Dairy products and substitutes":

> Row **D.1**: "Cheese, including cream cheese and cheese spread, except those listed as a separate item in column 1" — reference amount "**30 g**"
> (Also: D.2 "Cottage cheese" — 125 g; D.3 "Cheese used as an ingredient, such as dry cottage cheese or ricotta cheese" — 55 g; D.4 hard/grated cheese — 15 g.)
> — https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/technical-documents-labelling-requirements/table-reference-amounts-food/nutrition-labelling.html

**So cream cheese and cheese spreads sit at RA = 30 g → the "30 g or less" column → the 10% DV threshold applies.**

### 3.4 The daily values

> Sodium: "2300 mg" — "Sum of saturated fatty acids and trans fatty acids": "20 g" — Calcium (4 years+): "1300 mg"
> — Health Canada, Table of Daily Values, Part 1 and Part 2, https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/technical-documents-labelling-requirements/table-daily-values/nutrition-labelling.html

### 3.5 The arithmetic (regulatory-threshold math only; values are official-database or printed-label values, identified as such)

**Thresholds at RA = 30 g (10% DV column):**
- Sodium trigger: 10% × 2,300 mg = **230 mg** per RA/serving (whichever greater)
- Saturated + trans fatty acids trigger: 10% × 20 g = **2 g** per RA/serving

**(a) Canadian Nutrient File values — "Cheese, cream", CNF food code 28, per 100 g (official Health Canada database values):** sodium 365 mg; saturated fatty acids total 19.292 g; calcium 98 mg; total fat 34.24 g; moisture 54.44 g. (CNF reports no total-trans line for this food.)
— CNF, https://food-nutrition.canada.ca/cnf-fce/ (API record: https://food-nutrition.canada.ca/api/canadian-nutrient-file/nutrientamount/?lang=en&id=28)

Per 30 g reference amount (math: value × 30/100):
- Sodium: 365 × 0.30 = **109.5 mg** → 109.5 ÷ 2,300 = **4.8% DV** → below the 230 mg / 10% trigger.
- Saturated fat: 19.292 × 0.30 = **5.79 g** → 5.79 ÷ 20 = **28.9% DV** → above the 2 g / 10% trigger (even before any trans contribution).
- Calcium: 98 × 0.30 = **29.4 mg** → 29.4 ÷ 1,300 = **2.3% DV** (relevant to the exemption test below).

**(b) Printed label values — Philadelphia "Original Brick Cream Cheese," 250 g, as displayed on the Voilà retail listing's Nutrition Facts ("Nutritional Information per 30 g"):** Calories 100; Fat 10 g; "Saturated 6 g 32%"; "+ Trans 0.3 g"; "Sodium 125 mg 5%"; "Calcium 30 mg 2%"; Protein 2 g.
— https://voila.ca/products/philadelphia-cream-cheese-original-250-g/466160EA

Check the label's own math: (6 + 0.3) ÷ 20 = 31.5% ≈ printed 32%. Sodium 125 ÷ 2,300 = 5.4% ≈ printed 5%.
Against the FOP triggers: saturated+trans 6.3 g ≥ 2 g trigger (31.5% ≥ 10%); sodium 125 mg < 230 mg trigger.

**(c) CNF "Cheese spread, cream cheese base, flavoured", food code 5565, per 100 g (official database values):** sodium 436 mg; saturated fat 18.02 g; calcium 71 mg. Per 30 g: sodium 130.8 mg (5.7% DV); saturated fat 5.41 g (27.0% DV); calcium 21.3 mg (1.6% DV).
— https://food-nutrition.canada.ca/api/canadian-nutrient-file/nutrientamount/?lang=en&id=5565

### 3.6 The cheese exemptions — precisely what is and is not exempt

B.01.350 contains a conditional dairy carve-out. Verbatim:

> "(9) Subsection (1) does not apply, in respect of saturated fat and sugars, to prepackaged products that are cheese or yogurt – including drinkable yogurt – that are made from dairy products, kefir or buttermilk unless (a) in the case of saturated fat, the product contains an ingredient, other than any of the following ingredients, that contains saturated fat: (i) milk ingredients, (ii) modified milk ingredients, …"
>
> "(11) Subsection (1) does not apply, in respect of sodium, to prepackaged products that are cheese made from dairy products."
>
> "(12) For the application of subsections (9) and (11), the prepackaged products must (a) have a reference amount of 30 g or 30 mL or less and contain 10% or more of the daily value for calcium per serving of stated size or per reference amount, whichever is greater; or (b) have a reference amount greater than 30 g or 30 mL and contain 15% or more of the daily value for calcium per serving of stated size or per reference amount, whichever is greater."
> — FDR s. B.01.350, https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/C.R.C.,_c._870/section-B.01.350.html

**Putting (9)+(12) together with the numbers above — regulatory arithmetic only:**
- The cheese exemption from the saturated-fat symbol is **conditional on calcium**: at RA 30 g, the product needs **≥10% DV calcium = ≥130 mg per 30 g**.
- CNF cream cheese delivers 29.4 mg calcium per 30 g (2.3% DV); the Philadelphia brick label prints "Calcium 30 mg 2%".
- 2–2.3% < 10% → **the calcium condition is not met**, so the exemption in (9)/(11) does not attach, and the ordinary threshold test applies. On the label values shown, the saturated-fat criterion (31.5% DV vs 10% threshold) is met — i.e., this is the mechanism by which a "high in saturated fat" front-of-package symbol becomes required on cream cheese, while many higher-calcium cheeses can qualify for the exemption. *(For broadcast: state as "the regulation's calcium test" — no health framing.)*
- Sodium: cream cheese at ~110–125 mg per 30 g sits below the 230 mg trigger regardless of the exemption.

*(Also in B.01.350(5): exemptions for products with display surface under 15 cm², individual portions served with restaurant meals, etc. — quoted at the Justice URL above.)*

### 4.2 Import controls in practice: tariff-rate quotas + over-quota tariffs

| 0406.10.20 | "Over access commitment" (fresh cheese incl. cream cheese) | **245.5% but not less than $4.52/kg** |
| 0406.30.20 | Processed cheese, not grated or powdered — "Over access commitment" | **245.5% but not less than $4.34/kg** |
| 0406.90.12 | Cheddar and Cheddar types — "Over access commitment" | **245.5% but not less than $3.53/kg** |
| 0405.10.20 | Butter — "Over access commitment" | **298.5% but not less than $4.00/kg** |

— CBSA, Customs Tariff – Schedule, Chapter 4, https://www.cbsa-asfc.gc.ca/trade-commerce/tariff-tarif/2026/01-99/ch04-2026-eng.pdf

> "The WTO TRQ for cheeses of all types is set at 20,411,866 kilograms." ### 7.1 Philadelphia cream cheese: invented in New York, not Philadelphia

New York State historic marker (William G. Pomeroy Foundation), Chester, NY — marker text verbatim:

The foundation's accompanying history describes Lawrence pioneering mass production of what became Philadelphia Cream Cheese in Chester, NY, molding it into rectangles, wrapping the blocks individually and shipping to New York City markets. What can be said with full confidence: the standard quoted above is the law for anything sold as "process(ed) cheese spread," and B.01.042 / SFCR 201 (Part 2) govern whether that name may be used.

### 7.5 MacLaren's Imperial: the 1890s original — and what "first" really means

Dictionary of Canadian Biography (Alexander Ferguson MacLaren, 1854–1917):

> "Made from ground cheddar and sold in small porcelain containers, it became phenomenally popular." … "It was evidently one of the first soft, processed cheeses to be commercially distributed." … "In 1920 his cheese company would be purchased by J. L. Kraft and Brothers Company of Chicago, which also specialized in processed cheese."
> — http://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/maclaren_alexander_ferguson_14E.html

So the defensible claim is **"evidently one of the first soft, processed cheeses to be commercially distributed"** (DCB's own hedged wording) — launched by MacLaren Brothers of Stratford, Ontario (founded 1891; product on the market roughly a year later; incorporated 1899 as A. F. MacLaren Imperial Cheese Company Limited, per the same DCB entry). Avoid harder "Canada's first X" formulations — see UNVERIFIED. Today the brand sits in the Kraft Heinz Canada portfolio; the current Kraft Heinz Canada listing for MacLaren's Imperial shows ingredients, verbatim:

> "CHEDDAR CHEESE (MILK, MODIFIED MILK INGREDIENTS, SALT, BACTERIAL CULTURE, CALCIUM CHLORIDE, MICROBIAL ENZYME, LIPASE), MODIFIED MILK INGREDIENTS, WATER, SALT, SORBIC ACID, CITRIC ACID, LACTIC ACID, ANNATTO."
> — https://www.kraftheinz.com/en-CA/maclaren-imperial/products/00068100895056-maclaren-s-cheese-spread

The 1920 purchase by J.L. Kraft & Bros. (forming Kraft-MacLaren Cheese Co. Ltd.) is Kraft's historical entry into Canada — a tidy full-circle beat with Part 7.3's "more than 120-year-old commitment" quote. *(The "Kraft-MacLaren" corporate name detail is from a collector-history source — baybottles.com, https://baybottles.com/2021/05/10/maclarens-imperial-cheese/ — treat as colour, or re-verify.)*

### 7.6 Cold-pack in law vs. MacLaren's on the shelf

Note the legal geometry, purely factually: cold-pack cheese standards (7.4.17–7.4.20) permit added milk/milk products in the "cheese food" versions, and the classic MacLaren's jar has long been marketed in the cold-pack tradition (DCB: ground cheddar in containers, no heat). The current Kraft Heinz Canada page categorizes the product as a "cheese spread" name; the exact printed common name on the current jar ("cold pack cheese product" appears on many retail images) needs physical verification — flagged in UNVERIFIED.
