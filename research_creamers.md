# PART 2 — CREAM LAW, NAMING RULES, FOP & ECONOMICS
### (Verbatim from CFIA / Justice Laws / Health Canada / StatCan / Gazette, retrieved Aug 30, 2026)

## 2.1 THE LEGAL CREAM — CFCS Volume 7 (Dairy), s. 7.2.1
- Verbatim: "**Cream** (a) is the fatty liquid prepared from milk by separating the milk constituents in such a manner as to increase the milk fat content; and (b) in the case of cream for whipping that has been heat-treated above 100°C, may contain (i) skim milk powder ≤0.25%, and (ii) glucose solids ≤0.1%."
- **NO federal minimum milk-fat % for "cream." No federal standard for whipping/table/half-and-half classes.** The 10% / 16–32% / 32–40% classes are PROVINCIAL (Ontario Milk Act R.R.O. 1990 Reg. 761, per Canadian Dairy Commission: half-and-half ≥10%, table cream 16–<32%, whipping 32–<40%) + trade practice (CNF names: "table (coffee) cream 18%," "cereal (half and half) 10%").
- **The % on the carton is federally MANDATORY:** FDR B.08.002.3 — milk fat % "must be shown on the principal display panel followed by 'milk fat' or 'B.F.' or 'M.F.'" for cream, canned cream, sour cream, etc.
- Old FDR B.08.075 repealed by SOR/2024-244 s. 59 (Dec 18, 2024 — "same law, new address"); additive clauses (stabilizers etc.) moved to the Lists of Permitted Food Additives framework (item-by-item entries NOT checked — don't script specifics). B.08.077 survives: sour cream must be made from pasteurized cream.

## 2.2 "COFFEE WHITENER" HAS NO STANDARD — and the naming law that polices "cream"
- "Coffee whitener" = unstandardized food; common name under B.01.001(1)/SFCR general rule. CFIA itself uses "a 450 g jar of coffee whitener" as a worked example.
- CFIA common-name rule verbatim: names mislead by "incorporating words unwarranted by the composition of the food… resembling, directly or phonetically, the name of another product for which it is an imitation or substitute (for example, 'krab' on an imitation crab product)."
- CFIA "cream"-word guidance verbatim: "Care must be exercised in the use of the words 'butter' and 'cream'… These words should not be used to describe a food that is or has been made, in part, of cream or butter, unless the food contains an amount of cream or butter sufficient to characterize the product." (Texture uses OK: "peanut butter, cream eggs…")
- Milk parallel: "The term 'milk'… is a reference only to 'milk' as standardized in section 7.1.1" — "the normal lacteal secretion obtained from the mammary gland of the cow, genus Bos" — why Silk's shelf name is "Vanilla Oat-Based Coffee Enhancer."
- **SFCR s. 1 dairy definition (killer beat):** dairy product "means milk or a food that is derived from milk… and that contains no oil and no fat other than that of milk." A palm/coconut-oil whitener is BY DEFINITION not a dairy product.
- No "simulated dairy" regime exists (B.01.100 is meat/poultry only) — whiteners are policed via common-name rules + FDA s. 5(1).

## 2.3 "NON-DAIRY"/"DAIRY-FREE" WITH CASEINATE — CFIA VERBATIM (the key beat, primary-sourced)
- CFIA composition-and-quality page, live: "**The claim 'non-dairy' or 'dairy-free' cannot be used for products containing milk derivatives such as caseins or sodium caseinate.** The word 'dairy' when used as an adjective generally means 'made from milk'. Caseins and sodium caseinate are milk derivatives. **It is misleading to claim a product is 'non-dairy' or 'dairy-free' when it contains a milk ingredient or derivative**…" Also: trace amounts never permitted; "May Contain" statements not acceptable for such claims.
- Allergen law forcing "milk" onto whitener labels: FDR B.01.010.1 — milk protein = food allergen; source "must be shown" in the ingredient list or a "Contains" statement (SOR/2011-28 enhanced labelling). Sodium caseinate = milk protein ⇒ "Contains: Milk."
- "Modified milk ingredients" definition (archived Common Names doc, Table 2 item 7.1): "…casein, caseinates, cultured milk products, milk serum proteins, ultrafiltered milk, whey… the chemical state of which has been altered…" → **RENAMING: the current document uses "milk-derived ingredients"; transition ends January 1, 2030.** (Closing beat: the vocabulary itself is being renamed.)

## 2.4 FOP "HIGH IN" SYMBOLS — THE HONEST TWIST (Jan 1, 2026)
- B.01.350: RA ≤ 30 g/mL ⇒ **10% DV thresholds**: sat+trans 2 g; sugars 10 g; sodium 230 mg — per stated serving or RA, whichever greater. NO 50-g basis in FOP (that's nutrient-content claims — don't mix up).
- Reference amount: TRA item D.6 "Cream, including creamer…" = **15 mL**; substitutes share the RA. (Wording of D.6 row reads oddly in fetches — eyeball live page before quoting letter-for-letter.)
- OUR COMPUTATION (CNF averages; label as ours; re-run with real product panels before any brand-specific claim): 35% whipping ≈ 3.3 g sat/15 mL → **triggers "High in saturated fat"**; 32% ≈ 3.0 g → triggers; **18% table ≈ 1.7 g → just UNDER (borderline; CNF lacks trans values)**; 10% ≈ 0.95 g → under; liquid whitener ≈ 0.3–0.4 g → under; flavoured creamer sugars ≈ 5.1 g/15 mL → **UNDER the 10 g line**.
- **THE TWIST: at the tiny 15 mL serving, the fake creamer escapes both symbols while real 32–35% whipping cream gets flagged.** Script framing: the symbol system judges per-spoonful, not real-vs-fake — "the label law isn't a morality contest; read the ingredient list yourself." NEVER imply the creamer is "cleared" or the cream "condemned."
- Exemption oddity: cream sold in a refillable glass container is exempt (B.01.350(5)(f)).

## 2.8 RECALL & THE ONTARIO OIL LAW
- **On-theme recall (primary):** "Delight Chocolate brand **Dairy-free** Chocolate vegan ice cream recalled due to **undeclared milk**" — published **March 17, 2026**, Ontario + online. A live example of the exact failure mode the caseinate rules exist for. (Different product category — present precisely; no scare framing.)
- No recent recall of a coffee whitener/creamer as such, none of fluid cream — say so if needed.
- **Ontario Edible Oil Products Act** (R.S.O. 1990, c. E.1): restricted oil products imitating dairy and prohibited dairy/vegetable-oil blends; western provinces challenged it (AIT panel ruled against Ontario 2004); **repealed effective June 1, 2004** (Food Safety and Quality Act 2001 s. 61(2), as amended by Bill 231, 2002). After repeal, Ontario Milk Act O. Reg. 201/07 defined "dairy-edible oil spread" (≥80% fat, ≥50% of it milk fat, mandatory % marking). Beat: "Until June 1, 2004, an Ontario statute effectively banned blending dairy with vegetable oil." DO NOT claim a 2018 change (unverified).
