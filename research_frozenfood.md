## PART A — LAW AND STANDARDS

### A1. THE CENTREPIECE: Front-of-package (FOP) nutrition symbol — FDR B.01.350, mandatory January 1, 2026

**The mandate and the date.** Health Canada news release, "Front-of-package nutrition labelling" (June 30, 2022):
> "The food industry has been given until January 1, 2026, to make this change."
> "The new front-of-package (FOP) nutrition symbol includes a magnifying glass, intended to capture people's attention and act as a quick and easy visual cue to identify foods high in these three nutrients."
- URL: https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/news/2022/06/front-of-package-nutrition-labelling.html
- The enacting regulation is SOR/2022-168 (Canada Gazette, Part II, July 20, 2022), with transitional compliance flexibility through December 31, 2025: https://gazette.gc.ca/rp-pr/p2/2022/2022-07-20/html/sor-dors168-eng.html

Health Canada consumer page:
> "The front-of-package nutrition symbol is black and white. It has a magnifying glass and highlights what the food is high in: saturated fat, sugars, sodium or any combination of these."
- URL: https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/nutrition-labelling/front-package.html

**The rule itself — FDR C.R.C., c. 870, s. B.01.350 (Justice Laws consolidated text, retrieved Sept 7, 2026, verbatim):**
> "B.01.350 (1) Except as otherwise provided in this section, the principal display panel of a prepackaged product must carry a symbol that is set out in Schedule K.1 if (a) the product, as offered for sale, contains a nutrient that is set out in column 1 of the table to this section; and (b) the amount of the nutrient, calculated as a percentage of the daily value, meets or exceeds the applicable threshold set out in columns 2 to 7 of that table."

**The "whichever is greater" rule — B.01.350(2), verbatim:**
> "(2) For the purposes of subsection (1), the percentage of the daily value for the nutrient is calculated on the basis of the amount of the nutrient, by weight, per serving of stated size or per reference amount, whichever is greater."
- URL: https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/c.r.c.,_c._870/section-b.01.350.html

**The threshold table (verbatim column headings, from the same page):** The table "Thresholds Requiring a Nutrition Symbol" has, for general-population foods:
- Column 2: "Prepackaged product with a reference amount greater than 30 g or 30 mL, unless the product is described in column 4" — sodium row: "15% of the daily value for sodium set out in column 3 of Part 1 of the Table of Daily Values"
- Column 3: "Prepackaged product with a reference amount of 30 g or 30 mL or less" — "10% of the daily value for sodium…"
- Column 4: "Prepackaged main dish with a reference amount of 200 g or more" — "30% of the daily value for sodium set out in column 3 of Part 1 of the Table of Daily Values"
- (Columns 5–7 are parallel thresholds for foods "intended solely for children one year of age or older, but less than four years of age," where the main-dish column reads "Prepackaged main dish with a reference amount of 170 g or more.")

**"Main dish" definition — FDR B.01.001 (as reported from Justice Laws via search of section B.01.001):** a "main dish" means a combination dish, as set out in the Table of Reference Amounts, that does not require the addition of ingredients other than water, and that contains food from at least two of four listed categories (dairy and alternatives; meat/poultry/fish and alternatives; fruits and vegetables; breads/cereals/grains/pastas). French: "plat principal".
- URL: https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/C.R.C.,_c._870/section-B.01.001.html

**The sodium Daily Value.** Health Canada, "Nutrition labelling – Table of daily values" (published October 20, 2022), Part 1 sodium row (verbatim as displayed): "Sodium | 1200 mg | 2300 mg" — i.e., **2,300 mg** for the general population (column 3) and 1,200 mg for children 1–4 (column 2). (This is why the FDR sodium thresholds reference "column 3 of Part 1 of the Table of Daily Values.")
- URL: https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/technical-documents-labelling-requirements/table-daily-values/nutrition-labelling.html

**The sodium triggers (arithmetic):**
- General foods (reference amount > 30 g, not a main dish): 2,300 mg × 15% = **345 mg**
- Small-reference-amount foods (≤ 30 g/30 mL): 2,300 mg × 10% = **230 mg**
- Prepackaged main dishes with reference amount ≥ 200 g: 2,300 mg × 30% = **690 mg**

**Health Canada's industry guide states the "whichever is greater" rule this way (Front-of-package nutrition symbol labelling guide for industry, as retrieved):** "the amount of saturated fat, sugars and/or sodium in a specific quantity of a food must be assessed…that quantity of food is either the serving of stated size…or the reference amount for the product, whichever quantity is greater."
- URL: https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/legislation-guidelines/guidance-documents/front-package-nutrition-symbol-labelling-industry.html

**Reference amounts (Health Canada, "Nutrition labelling - Table of reference amounts for food," version "Published on: October 18, 2024"):**
- URL: https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/technical-documents-labelling-requirements/nutrition-labelling-table-reference-amounts-food.html
- French fries: "French fries, including spirales, smiles, wedges, fried potatoes, grated potatoes, hash browns, potato skins and pancakes, sweet potato fries" — **85 g frozen / 70 g prepared**

- Frozen entrées/casseroles (the classic TV-dinner category): "Combination dishes, such as chicken and rice casserole, hash, macaroni and cheese with or without meat, spaghetti with sauce, **lasagna**, stir fry, meat or poultry casserole, … stew, ragout, poutine, butter chicken with rice, shepherd's pie…" — **300 g**
- Plain vegetables: vegetables (fresh, frozen or canned) without sauce — **85 g fresh or frozen / 125 mL canned** (confirmed on a dedicated targeted read of the Vegetables category; one automated read returned 110 g, which belongs to the potatoes row — see UNVERIFIED note)

**Bonus exemption — huge for the episode (B.01.350(6)/(7), verbatim from Justice Laws):**
> "(6) Subsection (1) does not apply, in respect of a nutrient, to the following prepackaged products if the only ingredients containing the nutrient are, in the case of saturated fat or sodium, ingredients set out in subsection (7)…: (a) whole or cut fruits or vegetables, including frozen, canned or dried fruits or vegetables; …"
> "(7) For the purposes of subsection (6) in relation to saturated fat and sodium, the ingredients are the following ingredients to which no saturated fat or sodium has been added: (a) whole or cut fruits or vegetables, including frozen, canned or dried fruits or vegetables; …"
Health Canada's consumer page describes these as exempt "health protective foods": "fruits and vegetables without added saturated fat, sugars or sodium."
- URLs: https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/c.r.c.,_c._870/section-b.01.350.html ; https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/nutrition-labelling/front-package.html

**Real printed labels — who gets the symbol, who sails under (all arithmetic shown):**

1. **Plain frozen peas** — Canadian Nutrient File (Health Canada database, food code 2151, "Peas, green, frozen, unprepared"): **sodium 108.0 mg per 100 g** (API: https://food-nutrition.canada.ca/api/canadian-nutrient-file/nutrientamount/?lang=en&type=json&id=2151, retrieved Sept 7, 2026). Per 85 g reference amount: 108 × 0.85 = **91.8 mg = 4.0% DV**. Threshold: 345 mg. **No symbol** — and doubly so: Green Giant Canada's Frozen Summer Sweet Peas list ingredients as just "Summer Sweet Peas." (https://greengiantcanada.ca/our-products/frozen-summer-sweet-peas/), so with no added sodium the product is **exempt outright** under B.01.350(6)(a).
2. **Plain frozen fries** — McCain Superfries Straight Cut (mccain.ca, retrieved Sept 7, 2026): "Per 18 pieces (85 g)" — calories "100", sodium "105mg" "5%", saturated fat "0.3g" "2%". Ingredients: "Potatoes, Canola oil, Sea salt, Sodium acid pyrophosphate (to retain natural colour), Caramel, Annatto." (https://mccain.ca/en/products/superfries/straight-cut/). Math: serving = reference amount = 85 g; 105 mg ÷ 2,300 mg = **4.6% DV vs the 345 mg (15%) trigger. No symbol** — sails far under. (Because sea salt IS added, the fries don't get the B.01.350(6) exemption; they simply don't hit the threshold.)
3. **Frozen dinner** — Michelina's Lasagna with Meat Sauce, michelinas.ca Canadian nutrition panel (retrieved by direct page download Sept 7, 2026): "Serving Size 1 Package 255g … Calories 260 … **Sodium 700mg 30%**." (https://www.michelinas.ca/en/products/original/lasagna-meat-sauce). Math: lasagna is in the 300 g combination-dish reference amount category and meets the B.01.001 main-dish definition (pasta + beef + tomato sauce = foods from ≥2 categories, nothing added but nothing needed), so the **30% column (690 mg)** applies. Reference amount (300 g) > serving (255 g), so B.01.350(2) says calculate on the greater quantity: 700 mg × (300/255) = **823.5 mg = 35.8% DV ≥ 30%** → **"High in sodium" symbol required.** (Even on the label's own per-serving basis it is 700/2,300 = 30.4% — over the line either way.)
4. **Frozen pizza** — Delissio Rising Crust Pepperoni, per Nestlé Canada's product listing (madewithnestle.ca, values obtained via search snapshot Sept 7, 2026 — live page refused automated access; see UNVERIFIED for caveat): per 1/4 pizza (197 g): calories 460, saturated fat 7 g (37% DV), **sodium 1,160 mg (50% DV)**. Math: pizza "consumed as a meal" reference amount = 200 g ≥ 200 g main-dish floor; 200 g > 197 g serving, so scale: 1,160 × (200/197) = **1,177.7 mg = 51.2% DV — nearly double the 690 mg (30%) trigger, and more than triple the 345 mg general trigger** → symbol required.

**The narrative arc for the episode:** the plain frozen aisle (peas, mixed veg, plain fries) either is exempt or sits at 4–5% of the sodium DV; the "dinner" end of the same aisle (lasagna, pizza) lands at 30–50% DV per serving and wears the magnifying glass. And note the regulator's mercy: main dishes get a 30% threshold precisely because a whole meal reasonably carries more sodium than a side — a 15% threshold would have flagged nearly every frozen dinner in the case.

(2) Canada A is the name for the grade of frozen peas that (a) possess similar varietal characteristics; (b) possess a good flavour; (c) possess a practically uniform good colour; (d) are tender; and (e) are practically free from loose skins, splits, broken peas, spotted or otherwise discoloured peas, harmless extraneous vegetable matter and other defects."

Canadian grade names for frozen processed fruit or vegetable products are amongst the following: **Canada A, Canada B, Canada C**."

**Mandatory or voluntary?** Same CFIA page, verbatim:
> "All processed fruit or vegetable products for which grades are prescribed by the SFCR and that are interprovincially traded, imported or exported **must be graded, must meet the grade requirements set out in the Compendium and must be labelled with the applicable grade name** [306(1), SFCR]."

### A3. Compositional standards: yes — there IS a legal recipe for frozen french fries

**SFCR side — Canadian Standards of Identity: Volume 4 – Processed Fruit or Vegetable Products** (incorporated by reference into the SFCR; "Last amended on 2024-12-18"), downloaded Sept 7, 2026, verbatim:
> "56. Frozen French Fried Potatoes is the food that is prepared by freezing mature, sound potatoes that have been cleaned, sorted, trimmed, cut into strips, blanched and processed in vegetable oil. It may contain (a) sodium acid pyrophosphate; (b) dextrose; (c) ascorbic acid; (d) food colours; and (e) asparaginase."
And for frozen vegetables generally:
> "52. (1) Frozen (Naming the vegetable or vegetables) and frozen mixed vegetables are the foods that are prepared by blanching and freezing one or more types of fresh vegetables. They may contain (a) salt; (b) citric acid; and (c) natural flavouring ingredients."

### A4. "Product of Canada" vs "Made in Canada" vs "Prepared in Canada" (CFIA, verbatim)

CFIA "Origin claims on food labels" (downloaded Sept 7, 2026):
- URL: https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/origin-claims

> "A food product may use the claim 'Product of Canada' when all or virtually all major ingredients, processing, and labour used to make the food product are Canadian. This means that all the significant ingredients in a food product are Canadian in origin and that non-Canadian material is negligible."
> "…very low levels of ingredients that are not generally produced in Canada, including spices, food additives, vitamins, minerals, flavouring preparations, or grown in Canada such as oranges, cane sugar and coffee. Generally, the percentage referred to as very little or minor is considered to be less than a total of 2% of the product"
> "A 'Made in Canada' claim with a qualifying statement can be used on a food product when the last substantial transformation of the product occurred in Canada, even if some ingredients are from other countries."
> "A substantial transformation occurs when a food product undergoes processing which changes its nature and becomes a new product bearing a new name commonly understood by the consumer. For example, the processing of cheese, dough, sauce and other ingredients to create a pizza would be considered a substantial transformation."
> "If the 'Made in Canada' claim is used, it must also include a qualifying statement to indicate that the food product is made in Canada from imported ingredients or a combination of imported and domestic ingredients."
> "The claim 'Made in Canada from domestic and/or imported ingredients' is not permitted as it does not provide meaningful information to the consumer about the Canadian content."
> "'Prepared in Canada' to describe a food which has been entirely prepared in Canada" — listed among "other more specific statements or claims that describe the Canadian value added [that] may be used without further qualification, provided they are truthful and not misleading for consumers."
Frozen-food bonus, verbatim: "The claim 'Canadian' is considered to be the same as a 'Product of Canada' claim. As such, all or virtually all major ingredients, processing, and labour used to make the food product must be Canadian. For example, the claim 'Canadian' on a container of **frozen lasagna** would mean that the food meets the 'Product of Canada' criteria."

**Country-of-origin rules for imports (two different tests — great TV detail):**
- Imported *processed fruit or vegetable products* (frozen vegetables/fries): "The label of an imported prepackaged processed fruit or vegetable product must bear the name of the country of origin. For an imported prepackaged processed fruit or vegetable product, **country of origin refers to the country where the product was packaged** [274(1), SFCR]." Also: "All country names must be written out in full, except for the United States which may be abbreviated to USA as it is recognized worldwide." (https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/processed-fruit-vegetable-products)
- Imported *fish*: "For prepackaged fish imported into Canada, the name of the country of origin must be clearly identified on the label [266, SFCR]. **The country of origin is the country where the last substantial transformation occurred.** The wording 'product of' / 'produit de' is recommended…" (https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/fish)

### A5. Date marking: why frozen food has no best-before date — and what the date means anyway

FDR B.01.007 (Justice Laws, verbatim): the label must show
> "(b) where a prepackaged product having a durable life of 90 days or less is packaged at a place other than the retail premises from which it is to be sold, (i) the durable life date, and (ii) instructions for the proper storage of the prepackaged product if it requires storage conditions that differ from normal room temperature"
- URL: https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/c.r.c.,_c._870/section-b.01.007.html

CFIA "Date markings and storage instructions on food labels" (downloaded Sept 7, 2026, verbatim):
> "Foods with a shelf life greater than 90 days (for example, cereals, semi-dry cured or dry cured sausage, etc.) are not required to be labelled with a 'best before' date and storage instructions or a packaging date and durable life information."
> "**If a product is in a frozen state at the time that it is being packaged, then a best before date or packaging date need not be applied because the product is deemed to have a durable life date of greater than 90 days.**"
> "**A durable life date is not an indicator of food safety, neither before nor after the date.** It applies to unopened products only; once opened, the shelf life of a food may change. Products may be found for sale after the 'best before' date has passed as the date is based on freshness and quality, rather than safety. When this date has passed, the food may lose some of its freshness and flavour, or its texture may have changed."
Also the charming "Freeze by" rule: "It is acceptable to declare a 'freeze by' date in addition to, and that is the same as, the 'best before' date in a clear statement indicating that the product can be frozen if not consumed by the 'best before' date."
- URL: https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/date-markings-and-storage-instructions

Closest official quality-vs-safety line for freezers — Health Canada "Safe food storage":
> "Make sure your refrigerator is set at 4 °C (40 °F) or lower and your freezer at -18 °C (0 °F) or lower."
> "The following recommended refrigeration times are for safety, and the freezing times are for quality."
- URL: https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/general-food-safety-tips/safe-food-storage.html

### B2. Origin story verified: frozen food was born on Canadian ice

History.com, "How the Modern Frozen Food Industry Took Inspiration from Inuits" (verbatim):
> "Between 1912 and 1917, Birdseye, a Brooklyn native, lived in chilly Labrador, Canada, where he worked briefly on a hospital ship before starting a fox-breeding venture."
> "It was during this period that he learned about the customs of the Indigenous Inuit, who would go ice fishing and then let their catch immediately freeze in the frigid air."
> "When this frozen fish, which was left out in the cold, eventually was cooked, it tasted fresh."
- URL: https://www.history.com/articles/history-of-frozen-food-birdseye

Smithsonian Magazine (article dated Summer 2026, verbatim):
> "In 1912, a 25-year-old Brooklyn native with an adventurous streak journeys to the icy reaches of northeast Canada, where he spends the better part of five years learning Indigenous ways.
