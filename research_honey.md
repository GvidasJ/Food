### 0.2 The real Canadian story is a LABELLING rule, not an adulteration rule

**(a) CONFIRMED — primary source.** CFIA industry labelling guidance for honey:

> **"Combining declarations of country of origin and grade is not permitted. For example, 'Product of / Produit du Canada No. 1' is not a permitted statement."**

- Canadian-produced honey grades: **"Canada No. 1, Canada No. 2, Canada No. 3"**
- Imported honey grades: **"No. 1, No. 2, No. 3"**
- Blends: *"A Blend of Canadian and (naming the source or sources) Honey"* / *"mélange de miel canadien et de miel (naming the source or sources)"*, or *"A blend (naming the source or sources) Honey and Canadian Honey"*, **"in descending order of their proportion"**, in French and English.
- Blended-honey grade names are **No. 1, No. 2, No. 3** (no "Canada" prefix).
- "White" is a **colour class**, not an origin: it must appear **"in close proximity to the grade name."**

URL: https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/honey

**This is your whole thesis in one line: "Canada No. 1 White" is a grade and a colour, not a country.** A jar can say "Canada No. 1 White" because Canadian-graded honey is in it; that phrase is not a country-of-origin statement. The origin statement is a separate, legally distinct declaration. **Do not say any brand is breaking this rule** — the rule is what makes the *consumer confusion* legitimate, and the confusion is the story.

**(a) CONFIRMED — CFIA origin claims, Chapter 5:**

> **"A food product may use the claim 'Product of Canada' when all or virtually all major ingredients, processing, and labour used to make the food product are Canadian."**

> **"When the claim '100% Canadian' is used on a label, the food or ingredient to which the claim applies must be entirely Canadian rather than 'all or virtually all' Canadian."**

URL: https://inspection.canada.ca/food-labels/labelling/industry/origin-claims/eng/1626884467839/1626884529209?chap=5

**"100% Canadian" is the strictest claim on a Canadian honey jar — stricter than "Product of Canada."** Several brands below use exactly that phrase. That is a strong, defensible buying-guide point.

## 2. THE LAW ON WHAT CAN BE CALLED HONEY IN CANADA

### 2.1 The compositional standard — where it actually lives (this has moved, and most sources get it wrong)
**Tier (a) CONFIRMED PRIMARY.**

The honey standard is **no longer a numbered section of the Food and Drug Regulations**. I grepped the full consolidated FDR (C.R.C., c. 870) — the word "honey" appears only twice, incidentally, in the sugars-grouping and sweetening-ingredient definitions. There is no honey standard in the FDR text itself.

The chain is: **FDA/FDR → Canadian Food Compositional Standards (incorporated by reference) → s. 15.1.16** and **SFCA/SFCR → Canadian Standards of Identity, Volume 5 – Honey → which points back to s. 15.1.16.**

**Canadian Standards of Identity: Volume 5 – Honey** (last amended 2024-12-18), verbatim in full — the whole instrument is one sentence:
> "**1.** Honey is the food that meets the standard set out in subsection 15.1.16(1) of Volume 15 of the Food Compositional Standards Document and
> if it is derived mainly from the nectar of blossoms, meets the standard set out in subsections 15.1.16(2) and (3) of Volume 15 of the Food Compositional Standards Document; or
> if it is derived from secretions of living plants or from secretions on living plants, meets the standard set out in subsection 15.1.16(4) of Volume 15 of the Food Compositional Standards Document."
URL: https://inspection.canada.ca/en/about-cfia/acts-and-regulations/list-acts-and-regulations/documents-incorporated-reference/canadian-standards-identity-volume-5

**Canadian Food Compositional Standards, Volume 15 – Sweetening Agents, s. 15.1.16, verbatim in full:**
> "**15.1.16 Honey**
> (1) is the food produced by honey bees and derived from
> (a) the nectar of blossoms;
> (b) secretions of living plants; or
> (c) secretions on living plants; and
> it has the following characteristics
> (d) a fluid, viscous or partly or wholly crystallized consistency;
> (e) a diastase activity, determined after processing and blending, as represented by a diastase figure on the Gothe scale of not less than eight where the hydroxy-methyl-furfural content is not more than 0.004%; or
> (f) a diastase activity, determined after processing and blending, as represented by a diastase figure on the Gothe scale of not less than three where the hydroxy-methyl-furfural content is not more than 0.0015%.
> (2) Subject to subsection (3), honey derived mainly from nectar of blossoms does not contain
> (a) less than 65% apparent reducing sugar, calculated as invert sugar;
> (b) more than 20% moisture;
> (c) more than 5% apparent sucrose;
> (d) more than 0.1% water insoluble solids, except that pressed honey contains not more than 0.5% water insoluble solids;
> (e) more than 0.6% ash; and
> (f) more than 40 mEq acid per 1000 g.
> (3) Honey derived mainly from the nectar of lavender, rubinia, alfalfa, or banksia menziesii meets the requirements of paragraphs (2)(a), (b) and (d) to (f), and contains not more than 10% apparent sucrose.
> (4) Honey derived from secretions of living plants or from secretions on living plants does not contain
> (a) less than 60% apparent reducing sugar, calculated as invert sugar;
> (b) more than 20% moisture;
> (c) more than 10% apparent sucrose;
> (d) more than 0.1% water insoluble solids, except that pressed honey contains not more than 0.5% water insoluble solids;

### 2.2 Can you add ANY sugar or syrup to something called honey? No.
**Tier (a) CONFIRMED PRIMARY.**

**The structural argument — and it is airtight.** Read s. 15.1.16 against its neighbours in the same document. Other standards contain explicit permission clauses: s. 18.1.1 "Fish … **may contain** (i) salt, and (ii) seasoning". **Section 15.1.16 contains no "may contain" clause at all.** Honey is defined exhaustively as the product of bees from nectar or plant secretions, with numeric limits. There is no permitted-additions list. Anything added takes the product outside the standard.

CFIA states the consequence in plain words, verbatim:
> "In Canada, adding foreign sugars such as corn, rice or cane sugar syrup to food represented as honey is considered adulteration. Adulterated honey is not authentic and cannot be sold as honey in Canada."
URL: https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-guidance-commodity/honey/authenticity-requirements

And on imports, verbatim: *"No added ingredient (such as colour or sugar) is allowed in a product labelled as 'Honey'."*
URL: https://inspection.canada.ca/en/importing-food-plants-animals/food-imports/food-specific-requirements/honey

**If you add something, you must rename the product.** CFIA labelling guidance, verbatim:
> "If honey is combined with another ingredient, such as a flavour, royal jelly, or other, the common name must describe how the product deviates from the standard (for example, strawberry flavoured honey, honey with royal jelly)."
URL: https://inspection.canada.ca/food-labels/labelling/industry/honey/eng/1625512297254/1625512297753

**The statutory hooks (cite these with section numbers):**
- **Food and Drugs Act, s. 4(1):** "No person shall sell an article of food that … (d) is adulterated". https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/F-27/FullText.html
- **Food and Drugs Act, s. 5(1):** "No person shall label, package, treat, process, sell or advertise any food in a manner that is false, misleading or deceptive or is likely to create an erroneous impression regarding its character, value, quantity, composition, merit or safety."
- **Food and Drugs Act, s. 6(1):** "Where a standard for a food has been prescribed, no person shall (a) import into Canada, (b) send, convey or receive for conveyance from one province to another … any article that is intended for sale and that is likely to be mistaken for that food unless the article complies with the prescribed standard."
- **Safe Food for Canadians Act, s. 6(1):** "It is prohibited for a person to manufacture, prepare, package, label, sell, import or advertise a food commodity in a manner that is false, misleading or deceptive or is likely to create an erroneous impression regarding its character, quality, value, quantity, composition, merit, safety **or origin** or the method of its manufacture or preparation." https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/S-1.1/FullText.html
- **SFCR s. 199(1)(b):** false/misleading labelling "includes labelling a food with … any expression, word, number, depiction or symbol that may reasonably be considered to imply that a prepackaged food contains any matter that it does not in fact contain or that it does not contain any matter that it does in fact contain." https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-2018-108/FullText.html
- **International comparator (FOREIGN):** Codex *Standard for Honey* CXS 12-1981: "Honey sold as such shall not have added to it any food ingredient, including food additives, nor shall any other additions be made other than honey." https://www.fao.org/input/download/standards/310/cxs_012e.pdf

**Industry obligation, verbatim (CFIA):**
> "Under the SFCR, licence holders must maintain a written preventive control plan. This preventive control plan must include measures to ensure the safety of the food as well as ensuring that the food is not represented in a manner that is false, misleading or deceptive. Measures must also be in place to ensure compliance with prescribed compositional standards."

### 2.3 The Canadian grading system — and it is MANDATORY
**Tier (a) CONFIRMED PRIMARY.** This is where the reference video almost certainly gets it wrong. Grading honey in Canada is **not optional** for honey that crosses a border, in either direction, or a provincial line.

**SFCR s. 306(1), verbatim (marginal note: "Mandatory grading"):**
> "**306 (1)** Any eggs, fish, fresh fruits or vegetables, processed fruit or vegetable products, **honey**, maple syrup or beef carcass in respect of which grades are prescribed by these Regulations that are sent or conveyed from one province to another or that are imported or exported must
> (a) be graded;
> (b) meet the requirements that are set out in the Compendium … in respect of the applicable grade of that food; and
> (c) be labelled, in accordance with the Compendium … with the applicable grade name that is set out in the Compendium."

**The escape hatches, SFCR s. 306(3), verbatim:**
> "(b) honey that does not meet the requirements of the Regulations with respect to grade and that is sent or conveyed from one province to another, if it is labelled with the words 'Substandard' or 'sous-régulier'; and
> (c) honey that does not meet the requirements of the Regulations with respect to grade and that is exported, if the information on the label or the container with respect to the quality of the honey is not false, misleading or deceptive or is not likely to create an erroneous impression."

**⚠️ The real-world limit on "mandatory."** Grading is triggered by **interprovincial trade, import or export**. Honey produced and sold **entirely within one province** is not caught by s. 306. CFIA states, verbatim: *"When sold intraprovincially, honey is subject to the labelling requirements under the FDA and FDR, as well as specific requirements of the SFCA and SFCR that apply to prepackaged foods sold in Canada, regardless of the level of trade. Provincial regulations may also have labelling requirements."* So: the farmers' market jar sold in its home province is in a different regime from the supermarket jar. Say it that way.

**The grade names — Canadian Grade Compendium, Volume 6 – Honey (last modified 2021-04-28), verbatim:**
> "**2. (1)** There are three grades of honey produced in Canada with the grade names **Canada No. 1, Canada No. 2 and Canada No. 3** (see Volume 9, Import Grade Requirements for grade names used for imported honey).
> **(2)** There are three grades of honey that is a blend of imported honey and Canadian honey with the grade names **No. 1, No. 2, and No. 3**."
URL: https://inspection.canada.ca/en/about-cfia/acts-and-regulations/list-acts-and-regulations/documents-incorporated-reference/canadian-grade-compendium-volume-6

**What "Canada No. 1" actually means, verbatim (s. 3):**
> "**3.** Honey graded Canada No. 1 must
> a) be the food derived from the nectar of blossoms or from secretions of or on the living parts of plants by the work of honey bees;
> b) have a consistency that is fluid, viscous or partly or wholly crystallized;
> c) meet the composition requirements set out in section 10;
> d) have a diastase activity … not less than eight if the hydroxymethylfurfural content is not more than 40 mg/kg; or … not less than three if the hydroxymethylfurfural content is not more than 15 mg/kg;
> e) have no deterioration seriously affecting its edibility, appearance or shipping quality;
> f) contain not more than 17.8% moisture or, if its container bears the word 'pasteurized' … not more than 18.6% moisture;
> g) be free from any foreign material that would be retained on a screen having a sieve opening of 0.1778 mm …;
> h) contain not more than 0.1% water insoluble solids or, if its container bears the word 'pressed' … not more than 0.5%;
> i) have a flavour characteristic of its colour class and be free from any objectionable flavour, aroma or taint;
> j) **if its container bears the word 'liquid' … be clear, bright, uniform in colour and free from visible crystals;** and
> k) if its container bears the word 'creamed' … have a smooth fine texture and complete and uniform granulation."

Canada No. 2 (s. 5) relaxes moisture to 18.6% (20% if pasteurized), a coarser filtration screen (0.2489 mm), flavour "may be slightly off but with its honey flavour being not substantially impaired", and permits "not more than slight signs of crystallization" in product labelled liquid. Canada No. 3 (s. 7) is essentially moisture ≤20% plus the basic requirements.

**⚠️ THE KEY INSIGHT FOR THE SCRIPT — and it is the single best-supported "the label doesn't tell you what you think" beat in Part 2.** Read s. 3(a)–(k) again. **The grade is about moisture, clarity, filtration, flavour, colour and texture. It says nothing about country of origin and nothing about authenticity testing.** A Canadian-and-imported blend can carry "No. 1." Colour class is likewise cosmetic: four classes for consumer packs (White / Golden / Amber / Dark) measured on a Pfund honey grader, six for bulk (Extra White through Dark). Grade ≠ origin. Grade ≠ purity. Grade ≠ tested.

The Alberta Beekeepers Commission made exactly this point to Parliament — see 2.6 — and its complaint is **confirmed by the text of the Compendium itself**, which is why you can say it without relying on the advocacy source.

**Grade name is also a licensing matter.** SFCR s. 308(1)(d): a licence holder may apply a grade name only "if the honey has been graded by a licence holder."

### 2.4 Country of origin — including blends
**Tier (a) CONFIRMED PRIMARY.** SFCR, Part 11, Division 2, Subdivision J – Honey. https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-2018-108/FullText.html

> "**276** The label of prepackaged honey that is produced in Canada and graded in accordance with these Regulations must bear the expression 'Product of Canada' or 'produit du Canada' or 'Canadian Honey' or 'miel canadien'."
> "**277 (1)** The label of imported prepackaged honey must bear the expression 'Product of' or 'produit de' followed by the name of the foreign state of origin.
> (2) In the case of imported prepackaged honey, other than consumer prepackaged honey, that information must be shown in characters that are at least 9.5 mm in height."
> "**278** The label of consumer prepackaged honey that was packaged from imported honey and graded in accordance with these Regulations must bear the expressions 'Product of' and 'produit de' followed by the name of the foreign state of origin."

**The blend rule — this is the answer to "what exactly must a multi-country blend declare?":**
> "**279 (1)** The label of prepackaged honey that is a blend of imported honey and Canadian honey and that is graded in accordance with these Regulations must bear the expression '**A Blend of Canadian and (naming the foreign state or states of origin) Honey**' or 'mélange de miel canadien et de miel (indication de l'État étranger ou des États étrangers d'origine)' or '**A Blend of (naming the foreign state or states of origin) Honey and Canadian Honey**' or 'mélange de miel (indication de l'État étranger ou des États étrangers d'origine) et de miel canadien'.
> **(2)** The states of origin, Canadian or foreign, must be shown in descending order of the proportion of honey from each state."

CFIA's plain-language gloss, verbatim: *"When honey is sourced from multiple countries of origin, these countries must be named in descending order of their proportion in the 'Product of' statement (e.g. Product of Argentina and Brazil)."*

**So: every country must be named, in descending order by proportion. But no percentages are required.** A blend can be 95% one country and 5% another and the label reads identically to a 50/50. That is the honest, verifiable gap — say that, not that origin is hidden.

**One more precise fact worth screen time.** CFIA, verbatim: *"Note: Combining declarations of country of origin and grade is not permitted. For example, 'Product of / Produit du Canada No. 1' is not a permitted statement."* The rules deliberately keep grade and origin apart on the label — which is exactly why consumers conflate them.

**"For Further Preparation Only" — the non-compliant-honey pathway, SFCR s. 18(1), verbatim (CFIA gloss):**
> "Any person may send or convey honey from one province to another or import honey that does not meet the requirements of the SFCA and SFCR, except for the requirements for standard container sizes, provided that: a label that bears the expression 'For Further Preparation Only' … is applied … and the food is manufactured, processed, treated, preserved, graded, packaged or labelled by a licence holder to bring it into compliance with SFCA and SFCR requirements within 3 months, or a longer period as specified by the Minister."

### 2.5 "PURE", "RAW", "UNPASTEURIZED", "UNFILTERED" — **MAJOR FINDING: none of these is a defined regulated term for honey in Canada**
**Tier (a) CONFIRMED PRIMARY — including the confirmed *absence*.**

I searched the full consolidated text of both the **Food and Drug Regulations** (C.R.C., c. 870) and the **Safe Food for Canadians Regulations** (SOR/2018-108). **The words "unpasteurized" and "unfiltered" do not appear anywhere in either instrument.** Neither the Canadian Standards of Identity Volume 5, nor CFCS s. 15.1.16, nor Canadian Grade Compendium Volume 6 defines "pure", "raw", "unpasteurized" or "unfiltered."

Term by term:

**"RAW" — guidance only, no regulation, no threshold, no test.** The only Canadian text on it is a paragraph of CFIA guidance, verbatim:
> "Honey can be considered as 'raw' when it has not undergone any treatment or process such as heat treatment (other than minimal heating used for the purpose of extraction) or filtering.
> To avoid being misleading, specific information which clarifies the meaning of the term 'raw' may accompany the claim, such as 'unfiltered'."
URL: https://inspection.canada.ca/food-labels/labelling/industry/honey/eng/1625512297254/1625512297753
Note the construction: *"can be considered as"*. That is not a definition, it is a view. There is no maximum temperature, no maximum time, no enzyme or HMF threshold, no filtration cut-off, and no verification requirement attached to the word "raw" on a Canadian honey label.

**"UNFILTERED" — not defined at all.** It appears in Canadian text exactly once: as a *suggested optional add-on* to a "raw" claim in the guidance quoted above. It has no standard of its own. Contrast this with the grade rules, which *do* specify filtration screens (0.1778 mm for Canada No. 1, 0.2489 mm for Canada No. 2) — so Canadian law sets a filtration spec for **grades**, but none for the word "unfiltered."

**"UNPASTEURIZED" — not defined.** The word "pasteurized" appears in Grade Compendium Vol. 6 only as a moisture allowance trigger (s. 3(f): 17.8% moisture, or 18.6% "if its container bears the word 'pasteurized'"). Its negation is nowhere defined. There is no Canadian standard for what "unpasteurized honey" means.

**"PURE" — no honey-specific definition; governed only by the general misleading-labelling prohibition.** The only guidance is CFIA's generic composition-and-quality-claims page, verbatim:
> "The term 'pure' should not be used on the labels of, or in connection with, an article of food that is a compound, mixture, imitation or substitute. Consumers expect a food described as 'pure' or '100% pure' to be uncontaminated and unadulterated, and to contain only substances or ingredients that are understood to be part of the food so described."
> "In all cases, the terms 'all', 'pure' or '100% pure' must be used with care. If these terms are used in such a way as to imply that other similar products are adulterated or not up to standard, then the use of these terms could be construed as being misleading."
URL: https://inspection.canada.ca/food-labels/labelling/industry/composition-and-quality/eng/1625516122300/1625516122800

**How to say this on air, accurately:** *"Canada sets numbers for honey — moisture, sucrose, ash, acidity, diastase. It sets numbers for grade and for colour. It does not set a number for 'raw'. It does not set a number for 'unfiltered'. It does not define 'unpasteurized' at all. And 'pure' has no honey-specific meaning in Canadian law — it's policed only by the general rule against misleading labels. Four of the words most likely to be on the front of the jar are the four the rulebook says least about."*

**Contrast, for balance:** "Manuka" claims *are* addressed by CFIA guidance (UMF/NPA/Bioactive statements are treated as implied health claims and are constrained) — but that is a health-claim matter and is **out of scope for this video per house rules**. Noted here only so you know it exists and can avoid it.

### 3.2 What actually happens at the border
**Tier (a) CONFIRMED PRIMARY.**

The honest answer: **there is no routine authenticity test at the Canadian border.** Border-stage control is documentary and licence-based; authenticity testing is done *inland*, after import, by CFIA inspectors sampling at the importer/processor, and by CFIA and a contract laboratory.

- **Licence + preventive control plan.** Importers must hold a Safe Food for Canadians licence and maintain a written preventive control plan that includes "measures to ensure … that the food is not represented in a manner that is false, misleading or deceptive" and "compliance with prescribed compositional standards." (CFIA honey authenticity page, verbatim.)
- **Where sampling happens.** CFIA's own description: targeted sampling occurs "at the regulated party that is responsible for the product in Canada … domestic processors, importers or … retailers." (FFR 2024-25.) The 2023–24 report's location breakdown for honey lists only "domestic processors" and "importers." Not the border.
- **Supply-chain controls are pushed onto industry**, verbatim: *"Control measures may include supply chain controls whereby industry members obtain assurance from their suppliers, or through their own analytical efforts, that the products they receive and offer for sale meet all regulatory requirements."*
- **CBSA involvement is on the tariff side, not the testing side.** From the 2018–19 report, verbatim: *"CFIA engaged the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) on this project from the outset, and CFIA's laboratory results were shared with CBSA. Pure honey is duty-free whereas artificial honey (whether or not mixed with genuine honey) involves a rate of duty."*

**Scale of testing vs. scale of imports — the fair, sourced framing.** CFIA tested **81 honey samples** in 2024–25 against **11,235 tonnes** imported in 2025. That is CFIA's published sample count against StatCan's published import volume, and you can state both numbers side by side without editorialising. Industry has made the same point publicly (Tier (b), Canadian trade press, Aug 2026): that CFIA tests "only 100 to 125 samples out of the 40 million pounds of honey coming in." Attribute that to industry, not to the regulator.

## 4. THE TESTING SCIENCE — the accurate version

### 4.1 Which methods CFIA actually uses: **two, and only two**
**Tier (a) CONFIRMED PRIMARY.** CFIA honey authenticity requirements page, verbatim:
> "The CFIA uses 2 techniques to detect adulteration of honey with foreign sugars:
> **Stable Isotope Ratio Analysis (SIRA)**, which detects adulteration with C4 sugars such as sugar cane and corn syrup
> **Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR)**, which detects adulteration with C4 sugars as well as C3 sugars from sources such as rice, beets, barley and wheat
> Samples are assessed as unsatisfactory if either SIRA or NMR testing shows the presence of added sugars in the honey."

Confirmed identically in the Food Fraud Annual Reports, verbatim (2023-24): *"Methodology: detection of C4 and C3 sugars by nuclear magnetic resonance; detection of C4 sugars by stable isotope ratio analysis."*

**CFIA does not use pollen analysis** in this programme. Pollen analysis appears nowhere in any CFIA honey authenticity report or guidance page I retrieved. The programme also separately monitors "veterinary drug residues, chemical contaminants, and compliance with specifications of the standard, such as moisture content" — **residues are a health/safety stream and are out of scope for this video.**

### 4.2 SCIRA / SIRA — what it actually detects, and its real limitation
Note on terminology for the script: the reference video's "SCIRA" and CFIA's "SIRA" are the same family — **stable carbon isotope ratio analysis**, sometimes written SCIRA, and run by isotope-ratio mass spectrometry (IRMS).

**The mechanism, plainly.** Plants fix carbon by one of two photosynthetic pathways. **C4 plants** — corn and sugar cane — discriminate less against the heavier carbon-13 isotope, so sugars made from them carry a measurably different ¹³C/¹²C ratio than **C3 plants**, which include most nectar-producing flowers. Honey carries its own internal reference: its protein fraction. The method compares the isotope ratio of the whole honey against the isotope ratio of the protein extracted from that same honey. If cane or corn sugar has been added, the two diverge, and the size of the divergence estimates how much was added.

**CFIA's own description of the method and threshold, verbatim (2018-19 report):**
> "Samples were assessed as unsatisfactory if either SIRA or NMR testing showed the presence of added sugars in the honey. The SIRA testing assessment is based on an internationally recognized Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC) method and has a **threshold limit for detection of adulteration of 7% calculated C4 sugars**."

The AOAC method is **AOAC Official Method 998.12, "C-4 Plant Sugars in Honey: Internal Standard Stable Carbon Isotope Ratio Method."** (Tier (a) for existence/title: https://academic.oup.com/book/doi/10.1093/9780197610145.003.3700)

**THE LIMITATION — state it exactly this way.** CFIA, verbatim (2018-19 report):
> "SIRA and NMR testing are complementary as each has advantages and limitations. **SIRA testing can detect lower levels of foreign C4 sugars (such as from corn and cane sugar) but cannot detect C3 sugars (such as from rice syrup).** NMR testing can detect adulteration with both C3 and C4 sugars."

**Why that matters, and the number that proves it.** CFIA broke out the two methods in 2018–19, verbatim:
> "15 samples were unsatisfactory for SIRA testing: 6.3% (15/240)
> 44 samples were unsatisfactory for NMR testing: 18.3% (44/240)
> Some samples were unsatisfactory for both tests while some were unsatisfactory for only one:
> **SIRA testing found 7 (out of 15) samples unsatisfactory that were not found by NMR**
> **NMR testing found 34 (out of 44) samples unsatisfactory that were not found by SIRA**
> These differences were expected, given the abilities and limitations of each technique, and demonstrate the importance of analyzing the results together to assess compliance."

That is the whole story in one table. **Isotope testing alone would have caught 15 of the 52 failures — under a third.** Rice syrup is a C3-plant syrup; it is isotopically close enough to nectar that the classic method is effectively blind to it. This is the precise, quotable, regulator-sourced reason the field moved on — and it is the fact the reference video almost certainly garbles.

**Second-order limitations, for accuracy** (Tier (b), peer-reviewed literature, FOREIGN): the 7% figure is a detection floor, so adulteration below roughly 7% calculated C4 sugars is not flagged; and honeys with an unusual or low protein fraction can produce interpretation problems. Documented false-positive tendency has been reported for pine (honeydew) honey under AOAC 998.12 — see *Elucidating the false positive tendency at AOAC 998.12 C-4 sugar test for pine honey samples*, ScienceDirect, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0889157522004057. **Use this only if you want to be scrupulously fair about false positives; it cuts against a "the tests catch everything" framing.**

### 4.3 NMR profiling — what it is and why regulators moved toward it
**The mechanism, plainly.** Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy places the sample in a strong magnetic field and reads the resonance of hydrogen nuclei. Because every compound in the honey contributes, the output is not a single measurement but a **spectrum — a compositional fingerprint** covering sugars, organic acids, amino acids and minor constituents at once. Authenticity is assessed by **comparing that fingerprint statistically against a reference database of authentic honeys** rather than against a single threshold.

**CFIA's own description of how the comparison is made, verbatim (2018-19 report):**
> "The NMR testing assessment is based on the comparison of the sample to the **profile of authentic honey, developed from the analysis of over 18,000 authentic honey samples and sugar syrups used for adulteration**."

**Why regulators moved toward it — three reasons, all sourced:**
1. **Coverage of C3 syrups.** CFIA, verbatim: NMR "detects adulteration with C4 sugars as well as C3 sugars from sources such as rice, beets, barley and wheat." SIRA does not.
2. **Demonstrated incremental yield.** CFIA's 2018-19 split: NMR flagged 34 samples SIRA missed; SIRA flagged 7 NMR missed. Neither replaces the other — CFIA runs both and fails a sample on either.
3. **Adoption as the complementary method of choice in official work.** European Commission, *EU Coordinated Action "From the Hives"*, March 2023, verbatim: *"To date, private laboratories have chosen NMR profiling as a complementary method to verify authenticity of Manuka honey while the French authorities' laboratory uses the LC-SMRI method."* **(FOREIGN — EU.)** URL: https://food.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2023-03/official-controls_food-fraud_2021-2_honey_report_euca.pdf

**The honest caveats on NMR — do not oversell it.**
- **It is database-dependent, and Canada does not have its own database.** Alberta Beekeepers Commission brief to Parliament, 19 March 2021, verbatim **(Tier (d), Canadian industry submission)**:
> "We are also learning that NMR on its own is not sufficient. In order for an NMR to accurately identify anomalies in honey (or any other food), a database of that particular food must be developed. A database for honey is created from many samples of honey provided from different geographical regions. **The current NMR database is primarily made of up samples of honey collected from regions in the European Union.** Neither Bruker (NMR) or QSI laboratories (further testing) have a Canadian database of honey so when CFIA is indicating they are testing domestic supply – there is no certainty how accurately the current database would be representative of Canadian honey and therefore accurately test for adulteration."
This is an advocacy submission, not a finding — but it is a *structurally sound* point about how reference-database methods work, and it is the fairest available critique of the Canadian programme. Attribute it clearly to the Alberta Beekeepers Commission.
- **Method disagreement creates real legal difficulty.** European Commission, *From the Hives*, verbatim **(FOREIGN)**: *"The results differ from one method to another and are strongly contested by operators. They are a source of legal uncertainty for the sanctioning procedures."*
- **The international method base is still incomplete.** European Commission, *From the Hives*, Conclusion, verbatim **(FOREIGN)**: *"improved, harmonised and generally accepted analytical methods are still needed to increase the capability of official control laboratories to detect honey adulterated with sugar syrups"* … *"it appears that some investigations were solely relying on official laboratory control techniques which are known to be deficient in detecting and proving adulteration of honey and thus inefficient in deterring fraudsters if used alone."*

**CFIA is actively communicating about this.** FFR 2022-23, verbatim: *"We also published an updated Notice to Industry (Honey) to clarify the techniques used at the CFIA to detect adulteration of honey with foreign sugars."* Same report: CFIA chemist **Jonathan Haché** discussed honey authenticity testing on AAFC's *The First Sixteen* podcast; CFIA's *Inspect and Protect* podcast featured Saskatchewan beekeeper **Jake Berg** and CFIA's **Carmen Leung**, with a French edition featuring Quebec beekeeper **Scott Plante** and CFIA's **Alia'a Ghiba**. Useful named Canadian voices if you want audio or attributed quotes.

### 4.4 Pollen analysis (melissopalynology) — what it tells you and what it does not
**What it is.** Microscopic identification and counting of pollen grains in a honey sample, matched against reference collections.

**What it CAN do:** support claims about **botanical origin** (is this actually clover honey, or manuka?) and give an indication of **geographical origin**, because pollen assemblages are regionally characteristic.

**What it CANNOT do — the three limits, stated carefully:**
1. **It cannot detect sugar-syrup adulteration.** Refined cane, corn, rice or beet syrups contain no pollen. Adding syrup to real honey dilutes the pollen count but introduces nothing a microscope can identify as foreign. Pollen analysis is a test of *what the bees visited*, not of *what was added afterwards*. It answers a different question from the one CFIA's programme is asking.
2. **Pollen can be removed.** This is the decisive practical limit, and the European Commission documents it directly. *From the Hives*, verbatim **(FOREIGN — EU)**:
> "Other intelligence gathered during the course of the EU coordinated action – and currently being investigated – concerns the use and the blending of honey with sugar syrups onto the EU territory itself and **the removal of pollens on imported honey to mask its true geographical and botanical origin.**"
Ultrafiltration strips pollen. Once stripped, pollen analysis has nothing left to read — and the absence of pollen is not by itself proof of anything, because ordinary filtration to meet a grade spec also removes some.
3. **It is operator- and reference-dependent, slow, and low-throughput** — it requires trained palynologists and comprehensive reference databases, which is one reason it is unsuited to routine regulatory screening at scale. (Tier (b), review literature; e.g. *Legislative and forensic applications of melissopalynology*, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13312079/ and https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2589871X26000549 — **FOREIGN/academic**.)

**Bottom line for the script:** *Pollen analysis is a real, legitimate technique — for answering "what flowers, what region." It is not an adulteration test, and Canada's regulator does not use it as one. A jar with no pollen in it has not been proven fake, and a jar full of the right pollen has not been proven unadulterated.*

### 5.2 The vinegar and iodine tests — no authoritative source debunks them; here is the argument that does
**I could not find a regulator, university or scientific body specifically addressing the vinegar or iodine tests.** The only sources making these claims are SEO and commerce blogs (listed in §7). **Do not cite them.** Instead, make the argument from primary sources — it is stronger anyway, and it is entirely defensible:

**The iodine test is testing for the wrong substance.** Iodine turns blue-black in the presence of **starch**. Starch is not what Canada's regulator finds in adulterated honey. CFIA states, verbatim, exactly what it is looking for: *"Adulteration with foreign sugars, such as those derived from sugar cane, corn syrups or rice syrups"* (FFR 2024-25) and *"C4 sugars such as sugar cane and corn syrup … C3 sugars from sources such as rice, beets, barley and wheat"* (CFIA honey authenticity page). Those are **hydrolysed sugar syrups** — glucose, fructose, maltose and higher saccharides — not starch. A syrup is what you get *after* starch has been enzymatically broken down. An iodine test looks for the raw material that was already converted before the syrup was made.
**Safe on-air formulation:** *"The iodine test detects starch. Every adulterant Canada's regulator actually reports finding is a sugar syrup, not starch. You would be testing for a thing that isn't there."*

**The vinegar test is likewise aimed at a different adulterant class** — effervescence with a weak acid indicates carbonates, i.e. chalk-type bulking agents. CFIA's published honey findings, across seven years of reports, record **only** foreign-sugar adulteration. No carbonate or chalk finding appears in any CFIA honey report reviewed.
**Safe on-air formulation:** *"The vinegar test looks for chalk. CFIA's honey findings, every year from 2018 to 2025, are about sugar syrups."*

⚠️ Do **not** assert "the iodine test always fails" or "vinegar never works" as sourced fact. Assert the sourced fact: **the tests target adulterants that are not the adulterants CFIA reports finding in Canadian honey.**

### 5.3 The open-flame test — add a safety line
The reference video tells viewers to apply an open flame. Beyond being diagnostically worthless (NHB, above: outcome depends on moisture content), it involves a naked flame and a hot sugar syrup. A single neutral sentence — *"we're not going to tell you to set anything on fire, and it wouldn't tell you anything if you did"* — is a clean differentiator and carries no health framing.

### 5.4 The structural point that ties the whole segment together
This is the strongest line you have, and it is built entirely on primary sources:

> Canada's own regulator needs **two different laboratory instruments** — an isotope-ratio mass spectrometer and an NMR spectrometer — running **in parallel**, because each one misses what the other catches. CFIA's own 2018–19 numbers: isotope testing caught 15 of 52 failures; NMR caught 44; **34 of the NMR failures were invisible to isotope testing, and 7 of the isotope failures were invisible to NMR.** And NMR only works because it is compared against a reference profile "developed from the analysis of over 18,000 authentic honey samples."
> That is what it takes. **A glass of water and a thumbnail are not a third method.**

### 5.5 CRYSTALLIZATION — the definitive Canadian answer
**Tier (a) CONFIRMED PRIMARY — and this is the best-sourced item in the whole home-test section, because Canadian law settles it outright.**

**Crystallization is neither a sign of authenticity nor a defect. It is written into the legal definition of honey.**

**Canadian Food Compositional Standards s. 15.1.16(1)(d)**, verbatim — this is part of the definition of honey itself:
> "(d) **a fluid, viscous or partly or wholly crystallized consistency**"

**Canadian Grade Compendium Volume 6, s. 3(b)** — a requirement of the **top grade, Canada No. 1**, verbatim:
> "b) have a consistency that is **fluid, viscous or partly or wholly crystallized**"

So: **fully crystallized honey can be Canada No. 1.** Crystallization is expressly inside the standard and inside the top grade.

**The one and only place crystals become a regulatory problem is when the jar claims to be "liquid."** Grade Compendium Vol. 6, verbatim:
> s. 3(j) [Canada No. 1]: "**if its container bears the word 'liquid' or 'liquide'**, be clear, bright, uniform in colour and **free from visible crystals**"
> s. 5(e) [Canada No. 2]: "**if its container bears the word 'liquid' or 'liquide'**, have a colour that may be dull and cloudy or turbid or slightly uneven and show **not more than slight signs of crystallization** in the form of a light suspension or minor sedimentation of crystals"

And crystallized honey has its own required descriptor. Grade Compendium s. 3(k), verbatim: *"if its container bears the word 'creamed', 'en crème' or another word indicating that the contents are granulated, have a smooth fine texture and complete and uniform granulation."* CFIA labelling guidance reinforces this under B.01.006.1 FDR, verbatim: *"Additional descriptive words that tell about the true nature of prepackaged honey must be shown on the principal display panel when their omission will likely result in the food being mistaken for another food. This includes, but is not limited to, words such as liquid, creamed or pressed."*

**Corroborating non-Canadian source (FOREIGN, Tier (b)):** National Honey Board, verbatim: *"MYTH: Pure honey will not crystalize. FACT: Crystallization is the natural process by which the glucose in honey precipitates out of the liquid honey. Different varieties of honey will crystallize at different rates, and a few not at all."*

**⚠️ THE TRAP TO AVOID — and this is important, because the internet gets it backwards in both directions.** The widespread counter-myth — *"real honey crystallizes, so if yours doesn't, it's fake"* — is **also wrong**, and NHB says so in the same breath: *"a few [varieties will crystallize] not at all."* Crystallization rate depends on the glucose-to-fructose ratio of the nectar source, moisture, and storage temperature. Some authentic honeys crystallize in weeks; some essentially never do.

**The accurate, three-part on-air formulation:**
> *"One: crystallization is not a defect. Canadian law writes it into the definition of honey and into the top grade — Canada No. 1 honey is allowed to be 'partly or wholly crystallized.' Two: crystallization is not proof it's real either, because some genuine honeys barely crystallize at all. Three: the only time crystals are a regulatory issue is when the jar says 'liquid' — because that word is a claim, and Canada No. 1 liquid honey has to be 'free from visible crystals.' Crystals tell you about the flower and the fridge. They do not tell you about the syrup."*

## 3. THE GOOD LIST — traceability and labelling only

### 3.1 The regulatory backbone (a) — CFIA, "Labelling requirements for honey," SFCR
**Canadian honey:**
> "Where honey produced in Canada is graded under the SFCR, the container shall be marked with the words **'Product of Canada' / 'produit du Canada'** or **'Canadian Honey' / 'miel canadien'** [276, SFCR]."

**Imported honey:**
> "Containers of honey must be marked with the name of the country of origin preceded by the words **'Product of' / 'produit de'**" [277(1), 278, SFCR]
> "When honey is sourced from **multiple countries of origin, these countries must be named in descending order of their proportion**."

**Blends:**
> "**'A Blend of Canadian and (naming the source or sources) Honey'** ... or **'A Blend of (naming the source or sources) Honey and Canadian Honey'** ... **The sources must be named in descending order of their proportion** [279(1), 279(2), SFCR]."

**Grade names — Canadian and imported use DIFFERENT WORDS:**
> Produced in Canada: **"Canada No. 1", "Canada No. 2", "Canada No. 3"**
> Imported honey, and blends of imported and Canadian: **"No. 1", "No. 2", "No. 3"**
> "**Combining declarations of country of origin and grade is not permitted.**"

**Colour class** must appear near the grade name: **White / Golden / Amber / Dark** (Pfund scale).

**"Raw" (a), verbatim:**
> "Honey can be considered as 'raw' when it has not undergone any treatment or process such as heat treatment (other than minimal heating used for the purpose of extraction) or filtering. To avoid being misleading, specific information which clarifies the meaning of the term 'raw' may accompany the claim, such as 'unfiltered'."

**Standard container sizes (a):** 375 g or less, 500 g, 750 g, 1 kg, 1.5 kg, 2 kg, 3 kg, 5 kg.
**"Substandard" / "sous-régulier"** — non-compliant honey moved interprovincially may be so labelled, and is then exempt from grade requirements [18(2)(c), 306(3)(b) SFCR].

## 6. THE BUYING GUIDE — DONE HONESTLY

**Principle:** the aisle tests in the reference video are not decisive. What IS decisive is that Canadian honey labelling is **prescriptive** — the SFCR dictates the exact words. A shopper who knows the required wording can read origin and processing straight off the jar in about fifteen seconds with no equipment. Better segment than a glass of water, and no competitor has it.

### CHECK 1 — Read the origin line. The words are legally fixed. (a)

| What the jar says | What it legally means |
|---|---|
| "Product of Canada" / "Canadian Honey" | Produced in Canada, graded under the SFCR [276] |
| "Product of [country]" | Imported. Multiple countries **listed in descending order by proportion** [277, 278] — **the first country named is the largest share** |
| "A Blend of Canadian and [X] Honey" | **Canadian is the larger share** |
| "A Blend of [X] Honey and Canadian Honey" | **The imported honey is the larger share** |

**THE SINGLE MOST DECISIVE ON-SHELF SKILL IN THE VIDEO:** in a blend declaration, **word order is quantity order, and it is legally required to be.** "A Blend of Canadian and Argentinian Honey" and "A Blend of Argentinian Honey and Canadian Honey" look nearly identical on a shelf and mean opposite things. **Nobody teaches this. Teach it.**

### CHECK 2 — "Canada No. 1" versus "No. 1" (a)
Produced in Canada: **Canada No. 1 / No. 2 / No. 3.** Imported, and blends: **No. 1 / No. 2 / No. 3 — no "Canada."** And "combining declarations of country of origin and grade is not permitted." **The word "Canada" in front of the grade number is itself an origin signal**, printed at a regulated type height.

### CHECK 3 — But do NOT read the grade as a quality ranking (a)
Bee Maid, primary, verbatim: https://www.beemaid.com/faqs/
> **"Canadian honey is graded based on colour, moisture, and filtration, and this grading system is not a quality measurement."**
> **"BeeMaid Raw Honey meets Canada No. 1 standards for both colour and moisture, and is classified as Canada No. 3 for filtration. This is because Raw Honey is strained using a wider filtration process than our Creamed Honey, which is intended to keep it as close as possible to honey straight from our beekeepers' hives."**

**THE COUNTERINTUITIVE GOLD OF THE WHOLE SEGMENT.** A **Canada No. 3** can be the LESS processed honey. A shopper trained by other videos to chase "No. 1" will systematically pick the more filtered jar. **No competitor video knows this.**

### CHECK 4 — Price per 100 g, and know the floor (a, our arithmetic on StatCan)
National farm-gate **$2.87/lb = ~$0.63 per 100 g** · Prairies **$2.25-2.29/lb = ~$0.50** · B.C./Ontario **$5.20-5.96/lb = ~$1.15-1.31**.
A **500 g jar** contains roughly **$2.50 to $3.20** of Canadian honey at farm gate — before packing, bottling, freight, retail margin and tax.
**THE HONEST FRAMING: this tells you a claim is implausible; it does not tell you what is in the jar. Say that out loud.**
**PRODUCER ACTION: no live Canadian retail prices were captured — Loblaws and Save-On-Foods both blocked automated access. Price-check in store before recording.**

### CHECK 5 — "Raw" has a definition. "Pure" does not. (a)
CFIA defines "raw" (see 3.1) and recommends pairing it with "unfiltered." **"Pure" is not a defined honey term in the SFCR or FDR.** It carries no origin or processing meaning. **The competitor's invented definition of "pure" is exactly the error to correct.**

### CHECK 6 — "Liquid," "creamed" or "pressed" on the front (a)
FDR B.01.006.1: descriptive words about the true nature of honey "must be shown on the **principal display panel**... words such as liquid, creamed or pressed."

### CHECK 7 — Container size against the legal list (a)
SFCR ss.187, 188(1): 375 g or less, 500 g, 750 g, 1 kg, 1.5 kg, 2 kg, 3 kg, 5 kg. Minor — don't over-weight it.

### CHECK 8 — "Substandard" means what it says (a)
SFCR 18(2)(c), 306(3)(b): honey labelled "Substandard" / "sous-régulier" is exempt from meeting grade requirements. A real, regulated warning word.

### CHECK 9 — Traceability schemes, and their limits
True Source Certified and the 100% Ontario Honey logo — see 3.2. **Say the limit out loud: True Source verifies where honey came from, not that it came from Canada.**

### CHECK 10 — CRYSTALLIZATION: what it does and does not tell you
**True (a), Bee Maid verbatim:** "Over time, **all honey naturally crystallizes**" — "a normal, natural process that **does not affect its quality – only its appearance**." Exposure to air speeds it up; liquid honey "should not be refrigerated, as it will crystallize faster."
**The refutation (a), Bee Maid verbatim:** "**pasteurization helps keep it in a smooth, liquid state longer**" — flash heating to about 160°F then rapid cooling, "to prevent fermentation." Bee Maid offers both pasteurized and unpasteurized.

**THE CORRECTION, ONE LINE FOR VOICEOVER:** *A jar that stays liquid for a year and a half hasn't necessarily been faked. It's most likely been pasteurized — which is legal, disclosed on the label, and done by the very Canadian co-op that every one of these videos names as the good guy.*

**Crystallization tells you essentially nothing about authenticity.** Floral source, moisture, storage temperature and pasteurization all drive it. Colour likewise — Bee Maid: "clover nectar tends to be light and white, while honey from buckwheat flowers is much darker."

### CHECK 11 — The expiry-date myth, half-corrected
Audience belief (@triedtoflydidntwork, 5 likes): "Honey does not expire or go bad. If there's an expiry date on the jar, don't buy it." **Bee Maid states honey doesn't expire — but its own traceability system runs on "lot-identified Best Before Dates."** A best-before date is a freshness and traceability marker, not an expiry date, and its presence is not a defect signal. **Correct this gently; it is widely held among these viewers.**

### DO NOT USE — tests to explicitly retire on air
- **Water glass / sink-or-disperse.** Refuted in the competitor's own comments. Note it also appears in a CBC clip with Peter Awram — worth saying, because it shows how far this has travelled.
- **Thumb, vinegar, iodine, flame.** *(Part 2 owns the technical refutation.)*
- **Upside-down bubble-speed test** (@griegw8954) — newly circulating folk test, no basis.
- **Pollen presence as a proxy for authenticity.** CFIA's actual methods are **SIRA and NMR** against a library of 18,000+ authentic honeys. **Peter Awram's NMR machine is the real version of what these home tests pretend to be — which closes the loop back to the cold open.**
