## 2. Canadian rules for olive oil labelling, grading and origin

### 2.1 Food and Drug Regulations standard (B.09.003) — repealed and moved

- FDR B.09.003 now reads "[Repealed, SOR/2024-244, s. 60]" (Regulations current to 2026-09-03). The former text (version 2006-03-22 to 2024-12-17): "Olive Oil or Sweet Oil ... shall be the oil obtained from the fruit of the olive tree (Olea europaea L)" with fatty-acid ranges, "shall show negative results when tested for semi-siccative oil, olive-residue oil, cotton-seed oil, tea-seed oil or sesame seed oil", and may contain alpha-tocopherol "in a quantity sufficient to replace that lost during refining, if such an addition is declared on the label".
  URLs: https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/C.R.C.,_c._870/section-B.09.003.html and https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/c.r.c.,_c._870/section-B.09.003-20060322.html — tier (a)
- The standard now lives in the Canadian Food Compositional Standards (CFCS), incorporated by reference into the FDR, Volume 8 – Fats and Oils, **8.1.2**: "Olive Oil or Sweet Oil is the oil obtained from the fruit of the olive tree (Olea europaea L.); (a) has a fatty acid content that is (i) not less than 56.0 and not more than 83.0% oleic acid, (ii) not less than 7.5 and not more than 20.0% palmitic acid, (iii) not less than 3.5 and not more than 20.0% linoleic acid, (iv) not less than 0.5 and not more than 3.5% stearic acid, (v) not less than 0.3 and not more than 3.5% palmitoleic acid, (vi) not more than 1.5% linolenic acid, and (vii) not more than 0.05% myristic acid, calculated as methyl esters; (b) does not contain more than minute amounts of arachidic acid, behenic acid, gadoleic acid or lignoceric acid; (c) has (i) a relative density of not less than 0.910 and not more than 0.916 ... (ii) a refractive index of not less than 1.4677 ..." 
  URL: https://inspection.canada.ca/en/about-cfia/acts-and-regulations/list-acts-and-regulations/documents-incorporated-reference/canadian-food-compositional-standards-0 — tier (a)
- **Canadian law does not itself define "virgin" or "extra virgin."** The Canadian standard defines only "Olive Oil or Sweet Oil". Grades are handled by CFIA policy that points to IOC/Codex (2.2).

### 2.2 CFIA "Labelling requirements for fats and oils" (date modified 2025-11-10)

URL: https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/fats-and-oils — tier (a). Verbatim:
- Common name: "Products that are represented or sold as olive oil or pure olive oil are subject to the standard of composition for this product, which states in part, that olive oil shall be obtained from the fruit of the olive tree (Olea europaea L) [8.1.2, CFCS]." "When designated as "extra virgin" or "virgin" olive oil, strict requirements must be met."
- "Extra virgin" designation (under Composition claims): "Currently, there are no definitions for "extra virgin" applicable to any vegetable oil except for olive oil. Products represented as "extra virgin olive oil" and "virgin olive oil" must meet the standards and definitions in the International Olive Council (IOC) and the Codex Standard for Olive Oils and Olive Pomace Oils (CXS 33-1981). These standards require, among other things, that the extra virgin olive oils and virgin olive oils must be cold pressed, and do not contain any refined olive oil, olive pomace oils, or any other types of oils. These must possess superior quality based on chemical composition and sensory characteristics." "Because there are no internationally recognized standards that define "extra virgin" for other vegetable oils, the use of the term "extra virgin" is only acceptable for olive oil."
- So: Canada does recognise IOC and Codex grades, by CFIA policy incorporated through the general "false or misleading" prohibitions (FDA s.5(1); SFCA s.6(1)) — not by a Canadian grade regulation.
- Pomace: "Acceptable common names for oil derived from olive residues are "olive pomace oil" and "olive residual oil" (in French, "huile de grignons d'olive" and "huile de résidus d'olive", respectively). ... The terms "sansa oil" / "huile de sansa" are not acceptable."
- "Pure": the page treats "olive oil or pure olive oil" as the same standardized product (8.1.2). No separate definition of "pure".
- "Light": the page contains no rule specific to "light"/"extra light" olive oil. (The general nutrient-content-claim rules apply to "light" claims; the fats-and-oils page does not address it for olive oil. Do not assert a Canadian definition of "light olive oil".)
- "Cold pressed" / "first pressed" / "first cold pressed": the fats-and-oils page does not define these terms for olive oil. They are enforced as claims that must be truthful; CFIA's testing pages say a "cold pressed" or "unrefined" claim is unsatisfactory "if refined oil is detected" (1.6). The EU has precise definitions (item 3.3); Canada does not.
- Where no Canadian standard exists, "other national or international standards are referred to in order to determine an appropriate common name. For example, if there is a specific definition for a fat or oil in a Codex standard, the Codex defined name ... would be an acceptable common name".
- Historical CFIA "Olive Oil Compliance" letter (2011, archived at Library and Archives) exists: https://epe.lac-bac.gc.ca/100/206/301/cfia-acia/2011-09-21/inspection.gc.ca/english/fssa/labeti/inform/olicone.shtml?nodisclaimer=1 — tier (a) (not opened; pointer only).

### 2.3 Country of origin for imported oil — what is actually required

CFIA "Country of origin on food labels" (industry page, date modified 2022-07-19), verbatim:
- "All prepackaged food products sold in Canada are required to be labelled with the name and principal place of business of the company responsible for the product, such as the importer or manufacturer."
- "When a food product is wholly manufactured outside of Canada, the label must show that the product is imported. This information can be provided in one of three ways: the name and principal place of business of the foreign manufacturer; the statement "imported for" / "importé pour" or "imported by" / "importé par" followed by the name and principal place of business of the Canadian company; the name and principal place of business of the Canadian company with the country of origin of the product"
- "In addition, it is mandatory to state the country of origin on some specific imported prepackaged products": "wine and brandy, dairy products, honey, fish and fish products, fresh fruits and vegetables, shell egg, processed egg, meat products, maple products, processed fruit and vegetable products". "Generally, for the above foods, the words "Product of [Name the country of origin]" must appear on the label."
- **Olive oil / edible oils are NOT on that list.** URL: https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/country-origin — tier (a)
- Consumer version (date modified 2026-09-10) says the same three options: https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/consumers/country-origin — tier (a)
- CFIA's own words in the 2023-24 Food Fraud report (1.3): "It is not mandatory to declare the country of origin on oils labels. But a company may choose to do so voluntarily, provided it is truthful and not misleading. When a food is wholly manufactured outside of Canada, the label must indicate that the food is imported." — tier (a)
- "Name and principal place of business" page (date modified 2022-07-06): the "imported by/for" requirement applies to "foods wholly manufactured, processed or produced outside of Canada that are imported in bulk and repackaged into a consumer prepackaged food at a level of trade other than retail" as well; a product is considered wholly manufactured outside Canada unless processing in Canada modifies its nature (blending/grinding etc. do not count). Regulatory citation SFCR s.223(1)-(2).
  URL: https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/name-and-principal-place-business — tier (a)
- "Origin claims on food labels" (date modified 2023-12-06): voluntary claims must be truthful; "Made in Canada" requires last substantial transformation in Canada plus a qualifier ("Made in Canada from imported ingredients" / "...from domestic and imported ingredients"); "Packaged in Canada" and "Processed in Canada" are acceptable when truthful. The page has no olive-oil-specific rule and no rule that the bottling country must be named.
  URL: https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/origin-claims — tier (a)
- CFIA 2019 consultation "Consultation on options for indicating the origin of imported food" (June 22 – Sept 4, 2019) proposed extending origin labelling to "most imported foods" with wording options "imported from [origin]" or "from [origin]". The page does not record an outcome; as of 2026-09-25 the industry page still lists only the ten mandatory categories, so no such expansion is in force.
  URL: https://inspection.canada.ca/en/about-cfia/transparency/consultations-and-engagement/completed/proposed-flm/origin-imported-food — tier (a)

Conclusion on the competitor claim "Canada requires only the bottling country on the label": **NOT SUPPORTED.** Canada does not require any country of origin on olive oil. It requires the label to show the product is imported, which can be satisfied by "Imported by/for [Canadian company]" with no country at all. If a company volunteers an origin ("Product of Italy"), the general prohibition on false or misleading representations applies (FDA s.5(1); SFCA s.6(1)). There is no Canadian rule that "Product of X" for olive oil means the olives were grown in X, nor a rule that it means "bottled in X"; CFIA has not published olive-oil-specific origin guidance. (By contrast, the EU mandates origin on extra virgin and virgin olive oil and defines it by where olives were harvested and milled — item 3.3.)

## 3. International grade standards (chemistry/sensory definitions, not health claims)

### 3.1 IOC Trade Standard COI/T.15/NC No 3/Rev. 21 (July 2025)

URL: https://www.internationaloliveoil.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/TRADE-STANDARD-REV-21_EN.pdf — tier (a). (Rev. 20, Nov 2024: https://www.internationaloliveoil.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/TRADE-STANDARD-REV-20_EN.pdf)
Verbatim:
- "2.1.1. Virgin olive oils are oils which are obtained from the fruit of the olive tree (Olea europaea L.) solely by mechanical or other physical means under conditions, particularly thermal conditions, that do not lead to alterations in the oil, and which have not undergone any treatment other than washing, decantation, centrifugation and filtration."
- "(i) Extra virgin olive oil: virgin olive oil which has a free acidity, expressed as oleic acid, of not more than 0.80 grams per 100 grams and the other physico–chemical and organoleptic characteristics of which correspond to those fixed for this category in this standard."
- "(ii) Virgin olive oil: virgin olive oil which has a free acidity, expressed as oleic acid, of not more than 2.0 grams per 100 grams ..."
- "(iii) Ordinary virgin olive oil: ... not more than 3.3 grams per 100 grams ..."
- "Lampante virgin olive oil: virgin olive oil which has a free acidity expressed as oleic acid, of more than 3.3 grams per 100 grams ... It is intended for refining or for technical use."
- "2.1.2. Refined olive oil: olive oil obtained from virgin olive oils by refining methods which do not lead to alterations in the initial glyceridic structure. It has a free acidity ... of not more than 0.30 grams per 100 grams"
- "2.1.3. Olive oil composed of refined olive oil and virgin olive oils: oil consisting of a blend of refined olive oil and virgin olive oils fit for consumption as they are. It has a free acidity ... of not more than 1.00 gram per 100 grams"
- "2.2. Olive pomace oil is the oil obtained by treating olive pomace with solvents or other physical treatments ..." Footnote: "Olive pomace oil cannot be sold with the designation or definition "olive oil"."
- Free acidity is only one parameter; "extra virgin" also requires the sensory result (median of defects = 0, median of fruity > 0) and other chemistry (peroxide, UV K232/K270/ΔK, etc.). These are grade/quality definitions, not health statements.

### 3.2 Codex Standard for Olive Oils and Olive Pomace Oils, CXS 33-1981

URL (FAO copy, Rev. 2015): https://www.fao.org/input/download/standards/88/CXS_033e_2015.pdf — tier (a). "Adopted in 1981. Revision: 1989, 2003, 2015. Amendment: 2009, 2013."
Verbatim: "Extra virgin olive oil: virgin olive oil with a free acidity, expressed as oleic acid, of not more than 0.8 grams per 100 grams"; "Virgin olive oil: ... not more than 2.0 grams per 100 grams"; "Ordinary virgin olive oil: ... not more than 3.3 grams per 100 grams"; "Refined olive oil ... not more than 0.3 grams per 100 grams"; "Olive oil: oil consisting of a blend of refined olive oil and virgin olive oils ... not more than 1 gram per 100 grams"; "Olive-pomace oil: oil consisting of a blend of refined olive-pomace oil and virgin olive oils ... not more than 1 gram per 100 grams". Organoleptic table: "Extra virgin olive oil Me = 0 [defect] Me > 0 [fruity]; Virgin olive oil 0 < Me ≤ 2.5; Ordinary virgin olive oil 2.5 < Me ≤ 6.0".

### 3.3 EU marketing standard (useful contrast on origin and "cold" terms)

Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2022/2104 of 29 July 2022. URL: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2104 — tier (a). Verbatim:
- Art. 8(1): "For extra virgin olive oil and virgin olive oil ... the place of origin shall appear on the label." Art. 8(2): for other categories "no place of origin shall be displayed on the label."
- Art. 8(3)(b): blends must say "'blend of olive oils of European Union origin'", "'blend of olive oils not of European Union origin'", or "'blend of olive oils of European Union origin and not of European Union origin'".
- Art. 8(6): "The place of origin mentioning a Member State or the Union shall correspond to the geographical area in which the olives were harvested and in which the mill where the oil was extracted from the olives is situated."
- Art. 8(7): "If the olives have been harvested in a Member State or third country other than that in which the mill where the oil was extracted from the olives is situated, the place of origin shall contain the following wording: '(extra) virgin olive oil obtained in (the Union or the name of the Member State or third country concerned) from olives harvested in (the Union or the name of the Member State or third country concerned)'."
- Art. 10: "(a) the indication 'first cold pressing' may appear only for extra virgin or virgin olive oils obtained at a temperature below 27 °C from a first mechanical pressing of the olive paste by a traditional extraction system using hydraulic presses; (b) the indication 'cold extraction' may appear only for extra virgin or virgin olive oils obtained at a temperature below 27 °C by percolation or centrifugation of the olive paste".
- Art. 11: harvesting year may be shown "only if 100 % of the contents of the container come from that harvest".
Key contrast: in the EU, bottling location is NOT origin; origin = where olives were harvested/milled. Canada has no equivalent rule for oils.

### 3.4 EU PDO / PGI

Legal basis Regulation (EU) 2024/1143 (in force 13 May 2024), replacing 1151/2012.

### 4.1 Kumar v. Salov North America Corp. (Filippo Berio), N.D. Cal. No. 4:14-cv-02411-YGR

- ALLEGATION (from the court's Feb 3, 2015 order, quoting the complaint): "The words "Imported from Italy" appear prominently" on the front label; the olives "are grown and pressed in many other countries, such as Spain, Greece, and Tunisia, and the oils are shipped to Italy where they are blended and bottled for export"; Kumar "alleges that the "Imported from Italy" statement on the product labels is false". A separate "extra virgin" claim (refined oil/clear bottles) was also alleged.
- Salov's defence as recorded by the court (this is the passage competitor videos appear to misdescribe as an "admission"): "Salov argues the complaint does not allege a plausible claim ... because no reasonable consumer would understand "Imported from Italy" to mean that the product was made entirely from Italian-grown olives. Further, Kumar has alleged that she read the "best by" statement on the back of the product label, which also contains a statement that the product is "Packed in Italy with select extra virgin olive oils from Italy, Spain, Greece & Tunisia."" — i.e., the back label itself disclosed multi-country sourcing; Salov argued the front statement was not misleading in context. This is a litigation argument, not an admission of wrongdoing.
- FINDING (procedural only), Order of Feb 3, 2015, Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers: "Nor can the Court find, as a matter of law on the pleadings alone, that a reasonable consumer would not understand "Imported from Italy" to mean that the product was made exclusively made from olives grown in Italy. Thus, the motion to dismiss on these grounds is DENIED." Breach of contract claims dismissed without leave to amend; remaining claims survived. No finding that the label was in fact misleading.

## 7. UNVERIFIED / DO-NOT-USE (competitor-video claims checked)

1. **"Canada requires only the bottling country on the label."** NOT SUPPORTED. CFIA: origin is "not mandatory" on oil labels; imported food must show it is imported, which may be done with "Imported by/for [Canadian company]" and no country at all; the mandatory "Product of" list excludes oils (item 2.3). There is no Canadian "bottling country" rule.
2. **"Salov's lawyers admitted the oil isn't made in Italy."** MISCHARACTERISATION. The court record shows Salov argued the back label already said "Packed in Italy with select extra virgin olive oils from Italy, Spain, Greece & Tunisia" and that no reasonable consumer would read "Imported from Italy" as 100% Italian olives. That is a defence, not an admission; the case settled with no liability finding and the Ninth Circuit referred to Salov's "legitimate defense" (item 4.1). Do not say "admitted".
3. **"Colavita's chemistry results."** PARTLY VERIFIABLE BUT DATED AND NON-CANADIAN. UC Davis 2010: Colavita SAC sample graded extra virgin; SF and LA samples graded "virgin" by sensory; LA sample K268 0.25 (limit 0.22). UC Davis 2011: 10 of 18 Colavita samples failed both sensory panels (56%); all passed IOC FFA/FAP/PV. No adulteration found. Samples bought in California in 2010; UC Davis itself says results should not characterise current product. Only usable with those caveats (item 4.3).
4. **"7,245 litres removed."** VERIFIED — CFIA 2024-25 report: "7,245 L of adulterated olive oil" (item 1.2). The 2023-24 figure was 37,300 L; do not mix years.
5. **"92 samples / 24% not authentic" from a "December 2025 report."** VERIFIED WITH CORRECTION — these are the 2023-24 fiscal-year figures (70/92 satisfactory, 22/92 unsatisfactory = 24%), in the report The Western Producer says was released Dec 10, 2025 (snippet only). "Not authentic" overstates: "unsatisfactory" includes false "extra virgin"/"cold pressed" grade claims as well as adulteration (item 1.3).
6. **"85 samples / 28 failed / 67% satisfactory / worst category."** VERIFIED — CFIA 2024-25 report (item 1.2). Attach CFIA's caveat: "Results of targeted sampling are not representative of overall compliance in the Canadian marketplace."
7. **"Kirkland tested by Bureau Veritas."** NOT VERIFIED FROM A PRIMARY SOURCE. Costco's own pages say the 100% Italian EVOO "is certified by an independent third-party organization, with each bottle carrying an ISO-certified sticker" without naming it; costco.com/costco.ca product-page text does not mention Bureau Veritas; no Bureau Veritas page confirming it was found. Only lifestyle/aggregator sites (tier c) name Bureau Veritas from the label image. Say "third-party certified (unnamed on Costco's site)" unless a bottle is photographed.
8. **"Operation Mamma Mia."** NOT VERIFIED as the name of the 2015 Turin (Guariniello) investigation in any named outlet opened here.
9. **Italian brands "convicted."** NOT SUPPORTED. The Turin criminal file was archived on 23 Nov 2016 (single named source); the AGCM fines were administrative; Lidl's fine was annulled by TAR Lazio (2018) and the annulment upheld by the Consiglio di Stato (2020). The Deoleo €300,000 fine's appeal status is unverified. The only court-upheld item is the 2025 Florence first-instance ruling on a 2017 French shipment (administrative sanction).
10. **Canadian Bertolli class action "settled"/"certified."** NOT VERIFIED. Only a law-firm intake page exists; the linked settlement is the U.S. one; Quebec registry not checked (blocked).
11. **CFIA "December 2025 report" as a distinct report.** There is no separate December 2025 report; the 2023-24 report (released Dec 2025 per Western Producer snippet, updated May 2026) and the 2024-25 report (released June 25, 2026) are the only two recent ones. No 2025-26 report exists as of 2026-09-25.
12. **Metro "Selection — Imported from Italy" and PC/Great Value origin wording.** Search-snippet only; pages blocked. Re-verify by hand before use.
13. **CNBC 2026 price figures (€3.9/kg; €9.3/kg Jan 2024 record).** Snippet only; page blocked. Use IOC bulletins (verified) instead, or open CNBC manually.
14. **Any statement that CFIA found a named brand adulterated.** NOT SUPPORTED — no annual report names an olive oil brand; sample-level data is on the Open Government Portal and would need to be read directly.

# OLIVE OIL — STRUCTURE NOTE (from the four videos that worked)

Sources read in full: Food Fraud Watch "9 OLIVE OILS To AVOID And 5 That Are Actually Olive Oil" (1.22M, 60x, 20 May 2026, 3,082 words); Consumer Exposed "12 Olive Oil Brands to Avoid (And 5 That Are Actually Real)" (171K, 7.9x, 26 Jul 2026, ~4,300 words); Canada Insight "We Sent 10 Popular Olive Oils in Canada to a Lab" (84K, 80x, 2 Sep 2026, 4,257 words); Canada Food Insider "4 Olive Oils You Must AVOID (And 3 That Are Actually Real)" (73K, 2.3x, 24 Jun 2026, ~2,300 words). Also skimmed: Broken Canada "9 Olive Oils Canadians Should AVOID" (51K, 1.7x).

## What all four do (the skeleton to copy)

1. COLD OPEN = one hard number in the first sentence, then "it's in your pantry", then the count, then the sources, then a skip-ahead hook.
   - FFW: "Roughly 73% of the top selling extra virgin olive oil brands are not actually extra virgin olive oil… some of these are probably in your pantry. We're exposing nine… and five that are real… based on a study by the University of California, Italian investigations, and numerous class action lawsuits."
   - CE: "More than 70% of imported samples failed… Not close calls. Failed… We're going through 12 of them today, starting with lower concern and building toward the most serious… The worst brand on this list is also the most trusted name in the category. That one is last."
   - CI: "85 bottles of olive oil went into a federal lab in this country. 28 of them failed. That's one in three and it was the worst result of any food the Canadian Food Inspection Agency tested that entire year. Worse than honey. Worse than fish… the report doesn't name a single brand."
   - CFI: "In December 2025, the CFIA released its Food Fraud Annual Report… 92 olive oil samples… 24% were not authentic… One brand is in millions of Canadian pantries right now. Marketed as Italian, their own attorneys admitted in federal court it isn't… brand number two, that's the one with the court records. Don't skip ahead."
   → CC open: the CFIA number (whichever the report actually says), "worse than honey, worse than fish", "the report names no brand, so we read the labels", the count (seven to think twice about, two worth the money), and the hook "number one is the bottle in a million Canadian fridge doors".

2. YARDSTICK BLOCK (CI, CFI) before the brands, 60–120 seconds: what the grade words legally mean (IOC/Codex: extra virgin, virgin, refined/"pure"/"light"/"classico", pomace); origin statement ("Product of" vs "Packed in"/"Imported from" vs "blend of EU and non-EU"); harvest date vs best-before; bottle material; price per 100 mL; and the Canada-only check: read the English line and the French line ("Produit d'Italie" vs "Emballé en Italie"; "première pression à froid" is a historical phrase). CI: "write that number on your hand. We're going to use it seven more times."

3. COUNTDOWN, worst last (all four). Per-brand beat, 250–350 words:
   a. recognition hook (the bottle everyone knows: "your mother bought it"),
   b. front of label vs back of label (what the story says vs what the origin line says),
   c. the documented record (owner/parent, lawsuit/settlement, study) — labelled as US case / allegation / settlement,
   d. the honest concession (CI does this every brand: "full credit where it's earned", "there is nothing wrong with that"),
   e. one takeaway habit ("count the countries", "read the grade word", "buy the specific bottle, not the logo").
   Every video also has ONE "the word that fools you" interlude: CFI = the Canadian origin loophole; CI = "light" (same calories, not a grade); CE = "pure" (a refined grade, not a purity claim); FFW = "made in Italy means bottled in Italy". CC uses two: "light/pure/classico are refined grades" and "Packed in vs Product of".

4. LIKE ASK at 40–55%: FFW after #7 ("take a moment to click the like button"); CI before the last two ("Quick favor before we get to the last two… it helps this reach somebody who's about to spend $20 on the wrong bottle"); CE after #6. Exactly one ask, phrased as a favour to another shopper.

5. THE TURN: "Now the good news / the ones I actually pull off the shelf." Picks are justified by a checkable reason, never by taste alone: third-party certification (Kirkland/Bureau Veritas claim; COOC seal; PDO/PGI), traceability code (Terra Delyssa), single named country (Gallo), harvest date printed, dark glass/tin, and the per-100 mL number. Kirkland is in all four videos; Terra Delyssa and Gallo in CI; California Olive Ranch in CFI/FFW/CE; Favuzzi in CFI.

6. COUNTER TEST the viewer can do (CI: the fridge test does not work; smell then taste; CE: harvest date, seal, named producer, named region, "count what's missing"). Written as instructions to the viewer, never as something we did.

7. RUN-BACK (CI): one line per brand in order. Then the split: "best quality vs best value are two different questions". Then the rule at the till (CFI: "Where did the olives come from? Is there a harvest date? Is it in dark glass or tin? If the bottle can't answer all three, put it back."). Comment ask (CI: "Which olive oil have you been buying? Does it name one country on the back or four? Go look right now. I'll wait."). Sign-off mantra (CI: "Read the label, not the ad."). Disclaimer line at the very end (CI).

## What the winners do that CC's rules forbid (and the substitute)
- Polyphenol/antioxidant/oleocanthal/"for your health" lines (FFW, CE, CFI) → replaced by grade, origin, date, bottle, price. CI itself says "I'm making no health claim here at all. I'm telling you the number on the panel is the same and the price often isn't." That is the model sentence.
- "Admitted in federal court" (CFI) → "settled, without admitting wrongdoing" if that is what the record shows.
- "UC Davis found Bertolli 72% failed" applied to Canadian bottles → the study is a 2010–2011 US study on US retail samples; say so; never imply today's Canadian bottle was tested.
- "We sent to a lab" (CI) → CC never says "we tested"; the CFIA report is the lab, and it names no brands.
- Named-reviewer/Reddit complaints (FFW) → documented consumer record only if from a named outlet or the retailer's own review page, otherwise dropped.
