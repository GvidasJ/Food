## 1. Can the working title be supported?

**"$1 Pasta vs $5 Pasta in Canada — Can You Taste the Difference?"**

### (a) The "$1" end for a 450–500 g pack — supported at $1.25, not $1.00

- Cheapest primary-source single pack found: **Dollarama, "Buon Gusto Penne Pasta (450 g)", "Buon Gusto Elbow Pasta (450 g)" and "Buongusto Spaghettini Pasta (450 g)", $1.25 CAD each = $0.278/100 g** (our arithmetic: 1.25 ÷ 4.5). Source: shop.dollarama.com storefront for the Dollarama store at 1-410 Main St E, Kingsville ON (store 43118689), currency printed as "CAD", badge "Many in stock", retrieved 2026-09-22. Verifier confirmed (R3-D1, D2, D3). Caveat: the storefront is a DoorDash-powered delivery/pickup channel whose own strings say "Prices may differ between Delivery and Pickup"; it is one Ontario store's online price, not a filmed shelf price. The Instacart Dollarama listing shows the same "$1.25 / 450 g" for "Buongusto Spaghetti Pasta" but its structured data tags the currency "USD" and its footnote calls the price an estimate (R2-34, narrowed); shop.dollarama.com, which prints "CAD", is the primary.
- Next-cheapest 450–500 g packs are third-party-indexed only (tier b, Flipp e-commerce index of Walmart Canada, not verified): "Primo Water Primo Spaghetti 454 G" $1.27 ($0.280/100 g); "Catelli Classic All-Natural Spaghetti Pasta 454g" $1.77 sale / $2.47 regular; "Divella Spaghetti 500 G" $1.97; "Great Value Organic Spaghetti 450g" $1.97.
- **No true $1.00 pack of regular wheat pasta was found from any primary or named source.** The only sub-$1 row ("Fiorfiore Organic Spaghetti 500 G" at $0.97 in the Walmart index) is flagged anomalous and must not be used unless seen on shelf (R3-W21, unresolved).
- Cheaper per gram than any 450–500 g pack, but only in 900 g bags (all tier b, Flipp): Great Value Spaghetti 900 G $1.97 ($0.219/100 g); Selection Pasta 500–900 g $1.97 "ALWAYS GREAT PRICE" ($0.219 at 900 g); No Name Pasta 900 g $2.00 flyer ($0.222). A shopper buying the 900 g house brand pays less per gram than at Dollarama. This is why the video must compare per 100 g, not per box.

### (b) The "$5" end for a 500 g pack — supported as a listed price; stock status is the weakness

- **Well.ca "La Molisana Extra Di Lusso #1 Spaghetto Quadrato", 500 g, $5.29 = $1.058/100 g; "La Molisana Extra DI Lusso #20 Penne Rigate", 500 g, $5.29** (separate page). Verifier confirmed the names, sizes and prices but found **both out of stock ("notify me when available") at retrieval** (R3-E2, narrowed).
- **Well.ca "La Molisana Organic #15 Spaghetti", 500 g, $5.27 (priceCurrency CAD) = $1.054/100 g** — availability metadata "PreOrder", "Notify Me" button, so not purchasable at retrieval (R3-E1, narrowed). Well.ca og:description for this item: "Product of Italy. Made from 100% Italian Organic Durum Wheat." (label fact as printed on the retailer page).
- **Well.ca "La Molisana Organic #5 Fettuccine", 500 g, $5.00 (og:price:currency CAD), page aria-label "Struck through old price $5.27"** = $1.000/100 g (R3-E4, narrowed: page shows no "sale" label and no unit price; the $5.27 is Well.ca's own "was" figure with no stated date).
- **Rummo Spaghetti 500 g, three Ontario grocers, verifier-confirmed:** ItalianMart.ca "RUMMO | Spaghetti N.3 | 500GR" $4.59 CAD (currency printed in page metadata; live) = $0.918/100 g (R3-I1); Denninger's "Rummo - Spaghetti - 500 g" $4.99 CAD, compare_at 4.99, "Available on Instacart only" = $0.998/100 g (R3-I5); Food Depot Toronto "Rummo Spaghetti No. 3 500g" $4.89 CAD, page shows "Sold out" and "Limited stock. Contact us to inquire." = $0.978/100 g (R3-I6, narrowed).
- De Cecco and Barilla are **not** the $5 end in any listing: De Cecco 454 g $2.98 (Walmart index), $3.99 (London Drugs index), $2.99 sale/$3.49 regular (Longo's flyer); Barilla 410 g $2.44 (Walmart index), $2.49 (Shoppers flyer). All tier b.

**Verdict on the price framing:** "$1 vs $5" is a fair description of the extremes for a single 450–500 g pack on Canadian listings on 2026-09-22 — provided the video says "$1.25" on screen and shows that the $5 pack is La Molisana (Well.ca $5.00–$5.29) or Rummo ($4.59–$4.99 at specialty grocers), not De Cecco or Barilla. The pack multiple is 4.232× ($5.29 ÷ $1.25); per 100 g it is 3.809× ($1.058 ÷ $0.2778 unrounded) because the Dollarama pack is 450 g and the La Molisana pack 500 g (R3-X1, interpretation). Every $5-end listing was either out of stock, pre-order, Instacart-only or at a specialty online grocer at retrieval, so **the producer must film a $5 pack on a physical shelf before the title is locked**.

### (c) The taste claim — not supported by anything the channel has done

- No test was performed by the channel (house rule 5). No independent, blind, multi-brand Canadian test of dry pasta was found (R4-29; CBC Marketplace, Globe and Mail searched; Toronto Star and Canadian Living block the crawler so "not found" is not "proven absent"). Protégez-Vous has a paywalled spaghetti comparison that could not be read (R4-26). The only Canadian award found is an entry-fee, editor-judged program (Chatelaine Pantry Awards: "$350 entry fee and five samples", "taste test by the editors in the Chatelaine Test Kitchen"; blind/not blind not stated) (R4-28, V).
- The only sensory results in hand are non-Canadian and quarantined: Altroconsumo (Italy, 26 Jan 2026, 20 spaghetti, consumer tasting; page does not say blind); Test-Achats (Belgium, 39 spaghetti, chefs on anonymised samples, published 20 Dec 2021, results paywalled); Carpentieri et al. 2024 (three unnamed Italian spaghetti, sensory panel). None involves a Canadian product and none may be transferred (house rule 6).
- Peer-reviewed process literature (section 2, group J) documents *what* changes with die material and drying temperature (surface roughness, firmness, cooking loss, colour) but is laboratory work on generic semolina, not a taste verdict on any retail brand.

So the question in the title can be **asked**, but it can only be **answered** on camera by (i) attributed third-party tests with scope and date, and (ii) a clearly labelled "our own kitchen comparison, not a laboratory test" if the channel performs the section-5 protocol from R4 (R4-35, proposal only, not performed). The script must never say "we tested" in the laboratory sense and must never say the $1 pasta is worse (or the same) as a fact.

### Proposed titles

1. **"$1.25 Pasta vs $5 Pasta in Canada — What Does the Extra $4 Actually Buy?"** — every element is supported: the $1.25 and $5.00–$5.29 packs exist on Canadian listings; "what it buys" is answered by label facts, the regulations, the process literature and the price-per-100 g ladder, with no taste verdict required.
2. **"Dollarama Pasta vs Italian Import: The Price-Per-100 g Truth (Canada, 2026)"** — same evidence base, leads with the per-gram framing that the competitor's audience demanded (R5-18, interpretation), and avoids a taste promise entirely.

If the producer insists on keeping "Can You Taste the Difference?", it is defensible only as a question, with the on-screen answer attributed to named tests and a labelled kitchen comparison.

## 6. The five strongest beats for the video (one line each, anchored to section 2)

1. **The real ladder:** the cheapest single pack is Dollarama's Buon Gusto at $1.25 for 450 g ($0.278/100 g) and the dearest is La Molisana Extra Di Lusso at $5.29 for 500 g ($1.058/100 g) — 4.232× per pack, 3.809× per 100 g — while the 900 g house brands (tier b) are cheaper per gram than the dollar store, so the honest comparison is per 100 g. [Rows R3-D1/D2/D3, R3-E2, R3-I1/I5/I6; interpretation R3-X1]
2. **Canada has no recipe for pasta:** the entire "Alimentary Paste" heading of the Food and Drug Regulations is two sections — 4% egg-yolk solids for "egg" pasta and the enrichment table — so "durum", "bronze die" and "slow dried" are not regulated terms; what a shopper can verify is the printed ingredient list and origin line. [R1-01, R1-02, R1-03, R1-06, R1-08, R1-10/11]
3. **Italy's rule is stricter than Canada's — and much of the wheat is Canadian:** DPR 187/2001 defines "pasta di semola di grano duro" as durum semolina and water only (soft wheat tolerated to 3%), Italian labels must state the wheat's country of cultivation and milling through 31 December 2026, and Canada shipped 774,530,000 kg of durum to Italy in 2024 (first by value and quantity), with Italy taking 16% of Canada's 5.8 Mt of exports in 2024-25. [R1-17, R1-19, R1-23, R1-31, R1-27, R1-30]
4. **What the science says the extra dollars buy — and don't:** bronze dies give a rougher, more porous strand that is 20–30% weaker and slower to extrude; high-temperature drying raises firmness and lowers cooking loss (Fang & Khan 1996: 7.9% → 6.5% cooking loss, 4.7 → 5.9 g·cm firmness from 40 °C to 90 °C) at the cost of browning; a 60-sample Italian survey found "slow-dried" wording "not sufficient" to indicate quality and high heat markers "even in some 'artisan pasta'"; and Italpasta's own Canadian lines print the same ingredient list on the regular and Artisan packs. [R4-06, R4-08, R4-10, R4-12, R4-20/21]
5. **Nobody in Canada has tested this blind:** the only Canadian award is an entry-fee ($350, five samples) editors' tasting; Protégez-Vous's comparison is paywalled and unread; the only Canadian-university sensory study found drying regime changed only perceived firmness (7.2 vs 6.8 on a 15 cm scale) on lab macaroni — so any taste verdict must come from a labelled kitchen comparison or a named foreign test, never "we tested". [R4-28, R4-15, R4-30/31 quarantined; unresolved U13]

## 2. CFIA origin claims: "Product of Canada", "Made in Canada", and imported-food origin

**CFIA industry guidance, "Origin claims on food labels" (a).** https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/origin-claims
> "A food product may use the claim "Product of Canada" when all or virtually all major ingredients, processing, and labour used to make the food product are Canadian."
> "Generally, the percentage referred to as very little or minor is considered to be less than a total of 2% of the product"
> "Products that contain foreign ingredients, regardless of their source, are not eligible to bear a "Product of Canada" claim."
> "If the "Made in Canada" claim is used, it must also include a qualifying statement to indicate that the food product is made in Canada from imported ingredients or a combination of imported and domestic ingredients."
> "The qualifying statements that can be used include "Made in Canada from domestic and imported ingredients" or "Made in Canada from imported ingredients"."
> "A substantial transformation occurs when a food product undergoes processing which changes its nature and becomes a new product bearing a new name commonly understood by the consumer."
> "A claim regarding the origin of ingredients within a food may be made, provided the claim is truthful and not misleading (for example, "Contains Italian olive oil")."
> "A claim regarding processing or added value that took place in a particular geographical origin may also be made on a food, provided the claim is truthful and not misleading (for example, "Packaged in Ireland")."

CFIA consumer page (a), https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/consumers/canadian-food:
> "Product of Canada means the processing and labour are Canadian and a significant amount of the ingredients are Canadian."
> "Made in Canada means the last substantial transformation of the product occurred in Canada."
> "There is no official logo for Canadian food products and a maple leaf on a product does not always mean the product is Canadian."

CFIA FAQ (a), https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/origin-claims/frequently-asked-questions:
> "The use of a maple leaf or other similar symbol should not imply that the product is wholly or partially Canadian without a clear domestic content statement."

Note on the "98% / 51%" numbers: the CFIA food pages read above express the food test as "all or virtually all" / "less than a total of 2%" and "last substantial transformation"; the 98%/51% *cost* thresholds appear in the Competition Bureau's guidance, which states it applies to non-food goods (a): https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/how-we-foster-competition/education-and-outreach/publications/product-canada-and-made-canada-claims
> "The Bureau enforces the CPLA which applies only to non food products. The Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) has a mandate to enforce Canada's food legislation."
> "Product of Canada": "all or virtually all (at least 98%) of the total direct costs of producing or manufacturing the good have been incurred in Canada."
> "Made in Canada": "at least 51% of the total direct costs of producing or manufacturing the good have been incurred in Canada" plus a qualifying statement.
> "Pictorial representations (e.g., logos, pictures, or symbols such as the Canadian flag or maple leaf) may by themselves be just as forceful as an explicit 'Made in Canada' written representation."
Do not present 98%/51% as the CFIA food rule on camera; use the CFIA wording for food.

**Is country of origin mandatory on imported dry pasta?** Country of origin ("Product of …") is mandatory only for listed commodities. CFIA "Country of origin on food labels" (a), https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/country-origin — foods requiring "Product of [Country]": "wine and brandy," "dairy products," "honey," "fish and fish products," "fresh fruits and vegetables," "shell egg," "processed egg," "meat products," "maple products," and "processed fruit and vegetable products." Dry pasta is not on that list. For other imported prepackaged foods the label must show importation by one of three methods:
> "the name and principal place of business of the foreign manufacturer"
> "'imported for'/'imported by' followed by the name and principal place of business of the Canadian company"
> "the name and principal place of business of the Canadian company with the country of origin of the product"

Regulatory basis, Safe Food for Canadians Regulations (SOR/2018-108) (a):
- s. 218(1)(b): label must bear "the name and principal place of business of the person by or for whom the food was manufactured, prepared, produced, stored, packaged or labelled, on any part of the label other than any part that is applied or attached to the bottom of the container" — https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-2018-108/section-218.html
- s. 223(1): "If a consumer prepackaged food was wholly manufactured, processed or produced in a foreign state and the name and principal place of business of the person in Canada for whom it was manufactured, processed or produced or the person by whom it was stored, packaged or labelled in Canada is shown on its label, that information must be preceded by the expressions "Imported by" and "importé par" or "Imported for" and "importé pour", as the case may be, unless the geographic origin of the consumer prepackaged food is shown on the label in accordance with subsection (3)." — https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-2018-108/section-223.html
- s. 223(3): "The geographic origin of a food must, subject to the requirements of any other federal or provincial law, be shown (a) in close proximity to the name and principal place of business of the person by or for whom the food was manufactured, processed or produced; and (b) in characters of at least the same height as those in which the information referred to in paragraph (a) is shown."
- CFIA "Name and principal place of business" page (a), https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/name-and-principal-place-business: "The expressions 'Imported by' or 'Imported for', when required on consumer prepackaged foods, must be declared in both official languages [206(2), SFCR]."

**Consumer-facing shorthand (interpretation, labelled):** an imported dry pasta may lawfully carry only "Imported by/for [Canadian company, city]" with no country named; a country name is required only if the importer chooses option 3, or if the product is in a listed commodity class. So the absence of "Product of Italy" on a pack is not evidence of anything.

## 3. "Italian", "Italian-style", foreign names and flag imagery on pasta not made in Italy

CFIA has no pasta-specific or flag-specific rule. The governing texts are the general prohibitions and CFIA's general-impression and common-name guidance (all (a)):

- Safe Food for Canadians Act s. 6(1), https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/s-1.1/section-6.html: "It is prohibited for a person to manufacture, prepare, package, label, sell, import or advertise a food commodity in a manner that is false, misleading or deceptive or is likely to create an erroneous impression regarding its character, quality, value, quantity, composition, merit, safety or origin or the method of its manufacture or preparation."
- CFIA "General principles for labelling and advertising", https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/general-principles: "all information on food labels or in advertisements, including words, pictures, vignettes and logos, will contribute to the overall impression created about a product."
- CFIA "Pictures, vignettes, logos and trademarks on food labels", https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/pictures-vignettes-logos-and-trademarks-food: "Pictures and charts...must not be deceptive, misleading or misrepresent the composition, qualities or value of a product."
- CFIA "Common name on food labels", https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/common-name: common names must not mislead by "improperly suggesting a place of origin (for example, 'New Orleans jambalaya' on jambalaya which was not made in New Orleans)". The same page lists "Italian dressing" among acceptable generic common names and states a company may use "pasta" or "torsade pasta" together with "fusilli".
- CFIA origin-claims page (above): origin-of-ingredient and processing claims ("Contains Italian olive oil", "Packaged in Ireland") allowed "provided the claim is truthful and not misleading".

Interpretation (labelled): whether an Italian flag, tricolour band or "Italian-style" on a Canadian- or US-made pasta is compliant is judged case-by-case under the s. 6(1) "erroneous impression regarding … origin" test and the overall-impression principle; CFIA publishes no per-se ban. No CFIA enforcement decision on pasta imagery was found (see §7).

## 4. Italy — DPR 187/2001 and the 2017 wheat-origin decree

**DPR 9 febbraio 2001, n. 187** — "Regolamento per la revisione della normativa sulla produzione e commercializzazione di sfarinati e paste alimentari, a norma dell'articolo 50 della legge 22 febbraio 1994, n. 146." > Art. 6, c. 1: "Sono denominati "pasta di semola di grano duro" e "pasta di semolato di grano duro" i prodotti ottenuti dalla trafilazione, laminazione e conseguente essiccamento di impasti preparati rispettivamente ed esclusivamente: a) con semola di grano duro ed acqua; b) con semolato di grano duro ed acqua."
> Art. 6, c. 2: "E' denominato "pasta di semola integrale di grano duro" il prodotto ottenuto dalla trafilazione, laminazione e conseguente essiccamento di impasto preparato esclusivamente con semola integrale di grano duro ed acqua."

> Art. 6, c. 4: "Salvo quanto previsto dall'articolo 12, commi 1 e 4, e' vietata la fabbricazione di pasta secca preparata con sfarinati di grano tenero."
> Art. 6, c. 5: "Nei tipi di pasta di cui al comma 3 e agli articoli 7 e 8 e' tollerata la presenza di farine di grano tenero in misura non superiore al 3 per cento."
> Art. 12, c. 4: "E', altresì, consentita la produzione di sfarinati e paste alimentari aventi requisiti diversi da quelli prescritti dalle norme del presente regolamento … purché si tratti di prodotti destinati all'esportazione e non nocivi alla salute umana, previa autorizzazione …"

**Decreto 26 luglio 2017** (MIPAAF + MISE), "Indicazione dell'origine, in etichetta, del grano duro per paste di semola di grano duro", GU Serie Generale n. 191, 17 agosto 2017 (code 17A05704). > Art. 2, c. 1: "Sull'etichetta della pasta devono essere indicate le seguenti diciture: a) «Paese di coltivazione del grano»: nome del Paese nel quale e' stato coltivato il grano duro; b) «Paese di molitura»: nome del Paese nel quale e' stata ottenuta la semola di grano duro."

**EU overlay (a).** Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2018/775 (primary-ingredient origin, applicable from 1 April 2020): applies "where the country of origin or place of provenance of a food is given by any means such as statements, pictorial presentation, symbols or terms, referring to places or geographical areas" — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32018R0775. **Does the Italian decree apply to pasta exported to Canada?** The decree contains no provision extending it to product sold outside Italy; it regulates labelling under Italian law, excludes DPR 187 Art. 12 export-derogated product, and its Art. 6 disapplies it to goods lawfully marketed elsewhere. Labels on pasta sold in Canada are governed by the SFCR/FDR (§2). Whether a given Italian brand keeps the "Paese di coltivazione del grano / Paese di molitura" line on its Canadian pack is a per-product fact — re-verify on shelf on camera. ## 1. WHAT THE MANUFACTURING DIFFERENCES ARE (documented, no health content)

### 1.1 Durum semolina: grade, ash, specks, protein, grind

**(a) Canadian Grain Commission, "Quality of Canada Western Amber Durum in 2025"**, https://www.grainscanada.gc.ca/en/grain-research/grain-harvest-export-quality/wheat/2025/harvest-quality-report-cwad-2025.html (read 2026-09-22):
> "CWAD wheat has superior milling quality, producing high semolina yield with low ash content and speck count. It also has the high yellow pigment content necessary to produce end-products with a bright-yellow colour. The high protein content and strong gluten properties of CWAD wheat ensure superior cooking quality of pasta and good performance in durum bread."
> "The quality of CWAD wheat in 2025 was good, with 44% and 83% of the crop in the top 2 and 3 grades, respectively."
> "Rheological test results, including gluten index and Alveograms, demonstrated strong and balanced gluten properties. In 2025, all grades of CWAD produced pasta with excellent bright yellow color, high firmness and superior cooking quality."
Limitation: describes the Canadian crop as milled and processed in the CGC's Grain Research Laboratory; it is not a statement about any retail brand.

**(a) Sarkar & Fu 2022 (Canadian Grain Commission authors), Foods 11(12):1796, doi:10.3390/foods11121796**, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9223010/ :
> "a higher semolina yield has associated elevated levels of ash content" … "studies have shown higher semolina ash content affecting pasta color negatively."
> "bright speck-free semolina is required to give the aesthetic appearance of premium semolina and pasta products."
> "finer granulation helped with quick and uniform hydration, due to a narrow range of particle size" … "reduced dough-mixing time and high-temperature drying works well with finer semolina granulation tripling the pasta production."
> "high-temperature drying is predominant in today's pasta industry".
Interpretation (labelled): the same review therefore documents the industrial logic behind lower-cost pasta — finer semolina, shorter mixing, high-temperature drying, higher throughput — without saying anything about any brand.

**(a) Bresciani, Pagani & Marti 2022, Foods 11(3):256, doi:10.3390/foods11030256**, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8834582/ :
> "Semolina that is high both in protein and gluten content is recognized as the ideal raw material to produce conventional dry pasta."
> "Semolina samples with low ash and damaged starch content result in a dried product characterized by an amber-yellow color, low brown specks (due to the presence of bran particles), and low heat damage. Low ash content is assured by a medium–low extraction rate (60–65%), whereas low-damaged starch is assured by milling durum wheat to large particle sizes (>400 μm). However, the choice is not so easy, because a medium extraction rate is synonymous with a low milling yield (and thus productivity), whereas a large particle size might result in low hydration kinetics with inadequate moistening of semolina."
> "A regular and even protein structure is essential to guarantee a product with good cooking behavior, resulting in high firmness and absence of stickiness and bulkiness."
> "In the first step of pasta-making, semolina and water are carefully dosed and blended together to form a hydrated mixture with a total moisture content of about 30–32%."

**(a) De Cecco (manufacturer claim), "Our method"**, https://www.dececco.com/us_us/our-method/ :
> "Coarse grain semolina is difficult to process: it takes longer to be completely hydrated by water."
> De Cecco states it obtains about 65 kg of semolina from 100 kg of wheat rather than a possible 80 kg (company claim).
> "At Fara San Martino we knead the semolina using this water alone, at a temperature of less than 15°C."
Recorded as company claims.

**(a) Cubadda et al. 2007, Cereal Chemistry 84(1):48–55, doi:10.1094/CCHEM-84-1-0048** (abstract):
> "The results indicate that optimum cooking time is governed by gluten quality. The positive effect on cooking quality of increasing gluten contents and of the application of HT drying is evident in weak gluten samples, but it is not significant in the strong gluten samples."
Interpretation (labelled): with a strong-gluten semolina such as the CGC describes for CWAD, the literature says high-temperature drying adds less to cooking quality than it does with weak-gluten semolina.

### 1.2 Bronze die versus Teflon(PTFE)-insert die

**(a) Bresciani et al. 2022** (same review):
> "As regards die extrusion, it is known how the coating material of the die inserts affects the appearance of the pasta: Teflon gives the product a smooth and bright yellow appearance, while bronze inserts produce a rough surface. Furthermore, the use of a bronze die has the disadvantages of lowering extrusion pressure and die extrusion speed as well as a more rapid consumption of the part in contact with the dough. Bronze-extruded spaghetti is more porous and therefore more fragile (breaking strength decreases by 20–30%) than Teflon-extruded products."
(The review's next sentence concerns storage-insect susceptibility of rough surfaces; recorded here only as existing, not used.)

**(b) Lucisano, Pagani, Mariotti & Locatelli 2008, "Influence of die material on pasta characteristics", Food Research International 41(6):646–652, doi:10.1016/j.foodres.2008.03.016** (citation confirmed via Crossref; abstract text not retrievable — publisher 403; abstract wording as indexed by search engine):
> "Traditional dies made entirely of bronze make the pasta surface rough, which helps to capture the sauce, whereas the Teflon insert gives the product an even surface and a smoother texture." Spaghetti were made with semolina of two particle-size distributions, extruded through Teflon and bronze dies, and dried at 92 °C and 70 °C.
Limitation: quote is from an index snippet of the abstract, not the page itself; the Bresciani review's die passage cites this paper.

### 1.3 Drying: temperature and time

Definitions used in the literature (a): low temperature (LT) ≈ 40–55 °C over many hours; high temperature (HT) ≈ 60–84 °C; ultra/very high temperature (UHT/VHT) ≈ 85–100+ °C over a few hours. Exact cut-offs vary by paper; the figures below are as each source states them.

**(a) West 2012, University of Guelph MSc thesis** (literature review, p. 9), https://atrium.lib.uoguelph.ca/bitstream/handle/10214/5137/West_Ryan_201212_MSc.pdf :
> "Traditionally, oven temperatures for drying pasta were set to 29-40 °C and took anywhere between 24 and 60 hours, depending on the size and shape of the pasta, to dry to the appropriate moisture level (Dexter et al., 1981)."
> "It was found that using high temperature-short time (HT-ST) drying resulted in pasta with improved quality. HT-ST drying increased pasta firmness, decreased stickiness, and reduced cooking loss when compared against traditional pasta (Wyland & D'Appolonia, 1982, D'Egidio et al., 1990, Novaro et al., 1993, Anese et al., 1999)."
> "HT-ST drying conditions at temperatures of 75 °C were first used commercially in 1974. Since then, temperatures have increased to 90 °C and even higher, allowing short cut pasta to be dried within 2-3 hours (Anese et al., 1999, Acquistucci, 2000, Kill, 2001)."

**(a) Fang & Khan 1996, Cereal Chemistry 73(3):317–322**, https://www.cerealsgrains.org/publications/cc/backissues/1996/Documents/73_317.pdf :
> "Several advantages of HTD or UTD (60-120°C) pasta, such as improved color, better firmness, less cooking loss, reduced stickiness, increased resistance to cracking, shorter drying time, reduced floor space and energy cost, and lower bacteria count, have been reported in the literature."
> "Increasing the product drying temperature reduced cooking loss and improved firmness (Table V)."
> Table V (spaghetti, control semolina; cooking loss %, firmness g·cm, cooked weight g): LTD 40 °C — 7.9, 4.7, 29.9; HTD 73 °C — 6.7, 5.5, 31.0; UTD 90 °C — 6.5, 5.9, 30.4.
> "The reduction of cooking loss may be due to the partially coagulated gluten structure formed during HTD (or UTD), leading to less starch loss (Braibanti 1980)."
> Colour: "color decreased when the products were dried at 90°C" — attributed by the authors to "product browning taking place during UTD".
Limitation: US pilot-scale study using regrind blends; the control-sample figures above are the relevant ones; values are for that laboratory's semolina and cycle.

**(a) Zweifel, Handschin, Escher & Conde-Petit 2003, Cereal Chemistry 80(2):159–167, doi:10.1094/CCHEM.2003.80.2.159** (abstract via Crossref):
> "The drying conditions were selected for the HT phase at 80 or 100°C applied at high, intermediate, or low product moisture content. Spaghetti dried at 55°C served as a reference sample."
> "HT drying at 100°C and low product moisture promoted browning of pasta. At the molecular level, HT drying promoted protein denaturation. At the microscopic level, HT drying contributed to a better preservation of the protein network and reduced swelling of starch and disintegration of granules. At the macroscopic level, HT drying enhanced the firmness of cooked pasta and reduced surface stickiness."

**(a) Bresciani et al. 2022** (section 3.8 Drying):
> "The overall cooking quality of the final product (high degree of firmness, low stickiness and low cooking loss) is the result of several simultaneous phenomena within pasta, whose extent depends on both raw material characteristics and the temperature–moisture conditions applied during drying."
> "When protein coagulation in the continuous network prevails, the starch material is trapped within the network and the cooked pasta will be firm with no stickiness on the surface and consequent bulkiness. On the contrary, when the protein network is not strong and elastic enough, the starch swells and gelatinizes before protein coagulation takes place."
> "drying pasta at temperatures above 60 °C can partially compensate for the weakening of the structure of pasta … due to the reinforcing effect provided by protein coagulation."
> "Many pasta producers stress the importance of drying conditions, specifically the use of slow and/or low-temperature drying cycles. Unfortunately, this terminology is not sufficient to provide clear and univocal indications of pasta quality and/or the intensity of heat damage. The survey carried out on more than 60 pasta samples available on the Italian market highlighted that the furosine level was greater than 300 mg/100 g protein for almost all pasta produced at industrial scale. These values have been found, surprisingly, even in some 'artisan pasta'. Moreover, sensory analyses showed that low heat damage (furosine <250 mg/100 g protein) is not a guarantee of good cooking quality."
> "pasta with high furosine content (i.e., dried using high-temperature drying cycles) is perceived to be more bitter than pasta with low furosine content (i.e., dried using low-temperature drying cycles)".
(Furosine is recorded here strictly as the literature's process marker of drying heat; no health content is recorded.)

**(a) Carpentieri, Larrea-Wachtendorff & Ferrari 2024, Front. Food Sci. Technol. 4:1416654, doi:10.3389/frfst.2024.1416654** — three commercial Italian spaghetti (A, B, C) with maximum drying temperatures of 90 °C, 80 °C and 65 °C respectively; sample A "exhibited lower cooking losses (4.78 g/100 g)", the "highest hardness" and "lowest stickiness" and "lower water absorption", and scored high on beige colour (a Maillard marker); sample B, the intermediate, was "closest to the characteristics of good quality pasta". Scope: 3 products, Italian market, no price; not transferable to Canadian brands.

**(a) West 2012 (Guelph) — sensory finding**: "Drying conditions did not have a significant effect on sensory perception except for firmness where LT-LT drying produced firmer pasta than HT-ST drying." Panel: "Twelve panelists … were trained for 18 hourly sessions"; samples coded, served "Under red light"; pasta was made from Canadian Western Hard White wheat flours (refined and whole grain, not durum semolina) and manufactured at the University of Milan pilot plant; funded by MITACS and Kraft. Limitation: not durum semolina, not retail products; the result runs opposite to most durum literature on firmness, which the thesis itself notes.

Interpretation (labelled): the peer-reviewed literature is consistent that higher drying temperature generally raises instrumental firmness and lowers cooking loss and stickiness in durum pasta, at the cost of browning at the top of the range and of a heat-damage marker (furosine) that "slow-dried" marketing refers to. "Slow/low-temperature" on a box is a claim without a legal definition in Italy (De Cecco's own PDF says "there is no legal definition in Italy of 'high quality'"), and the Milan survey found high furosine in some "artisan" pasta. Neither price nor "bronze"/"slow" wording on a package is, by itself, evidence of cooking performance.

### 1.4 The standard laboratory measures of cooking quality

**AACC International (Cereals & Grains Association) Approved Method 66-50.01, "Pasta and Noodle Cooking Quality — Firmness".** The official summary page was 403 to this session; described from:
- (a) Fang & Khan 1996 methods: "Spaghetti cooking quality, expressed by cooked weight, cooking loss, and cooked firmness, was determined on all samples (Method 16-50, AACC 1983). Cooked weight was the weight of 10 g of dry spaghetti after cooking 12 min or 10 g of dry elbow macaroni after cooking 8 min in 300 ml of boiling distilled water. Results were expressed in grams. Cooking loss was the solids lost to the water during the cooking, reported as a percentage basis of the dry pasta. Firmness was measured using two strands of spaghetti and one elbow macaroni and expressed as grams centimeters."
- (b) Stable Micro Systems (instrument maker), https://www.stablemicrosystems.com/products/attachments/cooked-pasta-quality-firmness-rig/ : method "first approved in 1989"; "specifies the use of a knife blade, machined to a 1mm flat across the cutting edge" that "measures the force required to cut five strands of spaghetti (or approximately equivalent width of other pasta forms) positioned adjacent to one another"; "Firmness is defined in this method as the work in grams-centimetre required to shear one piece of pasta".
- (b) Pastaria (industry magazine), Curti & Carini, 12 Feb 2014, https://pastaria.it/measuring-pasta-quality-parameters/?lang=en : optimum cooking time — "compressing a single strand of spaghetti between two thin glass plates at different cooking times. The optimum cooking time corresponds to the time required for the disappearance of the 'white core'"; cooking loss — "completely drying the cooking water in a convection oven at a temperature of 105°C until a constant weight is reached. The solid residue remaining (g solids/100 g pasta)"; water absorption — "weighing the pasta after cooking, and comparing this with its initial weight"; firmness — "the work … required to cut 5 strands of spaghetti positioned one next to the other."
- (a) West 2012 (Guelph) used the same three measures: cooking-water residue dried and weighed for cooking loss, cooked sample dried for water absorption, and a texture analyser (Kramer shear cell) for firmness.
- (a) Bresciani et al. 2022: cooking quality means "high degree of firmness, low stickiness and low cooking loss".

Working definitions for the script (interpretation, labelled): cooking loss = the share of the dry pasta's solids that ends up in the pot water (lower is better; the more starch in the water, the cloudier it is and the stickier the pasta); water absorption / cooked weight = how much heavier the pasta gets; firmness = the work needed to cut through cooked strands with a standard blade.

## 5. PROPOSED ON-CAMERA PROTOCOL — NOT PERFORMED (proposal only)

Label on screen and in script: "Our own kitchen comparison — not a laboratory test — performed on [date] with [n] tasters." Never call it a test by a lab; never generalise beyond the packs bought.

Design (adapted from AACC 66-50.01 practice, the Test-Achats chef protocol and the Guelph descriptive-panel setup):
1. Products: same shape from every brand (spaghetti of comparable diameter, or penne rigate). Buy from shelf on camera; film label, lot code, best-before, price, size, store. Record ingredient list verbatim. Note stated country of origin and any "bronze"/"slow-dried" wording.
2. Water and salt: 1 L water per 100 g pasta, same tap water source, 10 g salt per litre, rolling boil confirmed by thermometer; same pot type and burner setting for every batch.
3. Cook time: two runs — (i) each brand's own package time; (ii) a single fixed time for all (e.g., 9 min) to compare at equal exposure. Start timer on re-boil. Stir at 30 s and every 2 min.
4. Cooking-loss stand-in (visual, not a measurement): decant 200 mL of cooking water from each pot into identical clear jars, let settle, photograph side-by-side under the same light. State clearly that cloudiness is a rough visual proxy and not AACC cooking loss.
5. Cooked-weight stand-in: weigh 100.0 g dry before, drain 30 s in identical colanders, weigh after; report the gain as "water pick-up in our kitchen".
6. Serving: drain, no rinse, no oil; portion 60 g into identical numbered white bowls within 60 s; one batch plain, one batch with 20 g of the same plain passata per bowl (to observe sauce cling); serve within 3 minutes of draining.
7. Blinding: a second person cooks and codes bowls with random three-digit codes; tasters never see packaging or the cook; tasting order randomised per taster; water and plain crackers between samples; a minimum of 8 tasters (12 if possible) so a "no difference" result is meaningful.
8. Scorecard: firmness (bite), surface stickiness, wheat flavour, off-flavour/bitterness, overall preference; 15-cm line scale or 1–9. Also a forced-choice "which is the $1 pasta?" guess, to report the hit rate against chance.
9. Report: means and how many tasters preferred each; state cook time used; state that instrumental firmness and cooking loss were NOT measured; do not claim any brand is "better made" from the result.
10. Disclosures: pasta bought at retail with channel funds; no brand contact; results apply to the specific lots bought.

## 5. What an honest "$1 vs $5" framing needs to answer for this audience

For a Canadian shopper the honest version of "$1 pasta vs $5 pasta" has to do five things, in order. First, establish the real ladder on a single shelf on a single day — item as listed, size, price, price per 100 g, store, URL, retrieval date, all flagged "re-verify on shelf on camera" — because the audience's loudest complaint about the competitor was invented pricing, and because the cheapest bag in Canada is usually a 900 g bag while the premium box is 500 g, so a per-box comparison misleads by default. Second, read the labels verbatim and stop there: first ingredient, whether enrichment is declared, protein grams per serving as printed, country of origin and wheat origin as printed — the channel has already explained what those lines mean, so this video only needs to show them side by side. Third, say plainly what the $4 gap can and cannot be shown to buy: "bronze die" and "slow dried" are manufacturer claims with no Canadian legal definition, so they are recorded as claims, and any statement about taste or texture must come from an attributed third-party test with its scope and date (a Canadian consumer magazine, a named outlet's blind panel), never from "we tested". Fourth, refuse the two easy inferences the niche runs on — that cheap means inferior and that expensive means Italian craft — because price is not evidence of quality, fraud or legality, and the channel's own August video already showed a bronze-die import sitting below the Statistics Canada average price. Fifth, leave the viewer with a decision rule they can use in the aisle in thirty seconds (price per 100 g, the label lines above, and which specific attributed test found a difference, if any), and explicitly not repeat the ownership, trade-war, enrichment and pesticide material the channel has already published.
