## 2. THE COSTCO CHICKEN AS CULTURAL OBJECT

### The US numbers (context, dated, attributed)

- **Volume**: Reported figures, attributed to Costco statements/earnings materials via food-trade press: **106 million** rotisserie chickens in fiscal 2021 (also cited in CBC/Radio-Canada International's rotisserie explainer: "Costco sold 106 million rotisserie chickens globally in fiscal 2021 and operates its own processing plants" — reported wording of RCI, Dec 30, 2022); **117 million** fiscal 2022; **137 million** — "the most recent stat... comes from a 2023 shareholder's meeting during which reps for the company said they sold an astonishing 137 million chickens that year" (The Takeout/Tasting Table reporting); **157.4 million globally in 2025** (The Takeout, "Costco Sold A Massive Number Of Rotisserie Chickens In 2025"). Use "over 100 million a year, company-stated at shareholder meetings" as the safe on-air line.
- **The $4.99 legend**: NPR (June 7, 2022, "Inflation is raising prices on almost everything, except rotisserie chicken"): the US price has held at **$4.99 since 2000**. Costco's then-CFO Richard Galanti, 2015, via Fortune: willing to give up "$30 million, $40 million a year on gross margin" to hold the price; and in 2023 (via Chowhound's reporting of the earnings call): the chicken is "an investment in low prices to drive membership, to drive sales in a big way." Attribute both to Galanti as reported.
- **Loss-leader framing**: Snopes has a fact-check ("Does Costco Lose Millions Annually for Keeping Rotisserie Chicken Cheap?") — useful if you want a neutral arbiter citation.

### The price-in-Canada story (the episode's own turf)

- **2022**: CBC Radio's Cost of Living (published via Radio-Canada International, Dec 30, 2022, "Why prepared rotisserie chickens are cheaper than ones you cook yourself"): rotisserie chickens at a **Calgary Costco cost $7.99**, while raw whole chicken at the same store was **$6.49/kg** — i.e., the cooked bird cost less per kilogram than raw. This is the source that validates your episode title. Same piece, per Rowe Farms: rotisserie birds weigh about **1.2 kg**, raw whole chickens typically **1.6–1.8 kg**. Also from that piece, verbatim: "According to poultry producers, chicken has been the most consumed meat in Canada...for years."
- **2022 (independent Canadian blog)**: Costcuisine (Canadian Costco review site, Oct 31, 2022): price "**$7.99 Canadian**"; verbatim: "People line up to get one warm off the rotisserie!"
- **2026**: Narcity Toronto taste test (Victoria Kuglin, May 20, 2026, "I compared rotisserie chickens from Costco, Loblaws and Metro — one is miles ahead"): Costco "**Price: $9**", weight 1.2 kg, rated 10/10 and called "**the platonic ideal of a rotisserie chicken**"; **Loblaws $13** (~900 g); **Metro $13.99** (~1,000 g, described as "Mammoth"). Narcity also noted the same Loblaws-banner chicken "cost significantly less at No Frills" — a fantastic same-parent-company gotcha for your show.
- INFERENCE (label): the Canadian Costco price appears to have moved from $7.99 (2022) to about $8.99–$9 (2026) — we have two dated endpoints, not a reported announcement of an increase. Verify the current shelf price on camera; that IS the story ("even the price change was quiet — we checked").
- Costco Canada's own same-day listing confirms the product identity: "Seasoned Rotisserie Chicken (Avg. 1.2kg)" (sameday.costco.ca — note delivery prices are marked up; do not quote a delivery price as the warehouse price).
- **Supply-management wrinkle** (attribute carefully): fresh chicken retailing in Canada is regulated under the quota/marketing-board system, so Costco Canada's birds are sourced through Canadian suppliers — while in the US, Lincoln Premium Poultry (Costco's own Nebraska operation) supplies about 40% of its rotisserie chickens (per Fortune/poultry-trade coverage). Frame as: "the famous Costco-owns-the-chicken-plant story is an American story; in Canada the birds come through the Canadian quota system." Label the Canadian-sourcing sentence as based on regulatory framework reporting, not a Costco Canada statement, unless you obtain one.

## 3. ROTISSERIE HISTORY FOR CANADA — RESTAURANTS FIRST, SUPERMARKETS SECOND

The Canadian angle no US video has: **Canada had a rotisserie-chicken culture 40 years before supermarkets did.**

### St-Hubert, Montreal, 1951 — first

From St-Hubert's own corporate history page (st-hubert.com/en/about/history.html, fetched, verbatim):
- "On September 25, Hélène and René Léger make their dream a reality and open the first St-Hubert rotisserie at 6355 Saint-Hubert Street, in a rented space with second-hand equipment, big enough for 78 seats."
- "Free home delivery service is offered—a first in Canada! Montrealers are introduced to the fleet of yellow Beetles." (Corporate claim — attribute to St-Hubert.)
- Company-history milestones: sauces in grocery stores 1965; **Expo 67**: "18,000 meals in one day" (corporate claim); call centre with 36 operators by 1972; first Express counter 1995; joined Recipe Unlimited (formerly Cara) 2016.

Radio-Canada's independent history ("La petite histoire des rôtisseries St-Hubert," March 31, 2016, fetched, verbatim in French): "le couple décide d'ouvrir sa première rôtisserie St-Hubert BarBQ après avoir vu les nombreuses files d'attente le dimanche soir devant les rôtisseries du Plateau-Mont-Royal et d'Outremont" — the Légers opened because of the **Sunday-evening lineups already forming outside Montreal rotisseries** in 1951. This is your sourced proof that rotisserie chicken was a Quebec Sunday institution before St-Hubert even existed. Also verbatim: "Leur flotte de coccinelles jaunes, qui sillonnent les rues, deviendra rapidement un symbole phare de l'entreprise" (the yellow Beetle fleet as brand symbol).

### Swiss Chalet, Toronto, 1954 — the empire

From Swiss Chalet's own story page (swisschalet.com/en/about/our-story.html, fetched, verbatim):
- "The first Swiss Chalet® restaurant opened at 234 Bloor Street West in Toronto in 1954."
- Inspiration, verbatim: "the Swiss method of cooking chickens, roasting chickens on a spit over an open flame." (Corporate claim — attribute to Swiss Chalet. This is the exact phrase requested, confirmed live on their site — connects to your restaurant-chains episode.)
- Corporate milestones: delivery in the 1970s; Rochester/Buffalo 1973 and Egypt 1978; Festive Special debuted 1987; grocery-store products in the 2000s–2010s.
- Scale today: roughly 200 locations (CBC's 2016 merger coverage and trade press cite "almost 200"/"212"; use "about 200, mostly Ontario," attributed to CBC).

### The two solitudes merge, 2016

CBC News, March 31, 2016: "Chicken chains merge as Swiss Chalet buying St-Hubert for $537M" — Cara Operations (Swiss Chalet's parent, now Recipe Unlimited) bought St-Hubert; St-Hubert had ~120 restaurants, almost all in Quebec (CBC). Companion piece: "St-Hubert's rise in Quebec over 65 years" (CBC) and "St-Hubert sale: Quebec reacts" — Quebec's emotional reaction to its chicken chain being sold to Ontario is documented, quotable texture. For a deeper cultural essay, Maisonneuve magazine ran "Comfort Feud" (March 31, 2020) on the Swiss Chalet–St-Hubert rivalry (paywalled/blocked in our fetch — writer should pull quotes manually).

### When supermarkets took the spit — the 1990s HMR boom (dated industry sources)

- Boston Chicken (later Boston Market), founded 1985, made takeout rotisserie chicken a US phenomenon; **Costco and Kroger both launched rotisserie chicken in 1994** in response (Eat This Not That, Food Republic, Mashed food-history reporting; Restaurant Business, "How grocers' rotisserie chickens hurt Boston Market").
- The trade term: "Coined by savvy retailers and grocers in the mid-'90s, the term home-meal replacement (or HMR) refers to pre-cooked, ready-to-eat meals purchased from a grocery store" (Foodservice & Hospitality magazine — a Canadian trade title, good for your purposes; reported wording).
- Dated stat: "In 1996, according to an article published that same year in Supermarket News, the average supermarket deli accounted for 4% to 6% of store sales. Then came HMR" (reported wording, The National Provisioner/Supermarket News archive; Supermarket News also ran "HMR IN MEAT DEPARTMENTS CALLED ROOST FOR CHICKEN" in the era — headline citable as period flavour).
- Scale of what it became: "Grocers and Costco sold about 625 million rotisserie chickens in 2017, according to the Wall Street Journal" (as cited by Restaurant Business). US/North America figure — attribute to WSJ via Restaurant Business.

### Loblaws / President's Choice and prepared food (sourced)

- Dave Nichol launched the **Insider's Report** in **November 1983** and the first **President's Choice** products hit Loblaw shelves in **1984**; the PC Decadent Chocolate Chip Cookie became the emblem (Globe and Mail obituaries, Sept 2013: "The veteran Loblaw pitchman behind President's Choice dies at age 73"; Canadian Grocer; Wikipedia corroboration). The Globe: Nichol "was a foodie before the term became popular, using a folksy style in television commercials that helped propel President's Choice into one of the country's top brands" (reported wording).
- Angle for the script: the 50+ viewer remembers Dave Nichol personally selling them fancy supermarket food in the 80s — the deli hot counter and today's $13 Loblaws rotisserie bird are the descendants of that pitch. (Framing, not a factual claim.)
- NOT FOUND: a dated source for when Loblaws specifically began in-store rotisserie programs. See UNVERIFIED list.
