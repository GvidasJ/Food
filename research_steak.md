## A. GRADING

### A1. Who grades, and under what law
(a) The Livestock and Poultry Carcass Grading Regulations (SOR/92-541) were REPEALED when the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations (SFCR, SOR/2018-108) came into force in January 2019. Beef grading now sits in SFCR Part 12 ("Grades and Grade Names", s. 304 ff.), with the grade requirements themselves incorporated by reference in a CBGA-authored document.
- SFCR consolidated text: https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-2018-108/FullText.html ("Regulations are current to 2026-07-21 and last amended on 2025-09-19").
- Archived repealed regulation: https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-92-541/page-1.html
- (b) BLG, "Safe Food for Canadians Regulations Change Compliance Landscape" (Feb 2019) confirms the repeal list: https://www.blg.com/en/insights/2019/02/safe-food-for-canadians-regulations-change-compliance-landscape-for-food-businesses

(a) The incorporated-by-reference grade document is "Beef, Bison and Veal Carcass Grade Requirements", "Prepared by the Canadian Beef Grading Agency", "This document was published on April 29, 2026. Date of publication of previous versions: January 9, 2020, June 13, 2018." PDF: https://beefgradingagency.ca/wp-content/uploads/CBGA-Carcass-Grade-Requirements-document-April-2026.pdf (22 pp., text extracted 23 Sept 2026).
- Its s. 1.0 defines: "'grader' means a person who is designated as a grader for the purposes of the Regulations under subsection 13(3) of the Canadian Food Inspection Agency Act." and "'Regulations' means the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations."
- CFIA's meat-labelling guidance says of this document: "The Beef, Bison and Veal Carcass Grade Requirements – PDF (260 kb), prepared and published by the Canadian Beef Grading Agency, may be referred to as the 'Grades Document' for the purpose of this guidance." — https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/meat-and-poultry-products (read in full 23 Sept 2026)
- Same CFIA page: "The particular conditions for grading that must be met for a grader to grade a livestock carcass are outlined in section 334 to 337 of the SFCR". And: "Licence holders may apply a grade name to and use a grade name in connection with a livestock carcass ... if the carcass has been graded by a grader, in addition to meeting other prescribed conditions [308(1)(b), (c), (e) and (f), SFCR]."

(a) Canadian Beef Grading Agency (CBGA), homepage https://beefgradingagency.ca/ : "The Canadian Beef Grading Agency (CBGA) is an industry-run organization that ensures impartial assessment of value for Canadian beef, bison and veal carcasses"; "The CBGA is a private not-for-profit corporation accredited by the Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA)" under the "Safe Food for Canadians Regulations"; "There are 13 grades and 5 yield classes of beef in Canada".
(a) CBGA beef-grading page https://beefgradingagency.ca/livestock-grading-in-canada/beef-grading/ : "Grading is not mandatory, but the grade is the commonly accepted criteria used to establish market value." Grading is done by "A certified and designated grader".

### A2. Is beef grading mandatory or voluntary? (the exact rule)
(a) SFCR s. 306(1) (read verbatim at https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-2018-108/section-306.html ): "Mandatory grading 306 (1) Any eggs, fish, fresh fruits or vegetables, processed fruit or vegetable products, honey, maple syrup or beef carcass in respect of which grades are prescribed by these Regulations that are sent or conveyed from one province to another or that are imported or exported must (a) be graded; (b) meet the requirements that are set out in the Compendium, the Fresh Fruit and Vegetable Grades Document or the Grades Document in respect of the applicable grade of that food; and (c) be labelled ... with the applicable grade name".
(a) BUT s. 306(2)(f) exempts: "a beef carcass, or a carcass side, hind quarter, front quarter, primal cut or sub-primal cut of a beef carcass, that is sent or conveyed from one province to another or exported, in the following cases: (i) if it is prepackaged, its container is labelled with the expression 'Ungraded Beef' or 'boeuf non classifié', and (ii) if it is not prepackaged, it is accompanied by documents for presentation to an inspector or grader that show that it has not been graded." Identical exemption for imported beef at s. 306(2)(e). (Amended SOR/2025-192, s. 16.)
(a) CFIA's plain-language restatement (meat-and-poultry-products page): "Beef carcasses that are interprovincially traded, imported or exported must be graded, must meet the grade requirements and be labelled with the applicable grade name ... Despite this grading requirement, beef carcasses may be ungraded when labelled with the words 'Ungraded Beef' /'boeuf non classifié' or 'boeuf non classé' [204, 306(1), 306(2)(e) and (f), SFCR]."
(a) Same page: "Grading is optional for bison, ovine or veal carcasses, as well as dressed or partially dressed poultry carcasses."
(b) BCRC (Beef Cattle Research Council, industry body), "Carcass Grading", last updated November 2024: "While the grading system is voluntary, virtually all fed beef carcasses processed commercially in Canada are graded." https://www.beefresearch.ca/topics/carcass-grading/
(a) CBGA weekly slaughter/grading report, week ending 12/09/2026 (image table published at https://beefgradingagency.ca/slaughter-grading-statistics/ , file 20260912grd.png): Federal inspected packing plants, Canada totals: "Graded Total 49516", "Ungraded 2206", "Total Slaughter 51722" (i.e., 4.3% of federally inspected cattle that week were not graded — arithmetic by compiler).

PLAIN ANSWER FOR SCRIPT (a): Grading is not compulsory. A packer can legally ship "Ungraded Beef" interprovincially or import it; a retailer may sell it. What IS prohibited is applying a Canada grade name unless the carcass was graded by a designated grader (SFCR s. 308). At retail, individual cuts are never themselves graded — see A4.

### A3. The grades and what each requires (verbatim from the Grades Document, April 29, 2026)
(a) s. 3.0(1): "There are 13 grades of beef carcasses with the grade names Canada A, Canada AA, Canada AAA, Canada Prime, Canada B1, Canada B2, Canada B3, Canada B4, Canada D1, Canada D2, Canada D3, Canada D4 and Canada E."
(a) s. 3.0(4): "A beef carcass graded Canada A, Canada AA, Canada AAA or Canada Prime must have (a) the maturity characteristics set out in Schedule I; (b) musculature that ranges from good, with some deficiencies, to excellent; (c) Longissimus muscles that, 10 minutes after being exposed by knife-ribbing, are firm and bright red in colour; (d) for the grade set out in column 1 of an item of the table to this subsection, the degree of marbling level set out in column 2 of that item; and (e) a fat covering that is (i) firm and white or slightly tinged with a reddish or amber colour, and (ii) not less than 2 mm in thickness at the measurement site."
(a) "Table – Degree of Marbling for Canada A, Canada AA, Canada AAA and Canada Prime": "1. Canada A — Traces; 2. Canada AA — Slight; 3. Canada AAA — Small; 3a. Canada AAA — Modest; 3b. Canada AAA — Moderate; 4. Canada Prime — Slightly Abundant; 4a. Canada Prime — Moderately Abundant; 4b. Canada Prime — Abundant; 4c. Canada Prime — Very Abundant".
(a) Schedule I "Maturity characteristics for youthful beef carcasses": "1. Cartilaginous caps on the thoracic vertebrae that are no more than half-ossified. 2. Lumbar vertebrae that have evidence of cartilage or at least a red line present on the tips of the spinous processes. 3. Spinous processes that are generally porous and red when split. 4. Ribs that are narrow, round and red. 5. A sternum that shows distinct divisions."
(a) s. 3.0(2) fat measurement: "a grader must determine the fat level of a beef carcass by measuring the external fat on the left side between the twelfth and thirteenth ribs over the Longissimus muscles ... at a point three-quarters of the length of the Longissimus muscles from its chine bone end."
(a) Yield classes (s. 3.0(5)-(6)): formula "2.9 + (0.0984 x adjusted fat thickness, mm) + (0.0086 x weight, kg) – (0.0496 x rib eye area, square cm)"; "Canada 1: 1.0 to 1.9; Canada 2: 2.0 to 2.9; Canada 3: 3.0 to 3.9; Canada 4: 4.0 to 4.9; Canada 5: 5.0 to 5.9". s. 1.0: "'yield' means, in respect of a beef carcass, the estimated percentage of closely trimmed, boneless retail meat product in the carcass derived from the primal cuts."
(a) Lower youthful grades:
- Canada B1 (s. 3.0(7)): Schedule I maturity; musculature "good, with some deficiencies, to excellent"; muscles "firm and bright red"; fat "firm and white or slightly tinged with a reddish or amber colour"; "(e) a degree of marbling that is devoid or practically devoid."
- Canada B2 (s. 3.0(8)): "musculature that ranges from deficient to excellent"; muscles "bright red in colour"; "(d) a fat covering that is yellow."
- Canada B3 (s. 3.0(9)): "musculature that ranges from deficient to good"; fat "white or slightly tinged with a reddish or amber colour."
- Canada B4 (s. 3.0(10)): "Longissimus muscles that, 10 minutes after being exposed by knife-ribbing, are dark red in colour; and (d) a fat covering that has a colour ranging from white to yellow." (CBGA web page: "Dark red (the determining factor in a B4 Grade) is established by comparison with a standardized CBGA colour chit.")
(a) Mature grades (Schedule II maturity — "Cartilaginous caps on the thoracic vertebrae that are more than half-ossified ... Ribs that are wide, flat and white ... A sternum that shows advanced ossification"):
- Canada D1 (s. 3.0(11)): "musculature that is excellent"; fat "extends well over the ribs and loins and moderately well over the hips and chucks ... firm and white or slightly tinged ... less than 15 mm in thickness at the measurement site."
- Canada D2 (s. 3.0(12)): musculature "medium, with some deficiencies, to excellent"; fat "ranges from firm to slightly soft ... colour ranging from white to yellow ... less than 15 mm".
- Canada D3 (s. 3.0(13)): "musculature that is deficient to a degree of emaciation"; fat "less than 15 mm".
- Canada D4 (s. 3.0(14)): "musculature that ranges from deficient to excellent; and (c) a fat covering that is 15 mm or more in thickness at the measurement site."
- Canada E (s. 3.0(15)): "the carcass of a male bovine animal that has pronounced masculinity."
(a) Roller-brand ink colours (Schedule III): A/AA/AAA/Prime = Red; B grades = Blue (B2/B3/B4 rolled as "Canada B"); D grades = Brown (D2/D3/D4 rolled as "Canada D"); E = Brown.
(a) CFIA description of the stamp: "The beef grade stamp has the outline of a Canadian maple leaf. Centered towards the top of the emblem is the word 'Canada'. Centered in the middle of the emblem is the letter grade assigned to the carcass ... the beef grade stamp must appear in red ink on the graded beef carcass." (meat-and-poultry-products page)
(a) CBGA web page: "Canadian marbling standards are grouped into categories: Canada A, Canada AA, Canada AAA and Canada Prime. These standards are based on USDA marbling standards (identified in bold): Trace, Slight, Small and Slightly Abundant." and "To qualify as Canada A, Canada AA, Canada AAA or Canada Prime, fat cannot have a yellow tinge and must also be assessed as firm and consistent."

### A4. Grade labelling AT RETAIL — mandatory or voluntary; what wording is allowed
(a) CFIA, "Labelling requirements for meat and poultry products", section "Retail beef cuts" (read verbatim 23 Sept 2026): "Since beef grades do not apply to individual beef cuts prepackaged by the retailer for retail sale, labels or advertisements for retail cuts may only include an indication of the grade of the carcass (or complete side, hind quarter, front quarter, primal cut or sub-primal cut) from which the retail cut was derived (for example, the label or advertisement should include words such as 'Cut from' or other appropriate words that do not give the impression that the retail cut was graded when indicating the carcass grade). Additionally, grade names must be reproduced in full. An appropriate reference would be 'Cut from Canada AA Beef'."
(a) Same section: "When retail beef cuts are derived from carcasses of: one specific grade only, the label or advertisement declaration must make reference to that specific grade only (for example, 'Cut from Canada AA' or 'Cut from USDA Choice'); more than one grade, the label or advertisement declaration must make reference to the lowest grade involved plus the words 'or higher' (for example, 'Cut from Canada A or higher', or 'Cut from USDA Select or higher')". "Former grade information declarations on retail packages or in advertising such as 'Cut from Canada A/AA', 'Cut from Canada A/AA/AAA' and 'Cut from Canada A Grades' are no longer acceptable." "Grade names that do not include the name of the country that established the grade must be accompanied by a declaration of the country of origin. For example, while 'A' is an Australian grade, the appropriate retail declaration would be 'Cut from Australian A Grade'."
(a) Mixed-grade container rules (same page): "Canadian beef graded Canada A, Canada AA and Canada AAA may be mixed provided the container is marked with a single grade name corresponding to the lowest grade quality followed by the expression 'or higher' /'ou plus' ... Example: 'Canada A or higher'"; "Canadian beef graded in the A grades shall not be mixed with non A grade product or ungraded product and be identified by grade name"; "containers of ungraded product must be marked with the words 'Ungraded Beef' ... Should anyone wish to mix graded and ungraded product, the containers must be marked as ungraded". "Note: The licence holder of an establishment is responsible for the accuracy of grade labelling."
(a) SFCR s. 326 (per CFIA): "The grade name that is applied to a prepackaged primal cut or sub-primal cut of a beef carcass must correspond to the grade of the beef carcass from which it is cut [326, SFCR]."
(a) Advertising trigger (WebSearch summary of CFIA "Food composition and quality claims"/Grades pages — page itself moved; cite with caution): "Grade names must be declared in advertisements when a price is declared and more than 1 grade of the food is available at retail" — RECHECK URL before use: https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/composition-and-quality
(a) Nothing in the SFCR or CFIA retail guidance REQUIRES a retailer to state any grade on a retail steak package. Grade at retail is a voluntary, "Cut from ..." claim that must be truthful. Retailers may therefore sell ungraded beef, or graded beef without stating the grade. (Compiler's reading of the above primary texts.)

### A5. Imported beef grades (USDA etc.)
(a) CFIA, "Grade requirements for imported meat products" (date modified Feb 18, 2019): Canada accepts foreign grades only from Argentina, Australia, New Zealand, the United States and Uruguay; USDA grades permitted include Prime, Choice, Select, Commercial, Standard, Utility, Canner, Cutter; "the grade description or word 'ungraded' is required on the OMIC for all single ingredient portions of beef"; containers must be marked with either the grade name or "ungraded beef"; statements such as "or higher" with USDA grades are acceptable. https://inspection.canada.ca/en/importing-food-plants-animals/food-imports/food-specific-requirements/grade-requirements-imported-meat-products
(a) CFIA meat-labelling page: "There are no import grade names for beef carcasses ... When these are imported from a country that has a grade designation having requirements that are substantially equivalent to requirements for a Canadian grade name ... they must be labelled with that foreign grade designation rather than with the Canadian grade name [2(1), Compendium, Volume 9]." SFCR s. 306(4)(d) as read: an imported beef carcass/primal not prepackaged may be "graded and labelled in accordance with the requirements, in respect of grades of beef carcasses, that are established by the foreign state of origin".
IMPLICATION FOR SCRIPT (a): US beef sold in Canada is labelled "Cut from USDA Choice" etc., not "Canada AAA". A "Canada AAA" claim on a "Product of USA" package would be inconsistent with the above (see Costco record at C5 — reported as a possible labelling error, no finding).

### A6. Canada Prime vs USDA Prime — official-source equivalence statements
(a) Canada Beef (industry marketing agency), "Canadian Beef Advantage Grading Standards": "The minimum marbling standards used for USDA Prime (slightly abundant), Choice (small) and Select (slight) are the same minimum standards used in Canada to segregate the youthful quality carcasses into Canada Prime, AAA and AA respectively." and "Quality and yield grades are assigned to carcasses by a Canadian Beef Grading Agency (CBGA) certified grader." https://cdnbeefperforms.ca/canadian-beef-advantage-grading-standards/
(b) BCRC "Carcass Grading" (Nov 2024): "The U.S. equivalent grade for Canada Prime is USDA Prime. Canada AAA, AA and A are equivalent to USDA Choice, Select and Standard respectively." https://www.beefresearch.ca/topics/carcass-grading/
(a) CBGA: marbling standards "are based on USDA marbling standards" (A3 above).
Differences that matter (b, BCRC and CBGA pages, paraphrased from WebSearch reading — verify before quoting): Canada removes carcasses with mature (Schedule II) ossification from the A/AAA/Prime grades entirely (they become D grades), and requires firm, bright-red muscle and non-yellow fat for all four top grades. ## B. LABELLING RULES

### B1. Country of origin — mandatory on imported meat
(a) CFIA consumer page "Country of origin on food labels" (date modified 2026-09-10): imported prepackaged foods that must show country of origin: "brandy, dairy products, honey, fish and seafood products, fresh fruits and vegetables, shell eggs and processed eggs, meat products, maple products, processed fruit and vegetable products, wine". Wording: "Product of [Name the country of origin]". https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/consumers/country-origin
(a) CFIA meat-and-poultry-products page, "Country of origin": "The words 'Product of' /'produit de', followed by the name of the country of origin, must be declared on the label of an imported meat product in close proximity to the product's common name and in characters that are at least 1.6 mm in height, or 0.8 mm in height if the area of the principal display surface is 10 cm2 or less [210(2) and (3), 297(1) and (2), SFCR]. This applies whether or not the imported meat product is subsequently packaged or labelled in Canada without being manufactured or prepared in Canada [297(3), SFCR]." "Country of origin declaration must be shown in: both official languages for consumer prepackaged products". "Note: In the SFCR, country of origin is referred to as foreign state of origin."
(a) There is NO mandatory origin statement for DOMESTIC beef; "Product of Canada"/"Canadian beef" are voluntary claims governed by B2.

### B2. "Product of Canada" / "Made in Canada" / "Canadian beef" — the meat rule verbatim
(a) CFIA, "Origin claims on food labels" (date modified 2023-12-06) https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/origin-claims :
- Product of Canada: "all or virtually all major ingredients, processing, and labour used to make the food product are Canadian." Non-Canadian content allowed: "very low levels of ingredients that are not generally produced in Canada" (under 2% total).
- Made in Canada: "the last substantial transformation of the product occurred in Canada, even if some ingredients are from other countries", with qualifier "Made in Canada from domestic and imported ingredients" or "Made in Canada from imported ingredients".
- MEAT: Product of Canada claims can be applied to meat from Canadian animals that are slaughtered in Canada. Animals are considered Canadian if they are "born or hatched, raised and slaughtered in Canada or, in the case of feeder cattle, if they have spent a period of at least 60 days in Canada prior to slaughter in Canada. The 60-day residency period is based on international animal health standards."
- Substantiation: "Proof of origin and segregation, such as paper documentation, of the animals used to create the product must be provided to a CFIA inspector upon request to substantiate the claim."
- Ingredient-level "Canadian" claim: when claiming "Canadian" for a specific ingredient, "all or virtually all major ingredients, processing, and labour used to make" that component "must be Canadian."
SCRIPT NOTE (a): A steer born in the US, fed in Alberta for 60+ days and slaughtered in Alberta can lawfully be "Product of Canada". Verbatim rule above. FAQ page: https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/origin-claims/frequently-asked-questions

### B3. Mechanically tenderized beef — LABEL WORDING ONLY (reason and cooking text EXCLUDED)
(a) Food and Drug Regulations, C.R.C., c. 870, s. - **Sobeys / Safeway / IGA-West (Empire)** print 'cut … from Canada AAA grade beef' on grilling steaks and 'Canada AA grade beef' on the simmering steak — the only banners that print the *lower* grade on the cheap line rather than leaving it blank. **Thrifty Foods** (also Empire, same Sterling Silver brand) prints no grade. **IGA Quebec** (Sobeys Québec) prints **no grade and no origin on any of its six steak lines**, including the Sterling Silver striploin that the Ontario/Alberta Sobeys flyer labels 'Canada AAA'.
- **Metro Inc**: Metro Toronto's Platinum Grill line says 'CUT FROM CANADA AAA GRADE'; Metro Montreal states a grade only where it is part of the private-label name ('AAA Platinum Plus') and prints **no grade or origin on 7 of 8 Montreal steak lines**; the tenderloin banner says 'Cat. «AA» ou supérieure' but has no price. **Super C** prints neither grade nor origin on either steak line — including the $8.88/lb 'bone-in striploin'.
- **Save-On-Foods, T&T, Urban Fare**: no grade, no origin on any steak line. **Farm Boy, Longo's, Nesters**: grade and origin in the item name itself ('Cut from Canada AAA'). **Co-ops**: 'Canada AA Grade or Higher' / 'Cut from Canada AA Beef', plus 'Aged 14 Days'.
- **Walmart online**: grade always in the copy (AA Canadian or 'AAA Angus'); origin missing on every 'Your Fresh Market' AAA Angus line. **Costco.ca**: 'Canadian AAA' / 'Canada Prime' / 'A5 Product of Japan' on every fresh-steak SKU except Baton Rouge ('AAA quality meat', no country).

## 5. WHAT AN HONEST "DON'T BUY STEAK AT THESE 5 STORES" MUST PROVE PER STORE

Under the channel's rule (verdicts rest only on documented price, label, disclosure or regulatory record — never taste, opinion or health), each store beat needs at least one of the following, photographed or cited with a date and a place, and ideally two so the beat survives the "isolated incident" pushback: (a) a **price record** — the store's own flyer, shelf tag or receipt showing $/kg for a named cut and grade, set against StatCan's average or a competitor's tag the same week, so the claim is "you paid X more for the same grade," not "it's expensive"; (b) a **label record** — the store's own package showing what it discloses and omits: grade (Canada AAA / AA / ungraded / USDA Choice), origin ("Product of Canada" vs "Made in Canada from domestic and imported ingredients" vs Australian/US), "mechanically tenderized" with cook-to-63 °C instructions, "previously frozen," "seasoned"/"water added," pack date vs best-before, and the net weight versus a home-scale reading; (c) a **disclosure record** — what the store says about the beef in its own words (PC Free From "Australian Angus" listing, Walmart's 2021 100%-Canadian press release, Sterling Silver as a Cargill brand, Costco's US-style lean percentages) and where those words stop short; or (d) a **regulatory record** — a CFIA recall, a CFIA "Product of Canada" penalty naming the banner, a CBC scale test naming the store, a CFIA warning on underweight strip loin, a filed class action — each with a date. The verdict must then be phrased as what the document shows ("this steak was labelled AAA at $X/kg; the same grade was $Y across the street"; "this package carries the mechanically-tenderized label; this chain's does not say"), never as what the steak tastes like, whether it is safe, or what the store "probably" does. Where a store's steak record is thin (the Feb video's Costco problem), either replace the store with one that has a record or say plainly that the record is thin and the finding is limited to what is on the label — the fish video's honesty paragraph cost it nothing. Finally, the rank order should follow the strength and recency of the documents, not the severity of the story, and the video should close on a positive, document-based list (which labels to look for, which chains print a pack date or a grade) so it delivers the payoff the Aug beef video never did.
