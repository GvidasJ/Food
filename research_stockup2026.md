## 5. THE THREE-BUCKET RE-SORT

In Canada the sorting criterion is not scarcity — it is the distance between the field and the shelf. That is the thesis and it is entirely defensible with the sources above.

### BUCKET 1 — THE RELIABLE BACKBONE
*"Canada makes so much of this that the world buys the surplus. Buy it because it's cheap, not because it's scarce."*

**Categories:** lentils and dry peas · durum-based pasta · oats · canola oil · mustard · salt · all-purpose flour · maple syrup.

**Why:** Saskatchewan alone exported roughly 34% of the world's lentils, about $2B to 80 markets in 2024; Canada is the #1 global exporter of durum and oats; StatCan confirms Canada was the world's top mustard seed exporter in 2021; the world's largest underground salt mine is at Goderich; Quebec produces roughly 70% of the world's maple syrup and runs the only strategic reserve of it on earth. These are structurally impossible for Canada to be short of.

**Script value:** this bucket is what makes the video not a doom video, and no UK or Australian creator can copy it. It also fixes the reference's biggest omission — lentils — which its own top-liked correction asked for at 113 likes.

**Voiceover framing:** *"This is the part where being Canadian is an advantage."*

### BUCKET 2 — THE CALM WINDOW
*"The price is genuinely soft right now, and the softness has a documented expiry date."*

**Categories:** dairy and eggs · fresh fruit · fresh and frozen vegetables · pork · bakery · seafood.

**Why (a):** August 2026 grocery inflation was 2.8%, down from 3.1% in July and 4.8% in January, and below all-items CPI for the first time since July 2024. StatCan named the decelerators verbatim: **dairy products rose 0.7% y/y in August against 3.1% in July** ("Cheese and yogurt were the top contributors"), plus "fresh or frozen pork (+1.6%), condiments, spices and vinegars (+0.7%) and fresh fruit (+4.7%)." In 2025, fruit (−1.1%) and vegetables (−0.9%) actually fell against 1–3% and 3–5% forecasts.

**Why the window closes (a):** the Food Price Report's own documented annual mechanic — "Food price bumps are observed in October and February every year, before and after supplier-retailer negotiations enter their blackout period" (p.17) — plus the Grocery Code of Conduct going fully operational in January 2026, plus Canadian counter-tariffs on 700+ US products including **seafood, cheese and dough** live since **8 September 2026**.

**Script value:** this is the actual argument of the video and the reason it isn't fearmongering. The window is real, StatCan measures it, and the mechanism that closes it is calendared. This bucket also directly answers @BlooSa's critique: yes, supply is fine, this was always about price.

**Voiceover framing:** *"Nothing here is running out. It's just quietly cheap, and it won't be in February."*

### BUCKET 3 — THE HIDDEN VULNERABILITY
*"Canada doesn't run out of food. Canada runs out of ways to move it."*

**Categories:** beef · imported pantry staples that cross the US border (canned tomatoes, olive oil, coffee, chocolate, citrus, rice) · anything reaching a store through a single distribution centre · everything sold north of 60.

**Three independent, fully-sourced legs:**

1. **Beef is a biology problem, not a price problem.** 11.1 million cattle on 1 Jan 2026 — the first herd increase since 2018, but from the smallest base since the late 1980s. Prices up **62.6% since 2021**, **+18.8% y/y in January 2026**. The lead time is on the record: **Tyler Fulton, president of the Canadian Cattle Association — "The earliest that the product would end up being available to consumers to use would be three years from now."** The Food Price Report's verdict: "We don't see how beef prices could normalize before mid-2027." **This is the one item where stocking up is an actual hedge rather than a saving.**
2. **The 55% problem.** More than half of Canada's consumer-oriented food imports — $20.96B of $37.8B in 2024 — come from a single country, now on both sides of a 50% tariff wall. Nothing is scarce. The route is expensive.
3. **The Laval proof.** 30 March 2026: about 550 workers at one produce distribution centre stop. By 4 April, CBC photographs "completely barren" shelves across five banners in an entire province, with signs blaming "circumstances out of our control." The broccoli existed. The cantaloupes existed. **One building was the whole supply chain.** And at the extreme end of the same logic: Iqaluit, where there is no road at all, where the standardized weekly basket ran $536 in March 2024 — and where, in 2018, one warehouse fire emptied a city.

**Script value:** the emotional and intellectual payoff. Where Brewster's line lands, and where @TruthAndLight4995's 132-like comment becomes the thesis: "Price doesn't matter if the shelf is empty."

**Voiceover framing:** *"The fifteen things on this list were never in danger. The road to them was."*

**STRUCTURAL NOTE.** The reference's three buckets are roughly equal. **Don't copy that.** Canada's Bucket 1 is unusually strong and Bucket 3 unusually specific. Weight it **4 / 5 / 6** across the fifteen items. Bucket 1 opens on national confidence, Bucket 2 carries the practical argument, Bucket 3 closes on the mechanism — and the thesis ending falls straight out of Bucket 3 without a seam.

Slow-moving shelf-stable goods are the LAST to reflect a cost fall and the LAST to reflect a cost rise. That is precisely why a stock-up list works, and it is sourced.

### 3.1 How dairy prices are actually set
**Canadian Dairy Commission, "How is the price of milk set in Canada?"** (a) https://cdc-ccl.ca/en/node/714
> "The provincial milk marketing boards routinely update the price of milk components."
> "Price adjustment in % = (50% of the variation in the cost of production) + (50% of the consumer price index)"

**This is the structural point: it is a formula, not a market.** Half the move is farm cost, half is CPI. Calculated once a year, applied on a single date.

### 3.6 WHY CANADIAN PRICES MOVE DIFFERENTLY — the structural point

American dairy and egg prices are set on commodity exchanges. They move every day, spike on an outbreak or a drought, and collapse just as fast. Canadian farmgate prices are set by a formula — half farm cost, half CPI — calculated once a year and applied on one date: **1 February**. Provincial boards administer it. Nobody on a trading floor is involved.

1. **Canadian dairy and egg prices are slower and shallower in both directions.** Eggs +19.9% over five years against a 29.0% grocery average.
2. **The move is scheduled.** The next farmgate adjustment will be announced around the end of October 2026 and take effect 1 February 2027. **You know the date.**
3. **The border is a wall, not a valve.** Imports come in under tariff-rate quotas with "within access commitment" and "over access commitment" lines — exactly why the 8 September counter-tariff list is written in those terms.
4. **It cuts both ways.** Supply management insulated Canadian eggs from the American spike. It also means Canadian shoppers will not get an American-style price collapse when conditions improve.

### 3.7 WHICH DAIRY/EGG ITEMS ARE USABLE IN A SHELF-STABLE COUNTDOWN

**USE, strongly:**
- **Canned evaporated and condensed milk.** HS 0402.91 and 0402.99, **on the 8 September 2026 counter-tariff list at 50%.** Shelf-stable, unglamorous, carries a real dated Canadian-only pressure mechanism. Close to a perfect entry for this format.
- **Milk powder / skim milk powder.** HS 0402.10 and 0402.21, **50%.** Long shelf life. Same story.
- **Butter.** Freezes for months and is treated as a stock-up item by this audience. Carries the cleanest supply-management explainer available: a named commission, an exact support price, an exact percentage, an exact date. Also fully exempt from the FOP symbol — see section 7.

**USE AS EXPLAINER, NOT AS AN ITEM:**
- **Eggs.** Not shelf-stable, so not a countdown entry. But the single best illustration of supply management: flat at $4.95 a dozen, −2.4% CPI, against American volatility. Put it in the midpoint or the re-sort.
- **Fluid milk and cheese.** Not shelf-stable. Cheese is on the counter-tariff list at 25%, worth one sentence inside the milk-powder entry, not a list item.

**AVOID:** whey protein and casein. On the list at 50% and shelf-stable, but they sit in supplement territory, which drags toward health framing. Skip.

## 7. THE FRONT-OF-PACKAGE LABELLING DEADLINE

**TREATED STRICTLY AS A LABELLING AND REFORMULATION STORY.** The source documents contain health rationale; it has been deliberately omitted and must not be reintroduced. Saturated fat, sugars and sodium appear below ONLY as the named regulatory trigger variables. No nutrient is characterised as good or bad anywhere in this section.

### 7.1 The rule
Regulations Amending the Food and Drug Regulations (Nutrition Symbols, Other Labelling Provisions, Vitamin D and Hydrogenated Fats or Oils). In force 20 July 2022. Transition period ended **1 January 2026**.
**Health Canada, "Front-of-package nutrition symbol labelling guide for industry," Version 2, May 2023, 92 pp.** (a)
https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/legislation-guidelines/guidance-documents/front-package-nutrition-symbol-labelling-industry.html

Verbatim on what triggers it:
> "The regulations... require that a nutrition symbol appear on the label (specifically, on the principal display panel (PDP)) of a prepackaged product when the amount of saturated fat, sugars and/or sodium is equal to or higher than the specified nutrient thresholds."

**The thresholds — Table 4.1, referencing the table to section B.01.350, FDR** (a):

| Product type | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Prepackaged foods, reference amount **> 30 g or 30 mL**, not main dishes | **≥ 15% DV** |
| Prepackaged foods, reference amount **≤ 30 g or 30 mL** | **≥ 10% DV** |
| Prepackaged **main dishes**, reference amount ≥ 200 g (or ≥ 170 g for children 1-4) | **≥ 30% DV** |

Verbatim: "For most prepackaged products, a symbol must appear on the label when the amount of saturated fat, sugars and/or sodium is ≥ 15% of the applicable DV." And: "The 10% DV threshold always applies to a product with a small reference amount (more specifically, ≤ 30 g or 30 mL), even when the serving size is larger than 30 g or 30 mL."

**What the symbol looks like** (a), per the guide's description of Schedule K.1 formats: "There is a black magnifying glass inside the box and it is left-justified" · "The heading 'High in' appears in bold black letters inside the box at the top" · "There is at least one bar inside the box that identifies the product as 'high in sat fat', 'high in sugars' and/or 'high in sodium', as applicable." Bilingual versions carry "High in / Élevé en" and the bars read "Sat fat / Gras sat.", "Sugars / Sucres", "Sodium". The box is footed with "Health Canada / Santé Canada".

### 7.2 CFIA's enforcement language — verbatim
**CFIA, "January 1, 2026, deadline for Front-of-package (FOP) labelling."** (a)
https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/nutrition-labelling/fop-nutrition-symbol
> **"There will be no enforcement discretion after January 1, 2026"**
> "Products that were imported, manufactured in Canada, or packaged at retail before that date can still be sold and remain on store shelves. Products imported, manufactured in Canada, or packaged at retail on or after January 1, 2026, will be subject to the standard regulatory response process."

**THE SHOPPER-FACING CONSEQUENCE, AND IT IS A REAL ONE:** stock made before 1 January 2026 can legally remain on shelves indefinitely. **Right now, in September 2026, Canadian shelves carry a mix of pre-deadline and post-deadline packaging of the same products.** Long-shelf-life goods — canned, dried, jarred — are exactly where old packaging persists longest. Two identical products side by side, one symbol, one not, both legal.

### 7.3 The exemptions — AND THIS IS THE BEST PART FOR THIS FORMAT

**Full exemptions, verbatim from the guide (subsection B.01.350(5), FDR).** Never required to carry the symbol regardless of content (a):
- Shipping containers not sold as a single unit to a consumer at retail
- **Products with an available display surface < 15 cm²** — wrapped hard candies, caramels, mints, sticks of gum
- Individual portions intended solely to be served by a restaurant or commercial enterprise with meals or snacks
- Ready-to-serve multiple-serving products for commercial, industrial or institutional service
- Products intended only as ingredients in other prepackaged products or in commercial food preparation
- Cow or goat's milk and cream in refillable glass containers
- **"Sweetening agents, including those listed in Division 18 of the Food and Drug Regulations (FDR) and sold as such"** — examples given: **sugar; agave syrup; corn syrup; maple syrup; table syrup; honey; and molasses**
- **"Salt and seasoning salt that includes 'salt' in its common name and sold as such"** — examples: **table salt; celery salt; garlic salt; onion salt**
- **"Fats and oils referred to in Division 9 of the FDR, fish and other marine fats and oils, butter, ghee, margarine and other similar substitutes for butter and sold as such"** — examples: **brick of butter; tub of margarine; bottle of coconut oil; bottle of canola oil**
- Individual rations for military personnel on operations or exercises

**Conditional exemptions (subsection B.01.350(13), FDR)** (a): beverages over 0.5% alcohol; raw single-ingredient meat, poultry, fish or seafood; products sold only where prepared and processed; products sold only at roadside stands, craft shows, flea markets, fairs, farmers' markets or sugar bushes by the individual who prepared them; individual servings for immediate consumption; and **products with an available display surface < 100 cm².**

**THE PRICE PROOF (a).** StatCan CPI, **Condiments, spices and vinegars**: August 2026 index **170.8, +0.7% year over year**, +3.4% two-year. Matches the figure StatCan published in *The Daily* naming "condiments, spices and vinegars (+0.7%)" among the categories that slowed grocery inflation.

**SHELF-LIFE FACT — Canadian source: NONE FOUND.** The only quotable "almost indefinite" line comes from **The Vinegar Institute, a US trade body — foreign and not applicable to a Canadian regulatory claim.** Use the FDR B.01.007 >90-day framing instead and say the bottle's date is voluntary.

**BUYING RULE.** Turn the bottle around and read the acetic acid percentage — it is legally required to be on the front panel. Canadian law sets the range at **4.1% to 12.3%**, and a 5% white vinegar and a 7% white vinegar are different products at the same shelf position. If a bottle says **"For Manufacturing Use Only,"** the maximum-strength limit does not apply to it under B.19.009 — that is not a consumer product.
