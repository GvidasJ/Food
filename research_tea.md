# PART 1 — BRANDS & LABELS

## 1. RED ROSE (episode anchor)

### 1.1 Ownership — the two Red Roses (VERIFIED, the core story)
Red Rose in Canada and Red Rose in the USA are **different products with different owners**:

- **CANADA:** Red Rose is a brand of **Lipton Teas and Infusions** (the former Unilever tea division, owned by private-equity firm CVC Capital Partners since July 1, 2022). Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Rose_Tea and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipton_Teas_and_Infusions (which lists "Red Rose Tea (Canada only)" in the brand portfolio).
- **USA:** Red Rose is owned by **Redco Foods, Inc., a subsidiary of Teekanne (Germany)** — and since 2018 the US product has been produced by **Harris Tea Company** "under license." Chain per Wikipedia (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Rose_Tea): Unilever bought Red Rose in 1985 and sold the **US rights** to Redco Foods; Teekanne bought Redco in 1995; Harris Tea began making the US product in 2018.
- Atlantic Business Magazine (2024 web exclusive) confirms: the brand is "owned and produced by Lipton Teas and Infusions in Canada and Redco Foods, a subsidiary of Teekanne, in the U.S." — https://atlanticbusinessmagazine.ca/web-exclusives/red-rose-tea-a-blend-130-years-in-the-making/
- Ownership timeline: Estabrooks sells to **Brooke Bond & Company** (England) in **1932** → **Unilever, 1985** → Unilever's tea business spun off as **ekaterra (2020–21)** → sold to **CVC Capital Partners Fund VIII for €4.5B, completed July 1, 2022** → renamed **Lipton Teas and Infusions**.

**Script framing that survives fact-check:** "The Red Rose box in a Canadian grocery store and the Red Rose box in an American grocery store are owned by two different companies on two different continents — a Dutch-headquartered, private-equity-owned tea giant here; a German tea family's US subsidiary there."

## 9. LABEL DECODES

### 9.1 "Orange Pekoe" — a grade, not a flavour (VERIFIED)
- Orange pekoe is a **leaf grade** in the Western black-tea grading system — leaf size/wholeness (classically the first leaf below the bud, whole-leaf grade), **nothing to do with oranges and not a flavour**. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tea_leaf_grading ; https://ladybakerstea.com/blogs/blog/what-exactly-is-orange-pekoe (PEI tea merchant)
- **"Pekoe"** likely from Chinese *bai hao* ("white down") — the downy hairs on young buds (Wikipedia, above).
- **"Orange"** — the House of Orange-Nassau story (Dutch East India Company marketing) is a **legend; present as such, attributed**: the company "*may have* marketed the tea as 'orange' to suggest association with the House of Orange."
- In North America "orange pekoe" colloquially = generic black-tea blend; Canadian boxes use it as the product's actual *name* — the joke: the best-selling "flavour" of tea in Canada is a leaf-size specification.

### 9.2 Ingredient lists: two words vs a paragraph
- Plain black tea lists one ingredient: Tetley — "Black (Orange Pekoe) Tea"; Red Rose — expect "Black tea / Thé noir" (**re-read both boxes on camera**).
- Flavoured contrast (verbatim from Amazon.ca listing — **re-read package on camera**): **Tetley Chai Vanilla** — "black tea, clove, natural flavours, black pepper, cinnamon, chicory, ginger, cardamom, cardamom husk, and vanilla pods" (https://www.amazon.ca/Tetley-Chai-Vanilla-Black-Tea/dp/B0GLQVYTRX).
- Herbal contrast, zero tea: Celestial Sleepytime (verbatim, §8).
- Even certified-organic herbal tea can contain added "flavours": Four O'Clock Chamomile Citrus lists "organic flavours, natural flavours" (https://fouroclock.ca/products/chamomile-citrus-herbal-tea-organic-fairtrade).

### 9.4 Origin lines — why no tea box can say "Product of Canada"
- **Tea is not grown commercially in Canada**; every leaf is imported. Any Canadian-sounding line on a tea box is a value-added claim, not an origin claim.
- CFIA (verbatim from https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/consumers/canadian-food): "Product of Canada means the processing and labour are Canadian and a significant amount of the ingredients are Canadian." "Made in Canada means the last substantial transformation of the product occurred in Canada" (must be qualified, e.g. "Made in Canada from imported ingredients"). CFIA's list of acceptable descriptive claims includes **"Roasted and blended in Canada" for coffee "since the beans are imported"** and "Packaged in Canada" — the exact template tea labels use.
- On-camera checklist per box: find the line — expect **"blended and packed in Canada (from imported tea)"** on King Cole; UK/EU packing lines on Tetley and Twinings; and whatever Red Rose now says (that's the reveal, §1.5).

# PART 2 — LAW, HISTORY & ECONOMICS

## 0. HEADLINE DISCOVERY — Canada's tea standard moved house in December 2024
Division 20 ("Tea") of the Food and Drug Regulations was **repealed** by SOR/2024-244, s. 116, and its content relocated nearly word-for-word into the CFIA-maintained **Canadian Food Compositional Standards** (incorporated by reference into the FDR — still legally binding).
- Verbatim, Canada Gazette Part II (SOR/2024-244, registered Nov 29 2024, published Dec 18 2024; https://gazette.gc.ca/rp-pr/p2/2024/2024-12-18/html/sor-dors244-eng.html): "116 Division 20 of Part B of the Regulations is repealed."
- CFIA (verbatim): "No transition period was provided for food compositional standards since there is no change to the regulatory requirements." — https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/summary-changes-compositional-standards
- RIAS: standards for 19 commodity groups including "vinegar; **tea**; marine and fresh water animal products" move "from the FDR to a document entitled Canadian Food Compositional Standards." The "[S]" symbol marking standardized foods is gone from the FDR.
- **On-screen framing:** "For 70-odd years the tea standard lived in the Food and Drug Regulations. Since December 2024 it lives in a CFIA standards document — same rules, new address."

## 1. THE CANADIAN LEGAL STANDARD FOR TEA (legal centrepiece)

### 1A. The OLD law — FDR Division 20 (in force until Dec 17, 2024)
All verbatim from Justice Laws "previous version" pages (versions "from 2006-03-22 to 2024-12-17"):
- **B.20.001** (https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/C.R.C.,_c._870/section-B.20.001-20060322.html): "B.20.001 [S]. Tea shall be the dried leaves and buds of Thea sinensis (L.) Sims prepared by the usual trade processes."
  - ⚠️ The historic text said "**Thea sinensis (L.) Sims**" — the older botanical synonym, NOT "Camellia sinensis." The 2024 standard modernized it. Beat: for decades Canadian law defined tea by a plant name botanists had long since renamed.
- **B.20.002 — black tea** (https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/C.R.C.,_c._870/section-B.20.002-20060322.html): "B.20.002 [S]. Black Tea shall be black tea or a blend of two or more black teas and shall contain, on the dry basis, not less than 30 per cent water-soluble extractive, as determined by official method FO-37, Determination of Water-Soluble Extractive in Tea, October 15, 1981, and not less than four per cent and not more than seven per cent total ash."
  - ⚠️ There is **no caffeine minimum** in the black-tea standard. Caffeine appears only in the decaf standard.
- **B.20.003 — unblended-tea escape hatch**: original unblended black tea needs only 25% water-soluble extractive if "packaged according to good commercial practice in the country of origin." (https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/C.R.C.,_c._870/section-B.20.003-20060322.html)
- **B.20.004 — green tea**: "not less than 33 per cent water-soluble extractive… and not less than four per cent and not more than seven per cent total ash." (Fun beat: green tea's minimum, 33%, is HIGHER than black tea's 30%.)
- **B.20.005 — decaffeinated tea** (https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/C.R.C.,_c._870/section-B.20.005-20060322.html): "(a) shall be tea of the type indicated, from which caffeine has been removed and that, as a result of the removal, contains not more than 0.4 per cent caffeine…" — **the 0.4% decaf ceiling is confirmed.**
- Current pages now read e.g. "B.20.001 [Repealed, SOR/2024-244, s. 116]".

### 1B. The NEW law — Canadian Food Compositional Standards, Volume 17 "Tea" (since Dec 18, 2024)
URL: https://inspection.canada.ca/en/about-cfia/acts-and-regulations/list-acts-and-regulations/documents-incorporated-reference/canadian-food-compositional-standards-0 (Volume 17, section 17.1). Verbatim:
- "17.1.1 Tea is the dried leaves and buds of Camellia sinensis prepared by the usual trade processes."
- "17.1.2 Black Tea is black tea or a blend of two or more black teas and contains, on the dry basis, not less than 30% water-soluble extractive… and not less than 4% and not more than 7% total ash."
- "17.1.4 Green Tea contains, on the dry basis, not less than 33% water-soluble extractive…"
- "17.1.5 Decaffeinated (naming the type of tea) is tea of the type indicated, from which caffeine has been removed and that, as a result of the removal, contains not more than 0.4% caffeine."
- Micro-changes: Thea sinensis → Camellia sinensis; "shall be/shall contain" → plain-language "is/contains".

### 1C. "Herbal tea" is not legally tea
- The standard defines tea as Camellia sinensis only — peppermint/chamomile/rooibos products are outside it. CFIA archived guidance (search-excerpt of archived page — RE-VERIFY before verbatim quoting): "'(Naming the plant) Herbal tea' is an appropriate name for a product not made solely from the tea plant"; French labels distinguish cleanly: "tisane" (herbal) vs "thé" (tea). Archived URL: https://epe.lac-bac.gc.ca/100/206/301/cfia-acia/2011-09-21/inspection.gc.ca/english/fssa/labeti/decisions/bevboie.shtml
- Current machinery: common-name rules — CFIA https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/common-name
- **Beat:** the box says "Peppermint Herbal Tea" because legally it can't just say "tea."

### 1D. Canada has a tea standard; the US does not — and America fired its tea tasters
- US **Tea Importation Act of 1897** created a **Board of Tea Experts** — federal tea tasters. Verbatim, FDA 2023 Federal Register final rule (https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-09-19/html/2023-20012.htm): "The Board was responsible for making recommendations to the Secretary of the Treasury to fix and establish uniform standards of purity, quality, and fitness for consumption of all kinds of teas imported into the United States."
- **Federal Tea Tasters Repeal Act of 1996** (Public Law 104-128, approved April 9, 1996): "The Tea Importation Act (21 U.S.C. 41 et seq.) is repealed." (https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Public_Law_104-128; GovTrack: https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/104/hr2969). Signed by President Clinton.
- **Kicker:** FDA didn't formally strike the Board from its advisory-committee regulations until **September 19, 2023** — 27 years later (same FR document). Colour: Reason, Mar 17 2024: https://reason.com/2024/03/17/after-a-century-the-federal-tea-board-is-finally-dead/
- Canada, meanwhile, has continuously maintained black/green/decaf standards (Division 20 → FCSD Vol. 17).

### 1E. Canada's own food-law history
- "The first federal legislation dealing with adulteration of food was enacted in 1874"; "On 1 January 1875, an Act to Prevent the Adulteration of Food, Drink and Drugs came into effect" — The Canadian Encyclopedia (https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/food-legislation). Framing "Canada regulated food adulteration in 1874; the US waited until 1906" is supported.
- No Canadian "Tea Act"/tea-taster corps found — do not claim one existed.

## 2. FOP SYMBOL & THE MISSING NUTRITION FACTS TABLE

### 2A. Why plain tea can legally have NO Nutrition Facts table
- FDR B.01.401(2) (verbatim; https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/C.R.C.,_c._870/section-B.01.401.html): "(2) Subsection (1) does not apply to a prepackaged product if (a) all the information set out in column 1 of the table to this section, other than in respect of item 1 ("Serving of stated size"), may be expressed as "0" in the nutrition facts table…"
- Plain tea rounds to zero across the board → box qualifies. CFIA exemptions page: https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/nutrition-labelling/exemptions. **Fragile:** exemption is lost if vitamins/minerals added or nutrient claims made (B.01.401(3)).
- Bonus (verbatim, CFIA NFT page https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/nutrition-labelling/nutrition-facts-table): "for specific forms of coffee (whole or ground beans and pods) and tea (dry loose leaves, bags and pods), the serving size is based on a serving of the food "as consumed" rather than "as sold"."

### 2B. FOP "high in" symbol — in force Jan 1, 2026
- CFIA FOP page: https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/nutrition-labelling/fop-nutrition-symbol (FDR B.01.350–354); no enforcement discretion after Jan 1, 2026.
- Plain tea: zero sat fat/sugars/sodium → never meets a threshold → no symbol. The math does the work.
- **Sweetened iced tea is Health Canada's own worked example** (FOP guide Annex 1, verbatim): "In this example, only the % DV for sugars declared in the NFt (43%) is equal to or greater than the 15% DV threshold. Therefore, the label must carry a symbol for "high in sugars"." — "Figure A1.2. Lemon-flavoured iced tea that requires a symbol for "high in sugars"."
- **Beat:** the same word — "tea" — spans a product needing no nutrition table at all and a product that must wear the "high in sugars" magnifying glass. Present purely as labelling law.

## 3. "ORANGE PEKOE" — LEGALLY UNDEFINED (verified by elimination)
1. FDR Division 20 / FCSD Vol. 17: standards only for Tea, Black Tea, Green Tea, Decaffeinated. No "orange pekoe," no leaf grades.
2. Canadian Grade Compendium (9 volumes: carcasses, produce, dairy, eggs, honey, maple syrup, fish, imports) — **no tea volume**.
3. CFIA "Grades of food" (verbatim; https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/grades-food): "It is not permitted to use a Canadian grade on a product for which the regulations do not prescribe a grade standard." — i.e., there IS no Canadian grade standard for tea; "orange pekoe" is an industry/trade term for leaf size and style.
- **Careful phrasing:** never "illegal" or "meaningless" — say: unlike "black tea" (a real, testable legal standard), "orange pekoe" has no definition anywhere in Canadian food law; only the general false-and-misleading prohibition (FDA s. 5(1)) governs it.

### 4C. Tariffs & tax
- Normal times: tea (HS 09.02) enters Canada MFN "Free" (secondary lookup https://tariffcalc.ca/en/hs-code/0902.10.90.10 — confirm against CBSA Customs Tariff Ch. 9 before an on-screen graphic).
- **2025 trade war (verified):** United States Surtax Order (2025-1), SOR/2025-66, effective March 4 2025 — 25% surtax on $30B of US-origin goods, **tea on the list** (with coffee, OJ, peanut butter, wine). Primary: https://gazette.gc.ca/rp-pr/p2/2025/2025-03-12/html/sor-dors66-eng.html — "goods that originate in the United States that are classified under any of the tariff items set out in the schedule are subject to a surtax in the amount of 25% of the value for duty." Corroboration: https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/canada-imposes-25-tariffs-united-states-retaliation-trump-tariffs
- Aug 22 2025: PM Carney announced removal of the surtax for CUSMA-qualifying US imports effective Sept 1, 2025 (steel/aluminum/autos excepted). https://www.blakes.com/insights/us-canada-tariffs-timeline-of-key-dates-and-documents/
- **Beat:** tea grows nowhere in the continental US at scale — but tea *packed or blended in the US* meant a leaf from Sri Lanka briefly cost 25% more because of a fight with Washington.
- **GST** (CRA Memorandum 4.3 "Basic Groceries," verbatim para. 120; https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/publications/4-3/basic-groceries.html): "Beverages dispensed at the place where they are sold are taxable. This includes servings of all beverages such as milk, coffee, tea and ice tea, hot chocolate and juice when dispensed where supplied, regardless of size and whether hot or cold." Buy the box: no GST. Buy the cup: taxed.

### 4D. History beats (attributed narrative)
- **HBC:** annual supply ship "laden with trade goods… and luxuries such as sugar, tea, and chocolate" (https://www.historylink.org/file/9881; https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/hudsons-bay-company).
- **CPR Empresses:** RMS Empress of India arrived Vancouver on maiden voyage **April 28, 1891** (City of Vancouver Archives). The Empresses "fed high-value silk and tea shipments to the railway… on schedules faster than the fast passenger trains" (Legion Magazine: https://legionmagazine.com/empresses-of-the-trans-pacific-fleet/). ⚠️ Pacific Empresses (India/China/Japan) — do NOT conflate with Empress of Ireland (Atlantic, 1914).
- **T.H. Estabrooks:** tea business at 61-63 Dock Street, Saint John, founded May 1, 1894; "Red Rose" brand launched **1899**; pitch = pre-packaged consistent blends instead of loose tea from chests; d. April 5, 1945 (https://www.redrosetea.ca/a-history-of-a-classic/).
- **Peter C. Larkin (Salada):** founded Toronto 1892; named after a Ceylon tea garden; foil packets for uniform flavour; "He prided himself on being known as 'The Tea King of America'"; **Canadian High Commissioner to the UK, 1922–1930** (Dictionary of Canadian Biography: https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/larkin_peter_charles_15E.html).
- **Barbour's:** "the only tea manufacturer in Atlantic Canada" (CBC 150th piece).
- **WWII rationing:** Canadian War Museum (verbatim; https://www.warmuseum.ca/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/AB21.1-Eng-RationBookandTokens.pdf): "Coupon rationing soon began, starting with gasoline in April 1942. Over the next year, sugar, coffee, tea, butter and meat were also rationed." Allowance widely reported: adults **1 oz tea OR 4 oz coffee per week, not both**; children under 5 no tea ration from July 1942; **off ration September 1944**. ⚠️ One source dates coupon start Sept 1943 — safe formulation: "rationed from 1942, an ounce of tea a week, off ration in September 1944."
- **Boston Tea Party contrast (colour only):** 1773 Boston dumped the tea; British North America kept the kettle on; Loyalist migration reinforced tea culture — fitting that Canada's two Victorian tea fortunes (Red Rose, King Cole) were built in Loyalist Saint John.

## 6. THE "NATURAL FLAVOUR" RULE
- Ingredient-list class names — CFIA "Common Names for Ingredients and Components" (incorporated by reference; https://inspection.canada.ca/en/about-cfia/acts-and-regulations/list-acts-and-regulations/documents-incorporated-reference/common-names-ingredients-and-components), Table 1, verbatim: item 4 → "flavour" (animal/vegetable raw materials); item 5 → "artificial flavour", "imitation flavour" or "simulated flavour" (chemical synthesis involved).
- CFIA "Guidelines for highlighted ingredients and flavours" (archived guidance, verbatim; https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/industry/composition-and-quality/guidelines): "Simple references to the flavour of products in flashes or the common name (e.g., "blueberry" or "cherry candy") do not require clarifying information on products such as puddings, soft drinks, jelly powders, gum, hard candy, popsicles, flavoured teas or flavoured coffees"
- **Beat:** "Peach tea" doesn't have to contain peaches — flavoured tea is a named category where flavouring is presumed. Flip the box: "flavour" = natural raw material; "artificial flavour" = synthesis involved. (Say "long-standing CFIA guidance" — page is archived.)
