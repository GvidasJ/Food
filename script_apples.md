In October 2025, Canada imported 15.6 million kilograms of fresh apples, and 15.3 million of them came from the United States, according to Statistics Canada's trade data. That was harvest month. The sticker on the apple in your hand has a four-digit number on it, and that number tells you the variety. It does not tell you the country. Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada's own overview, dated July 16, 2025, says that in any given year over 80 percent of Canada's fresh apple imports come from the U.S., with close to 80 percent coming from Washington state. So the apple you think of as local often is not, and the apple you pay the most for is one no Canadian is allowed to grow. Today, eight varieties, ranked by how many acres of them Canada actually grows, using the growers' own reports, most Canadian first, and the one no Canadian orchard is allowed to plant last. Then the three that are Canadian all the way down. In February we ranked these apples by what is inside them. Today we rank them by where they come from, how old the variety is, who owns it and what it costs, and on that basis three verdicts change. Because the truth is not always on the menu.

Number eight, and the most Canadian apple on the avoid list, Gala. It is the bag you grab without reading, and the single most planted apple in the country. The Ontario Apple Growers' 2025 annual report puts Gala at 3,162 acres, one fifth of every apple tree in the province and first by a wide margin. British Columbia's 2026 insured acreage adds 1,631 acres, second only to Ambrosia. So why is it here? Because it is also the most imported apple in Canada. The same Ontario report, using Statistics Canada data, records 83.9 million pounds of Gala imported in the 2024 crop year. Ontario's entire 2024 Gala crop was 80.1 million pounds. Canada imported more Gala than Ontario grew, and the four-digit sticker is identical on both. The variety itself is a New Zealand original, introduced to Quebec orchards in 1965 according to the Quebec growers' association, and named Royal Gala after Queen Elizabeth the Second chose it on a visit, according to the U.S. apple association's own account. Nobody owns it, so anyone anywhere can grow it, and they do. Washington harvests it from mid-July and markets it year-round, according to the Washington association's calendar. We captured 29 Gala listings across seven banners on September 28 and 29, 2026. One showed a country of origin. Prices ran from 2.20 a kilogram in a Walmart Mississauga single to 6.61 a kilogram loose at Voilà and Superstore. In fairness, the Ontario summary notes that Gala imports have decreased substantially since 2014 with demand being filled locally, and a Gala in a bag marked product of Ontario or product of Quebec in October is exactly what it says. The verdict is not the tree. It is the bag with no origin line. Next, the apple that pays a royalty. Number seven, with 3,137 Canadian acres.

Number seven, Honeycrisp. The apple that sells for eight dollars a kilogram. The University of Minnesota bred it, and its own pages and the Minnesota Historical Society give the record: a seedling saved from the discard pile in 1982, a U.S. plant patent filed in November 1988, commercial release in 1991, and 16.5 million dollars in royalties collected by 2019, the historical society says. Canada granted it Plant Breeders' Rights on August 10, 2001, certificate 1007, and those rights expired on August 10, 2019, according to the Canadian Food Inspection Agency's register. The university's page now says Honeycrisp trees are no longer under patent protection in the U.S. or Canada. Ontario has 2,538 acres of it, second in the province, and B.C. 599 acres. Now the money. The Ontario Apple Growers report the 2024 grower return for Honeycrisp at 67 cents a pound, the highest of any variety, about a dollar forty-eight a kilogram. On September 28, 2026, loose Honeycrisp was 8.80 a kilogram at Real Canadian Superstore in Vancouver and 9.90 at Voilà, 5.50 at Walmart Mississauga, and 4.41 on a flyer special at Loblaws Markham. The president of the B.C. Fruit Growers' Association, Deep Brar, told the Vancouver Sun in August 2026, they're selling our apples for 4.99 a pound, and I'm getting 30 cents. He did not name the variety, and the paper did not name the store. Honeycrisp was not imported into Canada at all until 2018, the Ontario summary says; 7.9 million pounds came in during the 2024 crop year, against an Ontario crop of 61 million. In fairness, that makes an October Honeycrisp one of the more likely apples to be Canadian, and the royalty has ended here. But the name is Minnesota's brand, the premium is the brand's, and the grower gets 67 cents of it. Number six, a name that was a marketing decision. 946 Canadian acres.

Number six, Red Delicious. Deep red, tall, the apple in every school lunch of the 1980s. Stark Bro's, the Missouri nursery that sold it, tells the story on its own site. A chance seedling on Jesse Hiatt's farm at Peru, Iowa, which he called Hawkeye, won first prize at the nursery's fruit show in 1894. Stark bought the sole rights from Hiatt and registered Stark Delicious as a trademark. Only after the nursery introduced Golden Delicious in 1914 did it become Red Delicious. So the name is a 1914 marketing decision by a nursery, and the apple is Iowa's. In Canada it is a shrinking variety. The Ontario Apple Growers' 2025 report puts it at 885 acres, down from 1,213 acres in 2019, a 22 percent drop in five years. The U.S. agriculture department's Ottawa post wrote in December 2025 that acreage of McIntosh and Red Delicious varieties continues to decline as producers replace older orchards with varieties that are more in demand with today's consumer. B.C. has 61 acres. Quebec grows it in small quantity, the Quebec growers say. And the imports? 20.3 million pounds in the 2024 crop year, according to the Ontario report, against an Ontario crop of 20.3 million pounds. Half of what Canadians buy is grown here and half is not, and in the United States it is still the second-largest variety, 39 million bushels in 2025 by the U.S. apple association's count. Sixteen listings captured; none showed a country of origin. Prices from 2.20 a kilogram at Walmart to 8.82 loose at Loblaws Markham. In fairness, an October Red Delicious in an Ontario bag is in season, the growers list it from October. But this is an apple Canada is pulling out of the ground, that Washington keeps sending, under a name chosen to sell. Number five, an apple from Japan. 438 Canadian acres.

Number five, Fuji. The big one, sold by the piece. The Washington State Apple Commission says it was named for Fujisaki, Japan, and is a cross between Red Delicious and Ralls Janet. So it is a Japanese apple with an Iowa parent, and a public variety with no rights on the Canadian register. Canada grows a little of it. Ontario has 383 acres, up 16 percent in five years, and B.C. 55 acres. Here is the catch. Ontario's growers list Fuji as available from mid to late October, and B.C.'s production guide gives the harvest as mid to late October. A Fuji on a Canadian shelf in early October is not this year's Canadian crop. It is storage fruit from somewhere else. Washington markets Fuji all twelve months, and Washington State University lists its storage life at twelve months. Chile's exporters forecast 123,000 tonnes of Fuji exports worldwide for 2026. The Vancouver Sun's photo caption on August 31, 2026, read: Fuji apples from Washington state at Save-On-Foods in Surrey. Statistics Canada's import classification has no line for Fuji at all. It sits inside a category called unspecified, which reached 120 million pounds in the 2024 crop year, the largest single line, and the Ontario summary notes that could be due to the rise of club cultivars. Sixteen Fuji listings captured; none showed a country of origin. Prices from 2.16 a kilogram on a Loblaws special to 6.59 at No Frills Toronto. In fairness, Ontario's Fuji plantings are growing, and by November a Canadian one exists. But the variety was bred in Japan, the shelf fruit in October is imported, and the sticker says neither. Number four, the first apple that was sold as a brand. 229 Canadian acres.

Number four, Pink Lady. The pink one with the trademark symbol on the sticker. The brand's own site says it plainly: Pink Lady is a brand, not a variety. The variety is Cripps Pink, bred by the Western Australia Department of Agriculture, and the department's own journal published John Cripps's account of it in 1993. The trademark is owned by Apple and Pear Australia Limited, managed globally by a firm called Twenty Degrees, and reserved for the exclusive use of licensees, the brand says, in over 120 countries. For Canada, the U.S. licence holder's page lists the territory as Pink Lady America, exports only. So the brand is Australian, the North American licence is American, and Canada is an export market. Canada does grow the variety. B.C. lists 229 insured acres, mostly in the Similkameen, with harvest in late October to early November, and Ontario does not publish a separate acreage for it. That means an early-October Pink Lady is imported storage fruit. Washington markets it from November through July, and Chile forecasts 116,000 tonnes of Pink Lady and Cripps Pink exports in 2026. Fifteen listings captured; none showed origin. Prices: 8.80 a kilogram at Voilà, 6.61 loose at Loblaws and Superstore, 5.50 at Walmart. In fairness, nothing on the record suggests anyone has done anything wrong; a brand is allowed to license itself. But a Canadian is paying a brand premium to an Australian licensor, for fruit shipped by an American licensee, on a variety B.C. only starts picking in late October. Number three, the grandmother's apple. 112 Canadian acres.

Number three, Granny Smith. The green one. The City of Ryde, the Sydney council where she lived, keeps the record. Maria Ann Smith, born in Sussex in 1799, found a seedling by a creek on her Eastwood farm around 1868, grown from the remains of French crab apples from Tasmania. The New South Wales Department of Agriculture named it Granny Smith's Seedling in 1895, twenty-five years after her death. It is a public variety, older than Canada's rights law. And Canada barely grows it. Ontario's growers do not list it as a variety at all; it appears only inside a footnote of other cultivars. B.C. has 112 acres, about 1,741 tons, which is under four million pounds. Now the import line. Canada brought in 42.4 million pounds of Granny Smith in the 2024 crop year, the second most imported named variety after Gala, according to the Ontario report, 27 million pounds of it through Ontario ports. The Washington association markets Granny Smith all twelve months, and Chile lists it among its top five export varieties. So on the numbers that are published, roughly nine in ten Granny Smiths sold in Canada are imports. Twenty-nine listings captured; not one showed a country of origin. Prices from 5.12 a kilogram at Giant Tiger to 8.80 loose at Voilà. In fairness, none of that is the apple's fault, and a green apple from Washington in October is exactly what most of them have always been. But a hundred and fifty-eight years after the seedling by the creek, the orchard on your sticker is not hers, and it is almost never ours. Now the two apples no Canadian grows. Number two, with no Canadian acres on any published list.

Number two, Envy. The one with the name on the sticker and the price to match. New Zealand's Plant and Food Research bred it. The Canadian register says breeder Allan White made a controlled cross in 1985 at Havelock North, Royal Gala by Braeburn, and selected the seedling in 1992. The marketer T&G Global says it was launched in 2008. Canada granted Plant Breeders' Rights on the variety, called Scilate, on November 16, 2018, certificate 5843, and they run until November 16, 2043, so the variety is protected in Canada for another seventeen years. T&G calls itself the exclusive global licence holder, and its majority shareholder is BayWa Global Produce of Germany. Is it grown here? We could not find a single Canadian grower on any T&G, Envy or association page. Loblaw told the Vancouver Sun in August 2026 that some varieties customers ask for, such as Cosmic Crisp, Envy and Jazz, aren't available from B.C. suppliers at all. In July 2025, New Zealand's Farmers Weekly reported that T&G's Envy and Jazz sold in Canada came in 2.27-kilogram bags with prominent product of USA labelling, from Washington state and the southern hemisphere. Washington markets Envy from October through March, and T&G's new-season New Zealand fruit ships from April. Voilà listed Envy at 7.70 a kilogram in a bag and 9.90 loose on September 29, 2026; Superstore in Calgary and Vancouver at 7.71. In fairness, the licence is the owner's to grant, and nothing on the record says otherwise. But the variety is New Zealand's, the licence holder is majority-owned by a German company, the fruit is Washington's or the southern hemisphere's, and the only Canadian thing about the transaction is the customer.

Number one, Cosmic Crisp, with zero Canadian acres by rule. The apple with the stars on its skin and the highest price per kilogram of any conventional apple we found. Washington State University bred it. Its breeder, Bruce Barritt, crossed Enterprise with Honeycrisp in 1997 in Wenatchee, according to the Canadian register, seeds germinated in 1998, and the first boxes reached stores in December 2019, the university says. Then the university did something no other breeder on this list did. It made the variety Washington-only. The licensing company's own page says Cosmic Crisp is exclusively available in Washington State for all of North America. The university's FAQ says trees are available to all Washington State growers, and that Washington-grown fruit can be marketed anywhere in the world. Growers pay a dollar a tree plus 4.75 percent of every box that sells for twenty U.S. dollars or more. In April 2026, Capital Press reported that the university extended that Washington-only exclusivity from 2027 through May 26, 2032, the day its U.S. patent expires, with royalties of more than eight million dollars in fiscal 2025 and 22 million trees in the ground. Kevin Brandt of the licensing company told FreshPlaza in May 2026: after 2032, it is basically up to WSU to decide whether areas outside Washington can sell the apple with the trademark or not. And here is the part for Canada. The Canadian Food Inspection Agency granted Washington State University Plant Breeders' Rights on the variety on September 8, 2022, certificate 6655, running to 2047. So the variety is protected in Canada, and no Canadian is licensed to grow it. Every Cosmic Crisp on a Canadian shelf is a Washington import by rule, not by chance, until at least 2032. On September 29, 2026, Voilà listed it at 10.34 a kilogram, Loblaws Markham at 8.82, No Frills Toronto at 8.80. Eleven listings captured; none showed a country of origin, and none needed to. In fairness, the rule is the university's to make, and nothing improper is alleged. But by the basis this video stated at the top, this is the apple with the most to answer for. It cannot be Canadian. It is not allowed to be. The most Canadian thing about it is the sticker that ought to say where it is from.

So where does that leave a Canadian who wants an apple from a Canadian tree this Thanksgiving? Three varieties, in order of how much of them we grow, least to most. We have no commercial relationship with any grower, packer, marketer or licensor named in this video, and none of them knew it was being made.

First, Spartan, 373 Canadian acres. The only apple on either list bred by the Canadian government. Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada's Summerland station made the cross in 1926, the tree first fruited in 1932, and it was named in 1936, according to the station's own scientists writing in 1999; the Quebec growers give the same year. Its pedigree is McIntosh by unknown. It was never patented; there is no rights record in Canada. B.C. has 183 insured acres and Ontario 190, and Quebec lists it among its four traditional varieties. Harvest runs from October in Ontario, late September in Quebec. We found it at 3.86 a kilogram at Voilà and 4.40 to 4.42 at Superstore, No Frills and Loblaws. To be fair, it is small, and the acreage in B.C. has shrunk from 293 acres in 2021 to 183. But a Canadian scientist, a Canadian orchard, a Canadian apple, and nobody has ever collected a royalty on it.

Second, McIntosh, 2,268 acres in the two provinces that count, and Quebec grows more than either. Parks Canada's record: in 1811, John McIntosh found an apple sapling on his land in Matilda township, Dundela, Ontario, and the family's propagation of it became a National Historic Event, designated October 28, 1999. It is the parent of Spartan, Cortland, Empire and Lobo. Never patented. Quebec's government says McIntosh is more than a third of the province's production, and the U.S. agriculture post writes that Quebec has the largest McIntosh acreage in Canada. Ontario has 1,943 acres. Canada imported zero pounds of McIntosh in the 2024 crop year. Zero. No Frills Toronto had it at 2.75 a kilogram in a four-pound bag; Loblaws Markham at 2.21 on special. To be fair, Ontario's acreage fell from 2,556 acres in 2019 to 1,943 in 2024, and the U.S. post calls it a variety no longer popular for fresh eating. But it is 215 years old, born here, never imported, and among the cheapest bags in the aisle.

Third, Ambrosia, 4,014 acres, and the most Canadian apple in the country. The B.C. government's Buy BC page: the first Ambrosia apple was discovered by chance in Cawston, B.C., and the mother tree is still alive. The organic growers' association says Wilfrid and Sally Mennell found the seedling in a row of new Jonagold trees in 1991. The Mennells applied for Canadian Plant Breeders' Rights on March 21, 1994, certificate 0388 was granted on August 20, 1997, and it expired on August 20, 2015, according to the CFIA register. The U.S. patent expired in June 2017. So a Canadian family found it, a Canadian certificate protected it, and when that certificate ran out the variety became open to any Canadian grower. The name remains a trademark used under licence. B.C. now has 2,274 insured acres, first in the province, and Ontario 1,740, up from 1,260 in 2019. Harvest is early to mid-October in B.C. It has not been an easy road. In July 2024 the BC Tree Fruits Cooperative, which had packed fruit for about half of the Okanagan's growers, stopped taking fruit two weeks before harvest and entered creditor protection on August 13, 2024, court file S-245481. Its Oliver packing house reopened in 2025 under Algoma Orchards of Ontario, and Loblaw told the Vancouver Sun that about 60 percent of the apples in its B.C. stores over the year are B.C.-grown. Superstore Calgary and Vancouver had a five-pound Farmer's Market bag at 5.98, which is 2.63 a kilogram, and the Loblaw listing said hand-picked and grown in Quebec. And the reversal. In February this channel put Ambrosia on the avoid list on a nutrition basis. On origin, ownership and acreage, it is the most Canadian apple on any shelf. Found by a family, protected by Canada, released to Canada.

How to protect yourself. First, the sticker and the sign. The four-digit code identifies the variety and whether it is organic, according to the body that assigns it; it never identifies the country. For imported apples in a bag or tray, federal regulation requires product of, produce of, grown in, or country of origin, followed by the country, on the front panel. For loose apples, read the bin sign, and if there is no origin on it, ask. Second, the calendar. Ontario's growers list McIntosh and Gala from September, Honeycrisp from late September, Ambrosia, Spartan, Empire, Cortland and Red Delicious from October, and Fuji from mid to late October. Pink Lady is late October in B.C. An apple on a Canadian shelf before its Canadian harvest month is either last year's or somebody else's. Third, the price per kilo. Statistics Canada's average retail price for apples, table 18-10-0245-01, was 4.40 a kilogram in October 2025 and 6.45 in July 2026, a 32 percent swing. October is the cheapest month of the year for the same fruit. Fourth, buy the Canadian-bred ones in season: Spartan, McIntosh, Ambrosia, plus Cortland and Empire, which were bred in New York but are grown here, never imported, and sat at 2.75 to 4.41 a kilogram this week.

Eight apples with something in common. Not one of them was bred in Canada, one of them is not allowed to be grown here, and the sticker on every one of them says the variety and not the country. The apple does not know where it was grown. The sign does. Thank you for watching.
