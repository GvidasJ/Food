In October 2025, Canada imported 15.6 million kilograms of fresh apples, and 15.3 million of them came from the United States, according to Statistics Canada's trade data. That was harvest month. The sticker on a loose apple carries a number, and that number tells you the variety. It does not tell you the country. Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada's own overview, dated July 16, 2025, says that in any given year over 80 percent of Canada's fresh apple imports come from the U.S., with close to 80 percent coming from Washington state. So the apple you think of as local often is not, and the apple behind the highest price we found is one only Washington growers are licensed to plant in North America. Today, eight varieties, ranked by acres in Ontario and B.C., the only two provinces that publish acreage by variety, most planted first and zero acres last. Quebec and Nova Scotia do not publish those numbers, so the apples they grow most, McIntosh above all, are undercounted. Then the three that started in Canada. In February we ranked these apples by what is inside them. Today we rank them by where they come from, who owns them and what they cost, and on that basis the list changes. Ambrosia and Spartan move to worth buying, Cortland and Empire come off the avoid list, and Gala, which we called worth it, goes on it. Because the truth is not always on the menu.

Number eight, Gala. It is the bag you grab without reading, and the most planted apple in the two provinces that count variety acreage. The Ontario Apple Growers' 2025 annual report puts Gala at 3,162 acres, just under a fifth of the province's apple acres, ahead of Honeycrisp at 2,538. The B.C. government's 2026 insured acreage adds 1,631 acres, second only to Ambrosia. So why is it here? Because it is also the most imported named variety in Canada. The same Ontario report, using Statistics Canada data, records 83.9 million pounds of Gala imported in the 2024 crop year. Ontario's entire 2024 Gala crop was 80.1 million pounds. Canada imported more Gala than Ontario grew, though B.C.'s crop keeps Canadian Gala ahead overall. The variety is a New Zealand original, introduced into orchards in 1965, according to the Quebec growers' association, and the original Gala carries no royalty we could find. U.S.-grown Gala is harvested from mid-July and is typically available year-round, the same association says. On the evening of September 28, 2026, we captured 29 Gala listings across six banners online. Only one displayed an origin, and most of those sites have no origin field at all. Among conventional listings in stock, Gala ran from 2.20 a kilogram for a single on a flyer feature at Walmart in Mississauga to 6.61 for a 1.36-kilogram bag at Voilà. In fairness, Ontario's agriculture ministry says Gala imports have decreased substantially since 2014 with demand being filled locally, although its own table shows 80.0 million pounds in 2014 and 83.9 million in 2024. The verdict is not the tree. It is the bag with no origin line. Never buy a Gala without one. Next, an apple whose Canadian rights ran out in 2019. Number seven, with 3,137 acres in Ontario and B.C.

Number seven, Honeycrisp. The University of Minnesota bred it, and the Minnesota Historical Society gives the record. A seedling was saved from the discard pile in 1982, patented in 1988 and released in 1991. By 2019 the university had received 16.5 million U.S. dollars in royalties, the historical society says. Canada granted it Plant Breeders' Rights on August 10, 2001, certificate 1007, and those rights expired on August 10, 2019, according to the Canadian Food Inspection Agency's register. The university's page now says Honeycrisp trees are no longer under patent protection in the U.S. or Canada. Ontario has 2,538 acres of it, second in the province, and B.C. 599 insured acres. Now the money. The Ontario Apple Growers report the 2024 grower return for Honeycrisp at 67 cents a pound, the highest of any variety, about a dollar forty-eight a kilogram. On the evening of September 28, 2026, Honeycrisp was listed at 8.80 a kilogram loose at No Frills on Fraser Street in Vancouver, and 9.90 for a three-count pack at Voilà. It was 5.50 as a single at Walmart in Mississauga, and 4.41 on sale at Loblaws in Markham. A four-pound Loblaw bag described as hand-picked and grown in Quebec was 5.52 to 5.80. Statistics Canada did not track Honeycrisp imports separately until 2018, Ontario's agriculture ministry shows. In the 2024 crop year 7.9 million pounds came in, against an Ontario crop of 61 million. In fairness, that makes an October Honeycrisp one of the more likely apples to be Canadian, and the royalty has ended here. But the variety is Minnesota's, and in 2024 the Ontario grower got about a dollar forty-eight a kilogram for an apple listed this week at up to ten. Number six, a name a nursery trademarked. 946 acres in Ontario and B.C.

Number six, Red Delicious. Stark Bro's, the Missouri nursery that sold it, tells the story on its own site. A chance seedling on Jesse Hiatt's farm at Peru, Iowa, which he called Hawkeye, won first prize at the nursery's fruit show in 1894. Stark bought the sole rights from Hiatt and registered Stark Delicious as a trademark. Only after the nursery named Golden Delicious in 1914 did it become Red Delicious. So the name was a nursery's trademark, and the apple is Iowa's. In Ontario it is a shrinking variety. The Ontario Apple Growers' 2025 report puts it at 885 acres, down from 1,213 acres in 2019, a drop of about 27 percent in five years. The U.S. Department of Agriculture's Ottawa post wrote in December 2025 that in Ontario, acreage of McIntosh and Red Delicious varieties continues to decline as producers replace older orchards with varieties that are more in demand with today's consumer. B.C. has 61 insured acres. Quebec grows it in small quantity, the Quebec growers say. And the imports? 20.3 million pounds in the 2024 crop year, according to the Ontario report, about the size of Ontario's own 20.3-million-pound crop. On the evening of September 28, 2026, sixteen online listings displayed no origin. Prices ran from 2.20 a kilogram for single apples at Walmart in Mississauga to 8.82 loose at Loblaws in Markham. In fairness, an October Red Delicious in an Ontario bag is in season; the growers list it from October. But this is an apple Ontario is pulling out of the ground, that the United States still sends in volume, under a name a nursery trademarked. Number five, an apple from Japan. 438 acres in Ontario and B.C.

Number five, Fuji. The Washington State Apple Commission says it was named for Fujisaki, Japan, and is a cross between Red Delicious and Ralls Janet. So it is a Japanese apple with an Iowa parent, and a public variety with no rights on the Canadian register. Canada grows a little of it. Ontario has 383 acres, up from 256 in 2019, and B.C. 55. Here is the catch. Ontario's growers list Fuji as available from mid to late October, and B.C.'s production guide gives the harvest as mid to late October. A Fuji on a Canadian shelf in early October is not this year's Canadian crop. It is either last season's storage fruit or an import. The Washington State Apple Commission's calendar shows Washington Fuji all twelve months, and Washington State University lists its storage life at twelve months. A Vancouver Sun photo on August 31, 2026, was captioned: Fuji apples from Washington state at Save-On-Foods in Surrey on Monday. Statistics Canada's import classification has no line for Fuji at all. It sits inside a category called unspecified, which reached 120 million pounds in the 2024 crop year, the largest single line. Ontario's agriculture ministry says that line's 308 percent rise over ten years could be due to the rise of club cultivars. Sixteen online Fuji listings displayed no origin. On the evening of September 28, 2026, conventional Fuji ran from 2.16 a kilogram loose on special at Loblaws in Markham to 6.59 loose at a No Frills in Toronto. In fairness, Ontario's Fuji plantings are growing, and by November a new Canadian one exists. But the variety comes from Japan, early-October shelf fruit is not this year's Canadian crop, and none of the sixteen listings said where it was grown. Number four, the apple its owner calls the world's first branded apple. 229 acres in B.C., and Ontario does not count it separately.

Number four, Pink Lady. The pink one sold under a registered brand. The brand's own site says Pink Lady is a brand, not a variety. The original variety is Cripps Pink, bred in Western Australia; the state agriculture department's journal published an account by John Cripps and Eleanor Melvin-Carter in 1993. The site says the trademark is owned by Apple and Pear Australia Limited, managed globally by Twenty Degrees, and reserved for the exclusive use of licensees. It says the apple is sold in over 120 countries. For Canada, PVM, the U.S. company whose subsidiary is Pink Lady America, says that subsidiary administers licences for exports from the United States to Canada and Mexico. So the brand is Australian, and Canada is on the export list. Canada does grow the variety. B.C.'s insurance data list 229 acres, mostly in the Similkameen, and its production guide gives the harvest as late October to early November. So an early-October Pink Lady is not this year's Canadian crop. The Washington State Apple Commission markets Washington Pink Lady from November through July, and Chile's exporters forecast 116,000 tonnes of Pink Lady and Cripps Pink exports in 2026. Fifteen online listings displayed no origin. On the evening of September 28, 2026, conventional Pink Lady ran from 4.40 a kilogram in a five-pound bag at a Vancouver No Frills to 8.80 for a three-apple pack at Voilà. In fairness, nothing on the record suggests anyone has done anything wrong; a brand is allowed to license itself. But in early October, a Canadian buying a Pink Lady is buying an Australian-owned brand of a variety B.C. only starts picking in late October. Number three, the grandmother's apple. 112 acres in B.C., and Ontario does not count it separately.

Number three, Granny Smith. The green one. The City of Ryde, the Sydney council where she lived, keeps the record. Maria Ann Smith, born in Sussex in 1799, found a seedling by a creek on her Eastwood farm around 1868, grown from the remains of French crab apples from Tasmania. In 1895, twenty-five years after her death, the New South Wales Department of Agriculture's fruit expert named Granny Smith's Seedling as a suitable variety for export. It is a public variety, older than Canada's rights law. And Canada barely grows it. Ontario's growers do not list it as a variety; it appears only inside a footnote of other cultivars. B.C. has 112 acres and, in the province's latest production table, 1,741 tons, under four million pounds. Now the import line. Canada brought in 42.4 million pounds of Granny Smith in the 2024 crop year, the second most imported named variety after Gala, according to the Ontario report, 27 million pounds of it through Ontario ports. The Washington State Apple Commission markets Washington Granny Smith all twelve months, and Chile's exporters' association lists it among its top five export varieties. So on the published numbers, Canada imports more than ten times as much Granny Smith as B.C. grows, and Ontario does not publish its own figure. Twenty-nine online listings displayed no origin. On the evening of September 28, 2026, conventional Granny Smith ran from 4.41 a kilogram in a five-pound bag at Superstore in Calgary and Vancouver to 8.80 for a three-apple pack at Voilà. In fairness, none of that is the apple's fault. But a hundred and fifty-eight years after the seedling by the creek, the apple is not hers, and on the published numbers it is rarely ours. Now the two apples with no Canadian acres on any published list. Number two.

Number two, Envy. The one with the brand name on the sticker. We picked it over the other club apples for a reason: Jazz lost its Canadian rights in November 2025, and SweeTango is grown under licence in Nova Scotia. New Zealand's Plant and Food Research bred Envy. The Canadian register names Allan White as the breeder and says the controlled cross, Royal Gala by Braeburn, was made in 1985 in Hawke's Bay, and the seedling was selected in 1992. T&G Global dates its launch to 2008. Canada granted Plant Breeders' Rights on the variety, called Scilate, on November 16, 2018, certificate 5843, and they run until November 16, 2043, another seventeen years. T&G calls itself the exclusive global licence holder, and its own site names its majority shareholder as BayWa Global Produce. Is it grown here? No Canadian grower appears on the T&G, Envy or Canadian association pages we opened, and T&G does not list its growing countries. Loblaw told the Vancouver Sun in August 2026 that some varieties customers ask for, such as Cosmic Crisp, Envy and Jazz, aren't available from B.C. suppliers at all. In July 2025, Radio New Zealand, in a story republished by Farmers Weekly, reported that T&G's Envy and Jazz exported to Canada came in 2.27-kilogram bags with prominent product of USA labelling. It said the fruit sold here came from Washington state and the southern hemisphere. On the evening of September 28, 2026, Voilà listed a 908-gram bag of Envy at 7.70 a kilogram, and Real Canadian Superstore in Calgary and Vancouver listed it loose at 7.71. In fairness, the licence is the owner's to grant, and nothing on the record says otherwise. But the variety is New Zealand's, the licence is T&G's, the fruit on record in Canada came from Washington or the southern hemisphere, and none of it is grown here. The Canadian part is the Vancouver distributor, Oppy, and you.

Number one, Cosmic Crisp, with zero Canadian acres. The apple with the stars on its skin, and the single highest per-kilogram listing we captured. Washington State University bred it. Its breeder, Bruce Barritt, crossed Enterprise with Honeycrisp in 1997, according to the Canadian register, and the licensing company says the seeds germinated in 1998. On December 1, 2019, the university said the first boxes would reach U.S. grocery stores that month. Then the university did something no other breeder on this list did. It made the variety Washington-only. The licensing company's own page says Cosmic Crisp is exclusively available in Washington State for all of North America. The university's FAQ says Washington-grown fruit can be marketed anywhere in the world. Under that FAQ, Washington growers pay one U.S. dollar a tree plus 4.75 percent of every box that sells for twenty U.S. dollars or more. In April 2026, Capital Press reported that the university extended that Washington-only exclusivity from 2027 through May 26, 2032, the day its U.S. patent expires. University royalties topped eight million U.S. dollars in fiscal 2025, with 22 million trees in the ground in Washington. And here is the part for Canada. The Canadian Food Inspection Agency granted Washington State University Plant Breeders' Rights on the variety on September 8, 2022, certificate 6655, running to 2047. So the variety is protected in Canada, and no Canadian orchard is licensed to grow it. Every Cosmic Crisp on a Canadian shelf is a Washington import under the licence terms, until at least 2032. On the evening of September 28, 2026, it ran from 4.84 a kilogram in a five-pound bag at Superstore and No Frills in the West to 10.34 for a three-apple pack at Voilà. Eleven online listings; none displayed an origin. In fairness, the licence is the university's to set, and nothing improper is alleged. By the basis this video stated at the top, it ranks last: under the licence, no Canadian orchard grows it, and none can until at least 2032.

So where does that leave a Canadian who wants an apple from a Canadian tree this Thanksgiving? Three varieties that started in Canada, in order of how much of them Ontario and B.C. grow, least to most. We have no commercial relationship with any grower, packer, marketer or licensor named in this video, and none of them knew it was being made.

First, Spartan, 373 acres in Ontario and B.C. It was bred at the federal research station in Summerland, B.C. R.C. Palmer made the cross in 1926, the tree first fruited in 1932, and it was named in 1936, according to the station's own scientists writing in 1999. Its pedigree is McIntosh by unknown. It was never patented, and it has no Plant Breeders' Rights record in Canada. B.C. insures 183 acres and Ontario has 190, and Quebec lists it among its four traditional varieties. Ontario's growers list it from October, and Quebec's growers set its 2026 selling start at September 23. On the evening of September 28, 2026, Voilà listed a 1.81-kilogram bag at 3.86 a kilogram, and Superstore, No Frills and Loblaws listed bags at 4.40 to 4.42. To be fair, it is small in the two provinces that publish acreage. But a Canadian scientist, a Canadian research station, a Canadian apple, and no rights holder collecting on it.

Second, McIntosh, 2,268 acres in Ontario and B.C., and the U.S. agriculture department's Ottawa post says Quebec has more than either. Parks Canada's record: in 1811, John McIntosh found an apple sapling on his land in Matilda township, Dundela, Ontario, and the family's propagation of it became a National Historic Event, designated October 28, 1999. Parks Canada names it a parent of Cortland, Empire, Lobo and Spartan. Never patented. Quebec's government says McIntosh is more than a third of the province's eating-apple production. Ontario has 1,943 acres. Canada imported zero pounds of McIntosh in the 2024 crop year. On the evening of September 28, 2026, a No Frills in Toronto had a four-pound bag at 2.76 a kilogram on special, and Loblaws in Markham had one at 2.21 on special. To be fair, Ontario's acreage fell from 2,556 acres in 2019 to 1,943 in 2024, and the U.S. post calls it a variety no longer popular for fresh eating. But it is 215 years old, found here, not imported at all in the 2024 crop year, and on special this week among the cheapest bags in the aisle.

Third, Ambrosia, 4,014 acres, B.C.'s largest variety, and on origin and ownership the most Canadian apple on this list. The B.C. government's Buy BC page: the first Ambrosia apple was discovered by chance in Cawston, B.C., and the mother tree is still alive. The CFIA register names Wilfrid and Sally Mennell of Cawston as the breeders. They applied for Canadian Plant Breeders' Rights on March 21, 1994, certificate 0388 was granted on August 20, 1997, and it expired on August 20, 2015. The U.S. patent expired in June 2017. After that certificate ran out, Good Fruit Grower reported that Ambrosia would be an open variety in Canada by 2019. The name remains a trademark used under licence. B.C. insures 2,274 acres, first in the province, and Ontario has 1,740, up from 1,260 in 2019. Summerland Varieties, which manages the name, gives the B.C. harvest as early to mid-October. It has not been an easy road. In July 2024 the BC Tree Fruits Cooperative, which had packed fruit for about half of the Okanagan's growers, stopped taking fruit weeks before harvest. It entered creditor protection on August 13, 2024, court file S-245481. Its Oliver packing house reopened in 2025 under Wildstone of Penticton and its partner, Algoma Orchards of Ontario. On the evening of September 28, 2026, Superstore in Vancouver listed a five-pound bag at 5.98 on a limit-two special, 2.63 a kilogram. A separate four-pound bag at 5.51 a kilogram was described as hand-picked and grown in Quebec. In February this channel put Ambrosia on the avoid list on a nutrition basis. On origin and ownership, it is the most Canadian apple on this list. Found by a family, protected by a Canadian certificate, and grown on more Ontario acres every year.

How to protect yourself. First, the sticker and the sign. The number on the sticker identifies the variety, and a leading nine marks it as organic, according to the body that assigns the codes. Country is not part of it. For imported apples in a bag or tray, federal regulation requires product of, produce of, grown in, or country of origin, followed by the country, on the main label panel, unless the store bagged them itself. For loose apples, read the bin sign, and if there is no origin on it, ask. Second, the calendar. Ontario's growers list McIntosh and Gala from September, Honeycrisp from late September, Ambrosia, Spartan and Red Delicious from October, and Fuji from mid to late October. B.C. picks Pink Lady from late October. If an apple is on the shelf before those months, it may be last season's storage fruit, another province's or another country's, so check the sign. Third, the price per kilo. Statistics Canada's average retail price for apples, table 18-10-0245-01, was 4.40 a kilogram in October 2025 and 6.45 in July 2026, so October's average ran about a third below July's. In that series, October was the lowest month of both 2024 and 2025. Fourth, buy the apples that started in Canada, in season: Spartan, McIntosh and Ambrosia. Cortland and Empire, bred at Cornell's station in Geneva, New York, and grown in Ontario and Quebec, were four-pound bags at about 4.41 a kilogram at Loblaws in Markham this week.

Eight apples with something in common. Not one of them started in Canada, one of them is licensed only to Washington growers, and on the two Canada grows most, Gala and Honeycrisp, only the origin line tells you whether it is ours. The apple does not know where it was grown. The sign does. Thank you for watching.
