Thirty-two packages of meat. Seven grocery stores. Every single package weighed less than the label said it did. That's not a rumour and it's not a lawsuit somebody filed hoping for a payday. That's what CBC News found in April of 2026 when it walked into seventeen Canadian grocery stores in Toronto, Vancouver and Halifax with a kitchen scale. And here's the part that should make you angry: they'd already done it once. Fifteen months earlier they caught four major chains doing the same thing, the companies apologized, promised to fix it — and then did it again. Tonight we're going through the beef counter at Loblaws, Real Canadian Superstore, No Frills, Sobeys, FreshCo, Safeway, Costco, Metro, Walmart and Save-On-Foods, in order of how many Canadians actually shop there. Everything here is built on CBC News investigations, the Canadian Food Inspection Agency's own food fraud reporting, federal recall records, Statistics Canada price data, and the ingredient lists these companies publish themselves. Nine things failed. Four at the end are genuinely worth your money. And before you reach for the next package of ground beef, ask yourself one question: do you actually know what the words on that label mean? Because most of them mean nothing at all. Let's start.

Number one, the Loblaw group. Loblaws, Real Canadian Superstore, No Frills, Zehrs, Provigo, Maxi, Fortinos, Atlantic Superstore, T&T. If you're Canadian, you shop here. As the industry itself told the Competition Bureau, Loblaw holds roughly twenty-nine percent of the entire Canadian grocery market, and the company says more than two thousand four hundred stores put ninety percent of Canadians within ten kilometres of one of its locations. Now here's what happened at the meat counter. In Halifax, CBC bought six beef briskets from two Real Canadian Superstores. All six were underweight. Combined overcharge, about four dollars, roughly four point seven percent. But the brisket story doesn't start there. A Halifax-area customer named Karen Webber bought four beef briskets in February of 2025, weighed them, found every one short, and took it to the store manager. He gave her a free brisket and told her it would be corrected. One year later, she bought four more briskets from that same store. All four were underweight again. Her words: "I was pissed. They were obviously just paying lip service to me." And this isn't isolated to Nova Scotia. A Real Canadian Superstore in British Columbia was caught selling underweight beef strip loin and received a warning — no fine — despite the chain already having been under CFIA scrutiny in early 2024 over underweight ground beef across Western Canada. That earlier episode ran across eighty stores until December of 2023. Loblaw called it an operational error from a packaging change. The company's line in April 2026 was, quote, "We are truly sorry this happened. We take weight accuracy seriously." Then there's the beef itself. Loblaw's flagship premium beef line is President's Choice Free From, and the retailer's own product listings for the Free From stewing beef, blade roast and rib steak describe it as grass fed Australian Angus beef. Australian. Canada's largest grocer, and its premium clean-label beef program is imported from the other side of the planet. And the phrase "Free From" itself? In January of 2026, no name brand Beef Burgers were pulled in a Class 1 national recall for possible E. coli O157:H7. Loblaw promised you Canada's grocer and a premium beef line called Free From. Number six, Save-On-Foods and the Pattison Food Group — a hundred and eighty stores across British Columbia, Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba and the Yukon, and if you live out west this is your store. Save-On sells Western Family Canadian Angus Beef Square Burgers. Canadian Angus, right in the product name. And in the product information for that Western Family burger line, you'll find the phrase "made in Canada from domestic and imported ingredients." That phrase is not an accident and not a typo — it is a legally mandated qualifier. Under CFIA rules, if you want to say "Made in Canada," you must add a qualifier telling people whether the ingredients are domestic, imported, or both. It's required precisely because "Made in Canada" alone would let you imply something that isn't true. So the burger has Canadian Angus on the front in large friendly letters, and the disclosure that the beef may not be Canadian in the fine print, exactly where the regulation puts it. Save-On-Foods promised you Canadian Angus. What you got was a product whose own required disclosure says the ingredients may be imported.

Number seven isn't a store, it's the thing sitting behind all of them. Ask yourself where the beef in every one of these chains actually comes from. Cargill's plant in High River, Alberta processes roughly four thousand five hundred head a day — on its own, around thirty-six percent of Canada's entire beef processing capacity. JBS Canada in Brooks, Alberta runs about four thousand two hundred a day. Between them, those two plants account for the large majority of federally inspected beef slaughter in this country; estimates run from roughly seventy percent upward depending on who's counting and what year. Cargill is American. JBS is Brazilian. And the JBS plant at Brooks has a history — it's the former XL Foods Establishment 38, site of the largest beef recall in Canadian history. September 2012. Eighteen people made ill by E. coli O157:H7. Around eighteen hundred products pulled in Canada and the United States. More than one and a half million pounds of beef recalled inside twelve days. The independent panel that reviewed it found XL Foods had a lax approach to food safety. The point isn't that Cargill or JBS is doing something wrong today. The point is that the logo on the package — Compliments, President's Choice, Western Family, Your Fresh Market — is a marketing decision made after the fact. For most Canadian beef, the animal went through one of two buildings in Alberta no matter which store you drove to. Your choice of grocery store promised you a choice of beef. What you got was the same two plants with different stickers on the outside.

Number eight isn't a store either. It's the word "burger." In Canada there's a legal standard called a meat patty, and to use it your product needs a minimum of fifteen percent meat product protein and sixteen percent total protein. But there's a second category — a meat patty with filler. Declare filler, and that legal floor drops to nine and a half percent meat product protein and eleven percent total protein when uncooked. Roughly a third less real meat protein, completely legal. And how does Canadian law define filler? Milk, egg, yeast or vegetable material, or any derivative or combination of them, that is edible and that is not visibly distinguishable after it is added. Read that again — not visibly distinguishable. The legal definition of filler in this country includes the requirement that you can't see it. That's why toasted wheat crumb and soy protein concentrate are perfectly legal inside something sold to you as an Angus Beef Burger. Nobody is breaking a rule. The rule was written with the door open. And it goes further than burgers. Under the Food and Drug Regulations, solid cut meat with added water or additives only has to hit ten percent meat protein raw, twelve percent cooked. Add phosphate salts, with or without water, and you're allowed to call the product "seasoned." Add only water, and the name has to include "water added." So when you see "seasoned" on beef, that's often not a chef's decision about flavour — it's a regulatory term telling you something was pumped into the meat. The word "burger" promised you beef. What the law promised you was nine and a half percent protein and a filler you're not supposed to be able to see.

And now the worst one on this list, because here the government's own paperwork does the damage. Number nine: the words "Product of Canada" on a package of beef. You see the maple leaf, you pay a premium, you feel good, you're supporting Canadian ranchers. Here is the Canadian Food Inspection Agency's own published guidance, in its own words. Product of Canada claims can be applied to meat from Canadian animals slaughtered in Canada. Animals are considered Canadian if they are born or hatched, raised and slaughtered in Canada — or, in the case of feeder cattle, if they have spent a period of at least sixty days in Canada prior to slaughter in Canada. Sixty days. A calf can be born in the United States, raised in the United States, trucked across the border, spend two months here, be slaughtered here, and that beef is legally Product of Canada. The exact same sixty-day rule governs Canada Beef's own Canadian Beef logo licence. It's not a loophole somebody found. It's the published standard. Meanwhile, the CFIA's own Food Fraud Annual Report for 2024 to 2025 tested sixty-six domestically-produced Canadian-origin meat samples and found sixty compliant — meaning roughly one in eleven samples claiming Canadian origin didn't hold up. That same report tested ninety-five meat samples for species substitution and six failed. And the agency noted meat was the commodity with the highest number of complaints that year, driven by two things: underweight products, and misleading or missing origin claims. That's not an activist group. That's the federal regulator, reporting on itself. Product of Canada promised you a Canadian animal on a Canadian farm. What you got was a rule that says sixty days is enough.

So who's actually enforcing any of this? Here's why it keeps happening. Three different regulators touch the meat scale. Measurement Canada certifies the scale itself under the Weights and Measures Act, on roughly a five-year cycle. The CFIA enforces the net quantity declaration on the label, and that rule is simple — net weight does not include packaging material. The Competition Bureau handles misleading representations. And the gap sits right in the middle of all three. A scale can be perfectly calibrated and certified and still print an overstated weight, because the number typed into it for the weight of the tray — the tare — is a product-specific entry a five-year device inspection does not check. The scale is accurate. The figure fed into it is nobody's job. And the enforcement numbers tell you how much pressure is behind that. In fiscal 2023 to 2024, the CFIA conducted six meat weight spot checks. Six, in a country with thousands of grocery stores. After CBC's first story, that jumped to two hundred and thirty-six since January 2025. The result of all two hundred and thirty-six? Warnings to seven retailers. Zero fines. And the maximum fine available is fifteen thousand dollars — against companies reporting between twenty-two and sixty-four billion dollars in annual revenue. Terri Lee spent twenty-four years as a CFIA inspector before retiring in 2021. On what a real deterrent looks like: "It needs to be enough so that it deters them — half a million dollars." On the stores: "Obviously, these retail stores are not to be trusted that the weight on the package is accurate." One piece of good news — the CFIA says the federal government is reviewing those penalty limits, with an outcome expected in the 2026 federal budget.

And all of this is happening while beef costs more than it ever has. Statistics Canada has ground beef at sixteen dollars and sixty-one cents a kilogram in June of 2026 — the highest figure in a series going back to 2017, when it was nine dollars and twelve cents. An eighty-two percent increase. Striploin peaked at forty-two forty-two a kilogram in April 2026. Every major beef cut Statistics Canada tracks hit its all-time high within the last nine months. And the reason isn't the grocery stores, it's genuinely supply — Canada's herd shrank for years under drought and the American herd is at a multi-decade low. Statistics Canada did record eleven point nine million cattle and calves on Canadian farms as of July 2025, the first year-over-year increase since 2021, but rebuilding a herd means holding cows back from slaughter, which keeps supply tight longer, not shorter. Prices are not coming down soon. Which is exactly why being short-weighted four point seven percent on a brisket matters more now than it did five years ago.

So what actually works? Four things, and these are real.

First, the single most useful habit in this video: weigh it yourself. Karen Webber's last line to CBC was, "I'm going to keep weighing everything," and she's right. You don't need a special device. Put the package on a kitchen scale at home, in its packaging. If the scale reads at or below the number printed on the label, you've been charged for the tray — because that reading includes the plastic, the pad and the film, and it still didn't reach the stated weight of the meat alone. That's the exact method CBC used to find thirty-two of them. Photograph it, take it to customer service, and file a complaint with the CFIA. Seven retailers got warnings because people did this.

Second, learn the four words that actually mean something. Almost every claim on a beef package is unregulated. "Grass fed" has no Canadian legal standard at all — the Canadian Cattle Association's own factsheet says outright that the CFIA does not have any regulation surrounding grass-fed beef. "Free From" isn't regulated. "Angus" has no genetic minimum in Canadian law, and Certified Angus Beef's live-animal screen is a predominantly black hide — a colour test, not a genetics test. But Extra Lean, Lean, Medium and Regular are different. Those are compositional standards with hard legal fat maximums: ten, seventeen, twenty-three, thirty percent. They're the only words on that package a company can actually be held to.

Third, look for the grade — and notice when it isn't there. Canada Prime, AAA, AA and A are graded by the Canadian Beef Grading Agency, and here's what almost nobody knows: the only difference between those four grades is marbling. Trace, slight, small, slightly abundant. Every other criterion is identical. And grading in Canada is voluntary. Virtually all fed beef gets graded, but cows and bulls frequently don't, and selling ungraded beef with no grade on the label is completely legal. A package with no grade stated is telling you something by staying silent. Costco earns real credit here — it runs an explicit Canada AAA program and brands the grade on the case. Walmart Canada's published claim of sourcing one hundred percent Canadian beef is also real and worth rewarding.

And fourth — the one that saves you the most money, precisely because it's the opposite of the advice you've been given. You've seen the videos telling you to buy a quarter cow and save fifty percent. I ran the numbers against a real Ontario farm's published pricing, and at today's Canadian prices it does not work. Farms quote you on hanging weight, and you take home only sixty to sixty-five percent of that as packaged meat. Run it through and a whole animal lands around fourteen to sixteen dollars a pound in your freezer. Statistics Canada has retail ground beef at seven fifty-three a pound and top sirloin at twelve sixty-seven — and a whole carcass is mostly ground and stewing-grade meat, not striploin. So buying direct gets you better beef, more of it, and a relationship with the person who raised it. All worth having. It does not get you cheaper beef, and anyone telling you it does is quoting hanging weight at you. If your goal is saving money, buy whole cuts on sale and grind them yourself. There's a food safety reason too: on a whole steak or roast, bacteria sit on the surface where heat kills them. Grinding takes that surface and distributes it all the way through — which is why ground beef has to be cooked further, and why one contaminated carcass can contaminate an entire batch. Grind it at home and only one animal's surface is in your bowl.

So here's where we land. The pattern never changed. The claim on the front was unregulated, and the disclosure that mattered was in the fine print, or in a government guidance document nobody reads, or it was the weight itself — and the weight was wrong. Sixty days makes American cattle Canadian. Nine and a half percent protein makes a burger. A thing you can't see is legally called filler. And two hundred and thirty-six federal inspections produced seven warnings and not one fine. The four things that work are the four nobody markets to you: a kitchen scale, four legally-defined words, a grade printed on the label, and a whole cut you break down yourself. Or just remember the one rule that carries all of them. Check the number, not the story. The maple leaf on that package is a picture. The grade is a standard. The weight is a fact — and it's the only thing on that label you can test yourself, in your own kitchen, in about four seconds. Karen Webber weighed hers and caught the same store twice in a year. So the next time a package of beef tells you it's Canadian, premium, Angus, grass fed and free from, put it on the scale before you decide what any of that was worth. Because if this is what's hiding in plain sight at the meat counter, imagine what's sitting in the aisle you walk past every single week without a second thought.
