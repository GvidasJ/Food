I'm holding two bags of bread. This one is Wonder Bread — the most American-sounding bread ever made. The balloons, the name, the whole thing was invented in Indianapolis in 1921. And this one is Dempster's — the company that calls itself, on its own website, the number one bread brand in Canada. Twelve bakeries from British Columbia to Newfoundland. The bread your mother bought. Now — one of these two brands is owned by a family company headquartered in Toronto. The other one answers to Mexico City. And I promise you, it's not the way around you think. The all-American loaf is the Canadian one. Canada's number one bread brand is not.

And that's only the second-strangest thing about this aisle. Because this is also the aisle where, for about fourteen years, the price of a loaf didn't just drift up — it marched up, on a schedule, and in 2023 a Canadian court handed down the largest price-fixing fine in this country's history over it. Fifty million dollars. If you got a cheque for forty-nine dollars and eleven cents in the mail this past spring and weren't sure why — this video is why. And if you remember a twenty-five dollar grocery gift card back in 2018 — same story.

So today we're doing what we always do. We read every bag in the Canadian bread aisle. We looked up who actually owns every brand. We read the law those labels have to follow — and bread, it turns out, has some of the oldest and strangest law in the entire Canadian food system, including one rule still on the books from 1942, still written in pounds. We're going to go brand by brand, from the most popular down, and by the end you'll know which three of these nine brands are actually Canadian-owned, why the biggest words on the bag — "whole wheat" — don't mean what almost everyone thinks they mean, and which loaf we'd actually put in the cart. As always: no scare stories, no chemistry lecture. We read labels. That's all we do.

First, the ownership map, because you cannot understand this aisle without it. For most of living memory, Canadian packaged bread was a two-family story. On one side: Canada Bread, founded in Toronto in 1911, which grew into the giant behind Dempster's, Villaggio, POM, Bon Matin and Ben's. On the other: the Weston empire — George Weston started with a Toronto bread route in 1882 — baking Wonder, D'Italiano, Country Harvest and Gadoua, right alongside owning Loblaws. Then, in the space of eight years, both sides sold. In 2014, Maple Leaf Foods — yes, the hot dog company; they owned about ninety percent of Canada Bread — sold it to Grupo Bimbo of Mexico City for one point eight three billion dollars. If you watched our hot dog episode, this should feel familiar: the same company that owned the wiener aisle owned the bread under the wiener too. And in December 2021, George Weston sold its entire fresh bakery business — Wonder, D'Italiano, Country Harvest, ACE Bakery, Gadoua, the whole portfolio — for about one point two billion dollars, to a company called FGF Brands. Never heard of FGF? It's a Toronto company, founded in 2004 by Sam Ajmera and his sons — a family that started in this country with one bakery business after Sam emigrated from Mumbai in 1974. Their bread division is called Wonderbrands, and their website says something no other big player in this aisle can say. We'll get there. If you're already reaching for the bread bag in your kitchen to check it — hit the like button first, and subscribe if label-reading is your kind of television. It genuinely helps more Canadians find these videos. Now, the brands — most popular first.

Brand one: Dempster's. By the numbers we could find — a federal agriculture report using 2024 market data — Dempster's is the single biggest bread brand in the country, at around eight hundred and forty million dollars a year, and the brand's own site claims the number one spot outright. That part, nobody seriously disputes. Here's what the bag doesn't say: since May 2014, Dempster's has been a brand of Bimbo Canada, which is Grupo Bimbo — the largest baking company on Earth, headquartered in Mexico City. The old canadabread.com website doesn't even exist anymore; type it in and it redirects you to bimbocanada.com. Watch us do it. Now, credit where it's due, because we always give it: flip over Dempster's 100% Whole Grains Whole Wheat and read the very first ingredient. "Whole grain whole wheat flour including the germ." Hold onto those words — "including the germ" — because in about four minutes that phrase is going to become the most important thing in this entire video. At the time of filming, that loaf was $3.99 on sale at No Frills — and its regular price at some banners was over five dollars. The bread your mother bought for one dollar and forty-three cents in 2001. We'll explain exactly what happened to that price. Patience.

Brand two — and collectively it might really be brand one: the store brands. No Name. President's Choice. Great Value. Compliments. Together, private label is over twenty percent of every baked-goods dollar in Canada — no single store brand outsells Dempster's, but as a bloc they're enormous. And here's the bread-aisle version of a question we ask in every episode: who actually bakes them? The bag won't tell you. No Name bread says "Prepared for Loblaws Inc." — prepared by whom, it doesn't say. But this time, we can actually prove part of the answer, and the proof comes from the least likely place: a recall notice. In November 2024, one contaminated shipment of salt triggered a recall, and the official Canadian recall notice lists the recalling company as Wonder Brands Inc. — for bread sold under Country Harvest, D'Italiano, Great Value, no name, President's Choice AND Wonder. One baker, seven bags. The same company bakes the brand-name loaf and the yellow no-name loaf, and the only public document that says so is a food recall. And one more thing about that recall, because it's genuinely reassuring: the system traced a single bad salt shipment through the entire supply chain and pulled roughly thirty-seven products off shelves before anyone reported an injury. That's the machinery working. Oh — and the history footnote that explains everything: until December 2021, the company that owned Loblaws and the company that baked Loblaws' bread were the same family's empire. George Weston Limited owned the store AND the bakery. Remember that for the scandal section. It matters.

Brand three: Wonder. The white bread. The balloons on the bag come from a real event — in 1921 an executive in Indianapolis watched the International Balloon Race and was filled with, his word, "wonder." South of the border, the ads promised it "helps build strong bodies 12 ways" — that's a historical advertising slogan, not a fact, and we quote it strictly as ad history. Wonder came to Canada in the twenties and spent decades being baked here by Weston. And now — the reveal we promised. Go to wonderbread.ca today. The banner across the top says, in giant letters, quote: "Canadian Baked And Owned." And it's true. Since the Weston sale closed in December 2021, Wonder in Canada belongs to Wonderbrands — FGF, the Ajmera family, Toronto. Meanwhile Wonder in the United States belongs to Flowers Foods of Georgia, bought out of the Hostess bankruptcy in 2013. Two Wonders, two owners, two countries — and it's the CANADIAN one that's family-owned. The most American-looking bag in the store is one of the only truly Canadian-owned big brands in the aisle. Only in Canada, you say?

Now flip the Wonder bag over, because this is where the biggest label lesson in the episode lives. Wonder's 100% Whole Wheat lists: "Whole wheat flour, Water, Sugar, Wheat gluten, Yeast, Vegetable oil, Salt, Wheat germ…" Did you catch it? Whole wheat flour… and then wheat germ, added back in separately. Why would you need to ADD germ to whole wheat? Because — and this is Health Canada's own explanation, on their own website, and it shocked us — in Canada, quote: "Up to 5% of the kernel can be removed to help reduce rancidity and prolong the shelf life of whole wheat flour." And the part that's removed, quote, "contains much of the germ and some of the bran." Which leads to the sentence I never expected to read on a government website, quote: "100% whole wheat bread may not be whole grain." That is Health Canada, word for word — and to be fair to them, the same page adds that whole wheat bread, quote, "remains a nutritious choice." We're not here to tell you it's good or bad — we're here to tell you what the words legally mean. "Whole wheat" flour in Canada can be missing much of the germ. If you want flour with the entire kernel, the label has to say "whole grain whole wheat flour." Now you understand Dempster's bag: "whole grain whole wheat flour INCLUDING THE GERM." Now you understand Wonder's bag: whole wheat flour, germ added back. Two different legal phrases, two different breads, one word — "whole wheat" — doing all the marketing. That's the decode. You now read bread bags better than almost everyone in the store.

Brand four: Villaggio. The Tuscan-sounding one — the sunflowers, the Italian flag colours, "Italian style" on every bag. Read the fine print: Villaggio is a Bimbo Canada brand. Same parent as Dempster's. The romantic little Italian village is a corporate sibling of the biggest brand in the aisle, owned from Mexico City. And Villaggio hosts our second label lesson: the multigrain trick. Villaggio's Rustico Harvest Multigrain — first ingredient, at the time of research: "Enriched wheat flour." That's white flour. The rye and the seeds are further down the list. "Multigrain" is not a legal promise of whole grain — it just means more than one grain showed up. And while we're in the percentages: you'll see loaves in Quebec and elsewhere literally named "60% whole wheat." That number is real law — the Canadian food standards say a bread named "(percentage) whole wheat bread" must contain at least sixty percent whole wheat flour of the total flour. The number on the front is the whole-wheat share. The other forty percent? White flour. The bag is telling you, if you know the code.

Brands five and six, together, because they share an owner — the Canadian one: D'Italiano and Country Harvest, both Wonderbrands, both FGF, both Toronto-owned. D'Italiano is the thick-slice attitude brand — their actual tagline is "Do it big" — and its Original White reads like Wonder's cousin: enriched wheat flour, water, sugar, yeast and the standard supporting cast. Country Harvest is the seed-covered one, and its 14 Grains loaf leads with "whole grain wheat flour" — with the germ and bran spelled out right there in brackets — followed by a genuinely impressive parade: flax, oats, sunflower, barley, rye, quinoa, teff, buckwheat. If you're keeping score at home, that's the whole-grain phrasing done right, on a Canadian-owned loaf. So there are your three actually-Canadian brands from the big shelf: Wonder, D'Italiano, Country Harvest — all one Toronto family company that most Canadians have never heard of.

And the regional royalty, quickly, because if we skip them the comment section will find us. Quebec: POM — the half-size 380-gram loaf is a genuinely Québécois format, and Montrealers will tell you POM stands for "Pride of Montreal"; we'll just say the brand's own history is mysterious enough that La Presse wrote a whole article about the name. POM is Bimbo Canada now — and its Ultra-Soft white lists "sugar / glucose-fructose," our old friend, Canada's legal name for high-fructose corn syrup. Also Quebec: Bon Matin — Bimbo — and Gadoua, a name with roots the company traces back to 1873, which went to Weston in 2004 and now belongs to FGF. The Maritimes: Ben's, founded in Halifax in 1907 by Ben Moir of the chocolate family, the biggest-selling bread in Nova Scotia by 1939. Canada Bread bought it in 2002, and in 2015 closed the Halifax bakery — a hundred and five jobs — moving production to New Brunswick. The bag on the Atlantic shelf still says Ben's. The bakery on Pepperell Street is gone. We've seen this movie in every episode, haven't we.

Which brings us to the part of this video we've been building to. The scandal. Let's be extremely precise, because real companies, real courtrooms, and real denials are involved — and precision is the whole brand of this channel. Here is what is admitted and proven. Between 2001 and 2015, there was — in the words of Loblaw and George Weston themselves — "an industry-wide price fixing arrangement" on packaged bread. They discovered it inside their own bakery division in March 2015, reported themselves to the Competition Bureau, and received immunity in exchange. According to the Bureau's court filings, the price of bread was raised again and again — at least fifteen times — in a coordinated pattern investigators described as roughly seven cents wholesale, ten cents retail a time. The Bureau's documents, as reported across Canadian media, estimated at least a dollar fifty was added to the price of a loaf. And you don't have to take anyone's word for it, because Statistics Canada was writing it all down in real time. The average 675-gram loaf: one dollar forty-three in December 2001. Two ninety-seven by December 2015. Up one hundred and eight percent, in a period when overall grocery prices rose about forty-five. And here's the quiet part said out loud: after the scandal broke in December 2017, the average price of bread actually went DOWN.

What happened next reads like three receipts, each bigger than the last. Receipt one: December 2017, Loblaw apologizes — Galen Weston himself: "This sort of behaviour is wrong and has no place in our business or Canada's grocery industry" — and offers every adult Canadian a twenty-five dollar gift card. Receipt two: June 21, 2023, Canada Bread — the Dempster's company — pleads guilty to four counts of price-fixing and is fined fifty million dollars. The Competition Bureau's own words: "The fine is the highest price-fixing fine imposed by a Canadian court to date." And the Bureau notes something crucial for our story: "At the time of the price-fixing, Canada Bread was under the ownership of Maple Leaf Foods." The hot dog company again. Grupo Bimbo, who bought Canada Bread in 2014, says it knew nothing — and is now suing Maple Leaf for more than two billion dollars over the purchase, while Maple Leaf denies everything and is suing them back for defamation. The two companies that ran your bread aisle are now feeding each other to the lawyers. Receipt three: July 2024, Loblaw and George Weston settle the class actions for five hundred million dollars — reported as the largest settlement of its kind in Canadian history — with another direct apology: "On behalf of the Weston group of companies, we are sorry." More than twenty million Canadians were in the class. Claims closed in December 2025, and this past May the money went out: forty-nine dollars and eleven cents each — twenty-four eleven if you'd taken the gift card back in 2018. One analysis for Maclean's put a family's overpayment across those years at roughly four hundred dollars. Measure that against your cheque.

And the fairness paragraph, which we will read carefully, because it matters. Only one company has been convicted: Canada Bread, which pleaded guilty. Loblaw and George Weston admitted their part, self-reported, and received immunity. The Competition Bureau said, as recently as June 2026, that it — quote — "continues to investigate alleged price-fixing by other companies, including Metro Inc., Sobeys Inc., Wal-Mart Canada Corporation, Giant Tiger Stores Limited, and Maple Leaf Foods Inc." Every one of those companies denies wrongdoing. None of them has been charged. Sobeys called the industry-wide characterization "unfair, unsubstantiated, and quite possibly defamatory." Metro says it found no evidence it broke the law and is counter-suing over its reputation. That's the record as of the day we filmed this. Nine years after the raids, the file is still open. Now — some law and history to wash that down, because Canadian bread law is genuinely wonderful. There IS a legal definition of bread, and until December 2024 it lived in the Food and Drug Regulations; then it moved — same words, new address — into the federal compositional standards, which is becoming a running theme on this channel. It says bread "is the food made by baking a yeast-leavened dough prepared with flour and water" — and then lists exactly what may be added. Some gems from the surrounding standards, all real: "milk bread" is a legally defined name — your bread can only claim it with enough milk solids. Raisin bread must contain raisins equal to at least fifty percent of the weight of the flour — one of the strictest recipe laws in the country; genuinely, the law will not let raisin bread cheat you on raisins. "Brown bread," meanwhile, is legally just bread coloured by whole wheat, graham flour, bran — or molasses. Brown can literally mean dyed with molasses. And the word "enriched" on your white bread? Not a bonus. It's the law. The CFIA states it plainly: "The sale of unenriched white flour or foods containing unenriched white flour is not permitted in Canada." Every white loaf in the country contains added thiamine, riboflavin, niacin, folic acid and iron because the regulations require it, down to the milligram — folic acid joined the list in 1998. When the bag brags "enriched," it's bragging about complying.

The history behind that rule is peak Canada. During the Second World War, the government wanted more vitamins in the nation's bread, but adding synthetic vitamins was legally considered adulteration at the time. So in 1942 they invented "Canada Approved Vitamin B" flour — milled the long way to keep the natural vitamins in. And here's our favourite fact of the episode: that 1942 wartime standard is still in Canadian food law today. Volume 12, section 12.1.2 — still called "Canada Approved," and still written in pounds. A fossil from the ration years, sitting in the 2026 rulebook. While we're back there: the wheat itself is Canada's quiet triumph — Marquis wheat, finished in Ottawa in 1904 by Sir Charles Saunders, who tested his crosses by chewing kernels into dough at his desk. Marquis ripened ten days earlier and opened the Prairies; by 1918 it covered twenty million acres, and Canada remains one of the largest wheat exporters on the planet — the best bread wheat in the world, grown here, milled everywhere. Canadian wheat feeds the world. The Canadian bread aisle, meanwhile, mostly answers to Mexico City and one quiet family office in Toronto. Two more quickies: sliced bread was invented in 1928 by a jeweller from Iowa — and in 1943 the United States briefly BANNED sliced bread as a wartime measure; it lasted forty-seven days before the public revolted. And that thing your aunt told you about twist-tie colours secretly telling you the baking day? Half true at best — some bakeries do colour-code ties for their own restocking, but there is no standard system and nothing in Canadian law about it. The printed best-before date is the only code that counts, and even that, legally, is a freshness promise, not a safety line.

So — who wins the bread aisle? Here's where we landed. If ownership is your compass, the honest surprise of this episode is that the "actually Canadian" answer is hiding behind the most American logo in the store: Wonder, D'Italiano and Country Harvest are the Toronto-owned trio, and Country Harvest's 14 Grains is the one of the three whose label leads with true whole-grain flour, spelled out germ and all. If the label itself is your compass, Dempster's Whole Grains earns its flowers despite the Mexico City address — "whole grain whole wheat flour including the germ" is exactly the phrase Health Canada tells you to look for, and it's printed in the first position where it belongs. And if you want the full independence package — Canadian, family-owned, never sold to anyone — our winner lives out west: Silver Hills Bakery of Abbotsford, British Columbia. Family-owned and family-run since 1989, baking sprouted-grain breads in BC, with "Made in Canada" on the bag and a whole-grain count printed right on the front. You'll pay more per loaf and you'll find it in the fancy corner of the aisle — but it's the one bag in this video where the flag, the family and the factory all match. One caution flag while you're over there: that rustic "Stonemill Bakehouse" loaf with the artisan look? Bimbo Canada since 2017. And "Dave's Killer Bread"? American brand — baked in Canada under license by, of all people, the Wonder folks. The premium shelf plays the same ownership games as the cheap one. Flip every bag.

Your five-second bread-aisle routine from now on: find the ownership fine print. Find the first ingredient — "whole grain whole wheat flour" beats "whole wheat flour," and now you know why. Treat "multigrain" as decoration until the list proves otherwise. Check the price per hundred grams, because the loaves have quietly shrunk to 570 and 600 grams while your memory still says 675. And when the price of a staple rises like clockwork for a decade, remember: the last time that happened in this aisle, it ended in the largest price-fixing fine in Canadian history and a fifty-dollar cheque seventeen years late.
