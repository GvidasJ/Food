TITLE: We Tested Burgers From McDonald's, A&W, Wendy's & 5 More Canadian Chains | These FAILED

We set three tests before looking at a single burger, and we are judging every chain in Canada by them tonight. Test one: what is actually in the patty — not what the commercial says, what the company's own documents say. Test two: does the story match the sourcing — if the front counter says Canadian, hormone-free, fresh, is that claim finished or is it marketing on a journey? Test three: does the price match the tray — because Canadian ground beef is up twenty-five percent in a single year, restaurant prices have outrun inflation two years running, and three-quarters of Canadians now say they are eating out less because of it. And before the first chain, one fact that frames this entire video, because almost nobody knows it: in Canada, restaurant food is exempt from the labelling laws that govern everything at the grocery store. No ingredient list is required. No nutrition facts table is required. The Canadian Food Inspection Agency's own guidance says food sold in restaurants is generally not prepackaged and therefore exempt. The only law of its kind is Ontario's — since 2017, chains with twenty or more locations there must post calories, and only calories, and only in Ontario. Everything else you have ever read about what is in a chain burger — every nutrition PDF, every sourcing page, every proud claim — is voluntary. The chains that tell you things are choosing to. The chains that don't are choosing that too. Which means tonight's real test is the simplest one there is: who shows you what they are, and who hopes you never ask. And the stakes are not abstract in this country. Canadian beef processing is among the most concentrated food systems we have — two plants, Cargill's at High River and JBS's at Brooks, handle on the order of seventy percent of the national supply, and in 2012 a single contaminated plant in Brooks triggered the largest meat recall in Canadian history: roughly four thousand tonnes of beef, pulled back across two countries, from one facility. When the pipe is that narrow, the difference between a chain that documents its sourcing and a chain that shrugs is not branding. It is the difference between a supply chain you can trace and one you are taking on faith. Eight chains, ordered from the most popular in Canada to the challengers, three tests each. Some pass cleanly. Some fail where you least expect. And the biggest scandal on this list belongs to the most Canadian name on it.

Chain one: McDonald's — roughly fourteen hundred restaurants, the most popular burger chain in the country, and the one carrying the biggest surprise of the night, because on test one, the patty, McDonald's Canada passes better than its reputation deserves. Their stated recipe, in the company's own words, is one hundred percent pure beef with no fillers, no binding agents and no additives — just salt and pepper after grilling. And here is the part you can actually verify: since 2003, McDonald's Canada's hamburger patties have been made from one hundred percent Canadian beef — the company says it is the largest ground beef purchaser in the Canadian restaurant industry, with patties made at a plant in Spruce Grove, Alberta. When COVID shut the Cargill plant in High River in 2020 and they briefly had to supplement with imported beef, they publicly disclosed it, and publicly announced the return to all-Canadian that September. That is what voluntary transparency looks like when a company actually does it. And in 2013, when University of Guelph researchers DNA-tested burgers from the big chains — Big Mac included — in the wake of Europe's horsemeat scandal, every single patty came back entirely beef. No fillers, no surprises, in any of them. Hold onto that finding, because it reframes this whole list: in Canada, the patty itself is real basically everywhere. The differences live in sourcing, seasoning, sodium, and disclosure. So where does McDonald's stumble? Test three. A Big Mac now runs around eight to nine dollars on its own, and the nutrition trackers put it near a thousand milligrams of sodium — nearly half of the daily ceiling, in one sandwich, before the fries touch the tray. And there is a quieter transparency story: when McDonald's Canada test-ran the plant-based P.L.T. burger in Ontario — a world-exclusive trial — it ended the experiment in 2020 by simply deleting the burger from its website, no announcement, nothing. CBC had to ask what happened. A company this good at telling you about its beef is equally skilled at not telling you things. Watch, too, what the pricing behaviour admits: when Canadians started pulling back, McDonald's Canada dropped its small coffee to a dollar — and reportedly gained millions of extra orders a month almost overnight — then began lowering value-meal prices as, in the Globe and Mail's words, cash-strapped customers cut back. Prices that can fall that fast the moment traffic dips were never costs. They were tests of what you'd tolerate. Verdict: the patty passes, the Canadian sourcing is real and long-standing, the sodium and the price are the fine print, and the disclosure is excellent — when it flatters them.

Chain two, and this is the centrepiece of the night: A&W. Over eleven hundred locations, Canada's second-largest burger chain — and here is the first thing most Canadians genuinely do not know: A&W Canada is not the American A&W. It has been a completely separate company since 1972, Canadian-owned outright since franchisees bought it in 1995, and it trades on the Toronto Stock Exchange today. The root beer, the Teen Burger, the retro glass mugs — that is a Canadian company, full stop, and it earns real credit for it. Which is exactly what makes the beef story so instructive. In September 2013, A&W launched the most successful marketing campaign in modern Canadian fast food: Better Beef — beef, in their own words, raised without any added steroids or hormones, no added preservatives or additives. And unlike the chicken aisle we covered before — where hormones have been banned since 1963 and "no hormones" is marketing air — this claim is real. Health Canada approves six hormonal growth promoters for use in Canadian beef cattle. Conventional beef can be, and routinely is, raised with them. A&W's promise was a genuine point of difference, and Canadians rewarded it. Then the ranchers read the fine print. Within weeks, Alberta Beef Producers were publicly furious — not about hormones, but about origin. To find enough hormone-free supply, A&W was buying significant amounts of its Better Beef from Montana and from Australia. The executive director of Alberta Beef Producers said it plainly: "We're disappointed that a large Canadian food-service chain would launch a marketing campaign that has them serving significant amounts of imported beef to Canadians." The cattlemen's association added that the hormone difference in the meat itself is, in their words, virtually indistinguishable. By 2017 the premier of Saskatchewan was calling the whole campaign a gimmick. The most Canadian burger chain in the country, running maple-leaf marketing, on imported beef. In March 2020, A&W responded with a new pledge: a move to one hundred percent Canadian, grass-fed beef. Real suppliers were named — Beretta Farms, Spring Creek Ranch in Vegreville — and the CEO, Susan Senecal, the same executive who had defended the import years as a supply problem, framed it warmly: "We buy millions of pounds of Canadian beef today, and we saw this as a way to really increase that supply." Even that pledge started a fight — Alberta's cattle feeders publicly objected that grass-fed marketing implied grain-fed Canadian beef was somehow lesser, when both, in their words, are raised sustainably here. This is the recurring lesson of the A&W story: every one of its marketing promises has been genuinely popular with customers and genuinely divisive with the very Canadian farmers the maple leaf implies. And then... watch the wording, because this is a masterclass. By that fall the language had become "we're on a journey to exclusively source and serve 100% Canadian, grass-finished beef." A journey. Today, in 2026, their newest burger launches advertise "grass-fed beef" — not Canadian grass-fed. And on A&W's own FAQ page, right now, sits a question they wrote themselves: "Why doesn't A&W source 100% Canadian beef?" Six years after the pledge, the question is still on the page, and no completion has ever been announced. Verdict: the hormone claim is real and always was. The maple-leaf assumption — the thing the entire brand radiates — remains, by the company's own FAQ, unfinished business. A&W deserves credit for the Beyond Meat first too — in July 2018 it became the first national chain in Canada to launch the plant-based burger, and within six weeks had to issue an actual press release titled "A&W's Beyond Meat Burger Temporarily Out of Stock!" — a sellout so complete the company had to formally apologize for its own success and restock the whole country that fall. This is a genuinely innovative, genuinely Canadian company whose most famous promise is the one it has not finished keeping. One last detail that proves tonight's voluntary-disclosure point perfectly: try to check the Teen Burger's sodium and the public nutrition trackers cannot even agree — one says around nine hundred milligrams, another over eleven hundred — because when disclosure is voluntary, even the fact-checkers are left comparing photocopies. The company knows the number. The law never requires it to settle the argument. Chain three: Dairy Queen — about seven hundred locations, celebrating eighty-five years in Canada, owned since 1998 by Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway, and the thinnest file in tonight's stack, which is itself the finding. One honest note on the count first: a large share of those seven hundred are treat-only stores — the Blizzard counters — so DQ's actual burger footprint is smaller than the sign count suggests, which may partly explain why the burger side of the business gets so little documentation. The burger menu describes a "100% seasoned real beef patty" — and there is that word from our grocery videos again: seasoned. Not a scandal — but a different food than a salt-and-pepper-after-grilling patty, and DQ publishes far less about its beef than the chains above it: no origin claim we could find, no sourcing page of substance for Canada. The trackers put the basic cheeseburger around 380 calories and 730 milligrams of sodium — actually the lightest flagship numbers on tonight's list, which makes the silence stranger: DQ has a defensible burger and simply declines to talk about it. Remember the frame: none of this disclosure is required, and DQ exercises that right more fully than most. To be fair, nobody walks into a Dairy Queen for the burger's biography — the Blizzard is the headliner and the burger is the opening act. But your money is the same colour at every counter on this list, and the acts that introduce themselves properly are two entries away. Verdict: fails the show-your-work test — not because of what we found, but because of how little there was to find.

Chain four: Wendy's — about four hundred and fifty Canadian locations, in the country since 1975 when the first one opened in Hamilton, every location franchisee-run. The chain marked fifty years in Canada last year, and it chose to mark the anniversary not with nostalgia but with a sourcing statement — because on test one, Wendy's makes the strongest freshness claim in Canadian fast food, on its own Canadian pages, in writing: fresh, never frozen beef from Canadian farmers, field to restaurant in less than ten days. Think about what that sentence commits them to operationally: a cold chain that moves raw beef from farm to grill faster than most grocery beef reaches a store shelf, maintained across four hundred and fifty locations, every week, forever. Companies do not put sentences like that in writing casually — legal departments make sure of it — which is precisely why claims in writing are worth more than claims in commercials. The square patty was designed by Dave Thomas in 1969 to hang over the edge of the bun — so you can see the beef before you bite it, a transparency statement built into the product's shape. Fresh beef behaves differently on a grill and tastes different on the bun, and Wendy's has built its entire Canadian identity on that difference and put the sourcing claim on the record where regulators and journalists can hold it. That is what we keep asking for. The caveats: the trackers put a Dave's Single north of a thousand milligrams of sodium — among the saltiest flagship burgers in the country — and at around ten dollars a burger, fifteen as a combo, Wendy's is pricing itself as the premium of the drive-thru tier while running four-dollar promos underneath, which tells you exactly how much room is built into the menu price. Verdict: the beef passes, the claim is on the record and Canadian, the sodium and the sticker are the trade.

Chain five: Burger King — three hundred and seventy-nine Canadian locations, and the best irony in this entire video: Burger King, the most American-feeling brand in the food court, is owned by Restaurant Brands International — headquartered on King Street in Toronto. When you buy a Whopper anywhere on Earth, the parent company collecting is Canadian. Which makes its Canadian disclosure record all the more remarkable, because we went looking for the two most basic statements a burger chain can make — where the beef comes from, and whether the patty is pure beef with nothing added — and for Burger King Canada we could not find either one. The flame-grilling is real: "more than a quarter pound of 100% flame-grilled beef," says the marketing — and this year the chain is actively reinvesting in the product, launching an "Elevated Whopper" in Canada this spring with its North American president declaring that "the Whopper has always set the standard for flame-grilled taste, and now that standard applies to everything we serve." A standard for taste, announced with fanfare. A standard for disclosure, nowhere. Flame-grilled describes the cooking. It does not answer origin, and it does not answer additives, and no Canadian sourcing page we could locate does. The trackers put the Whopper around 700 calories and eleven hundred milligrams of sodium. This is the exemption in action: nothing forces the Toronto-owned chain to tell Canadians what McDonald's, Wendy's, A&W, and Harvey's all voluntarily tell them — and so it doesn't. Verdict: failed the transparency test — the only major chain on this list where both origin and patty composition are simply unstated. Chain six: Harvey's — nearly three hundred locations, one hundred percent Canadian since 1959, born, by the company's own telling, when its founders spotted an available sign on Danforth Avenue in Toronto; today it belongs to Recipe Unlimited, the old Cara Operations, taken private in 2022 by Toronto's Fairfax Financial in a billion-dollar deal — Canadian-owned at every layer of the stack. And Harvey's passes the one test that matters most here in the most literal way possible: it is the only major chain in Canada where you physically watch your burger being dressed — you stand there, they hold the patty up, and you call the toppings. The build of your lunch is conducted in front of witnesses. On the patty, Harvey's own FAQ is refreshingly plain: the Original burger is "made with beef and a proprietary blend of seasoning and spices" — a seasoned patty, disclosed as such, in their own words, no pretending. And their official nutrition page — voluntary, remember — puts the Original at 380 calories and 840 milligrams of sodium, among the lightest flagship burgers in the country. Then there is the move that history should remember better: in October 2019, Harvey's became the first national chain to offer an Angus burger from beef raised without antibiotics, added hormones or steroids and one hundred percent sourced in Canada — supplied by Beretta Farms, announced with the supplier's name attached. That is the exact promise A&W has been journeying toward for six years, and the modest chain from 1959 quietly finished it first, for its Angus line, with receipts. Verdict: honest seasoning disclosure, published nutrition, a completed Canadian hormone-free option, and topping transparency you can watch. Harvey's never runs the flashiest campaigns and nobody has ever smuggled one across a border, but notice the pattern of the entire evening landing on this counter: the chain that discloses its seasoning instead of hiding it, publishes its sodium instead of letting trackers argue, finished its Canadian sourcing promise instead of journeying toward it, and dresses your burger where you can see it — is the quiet one from 1959 that never needed you to believe a story. The unfashionable Canadian chain is the closest thing this list has to a clean pass.

Chain seven, the challengers, quickly. Five Guys — seventy-three Canadian locations, still owned by the founding Murrell family out of the U.S., and the quality story is genuine: fresh never-frozen beef, patties formed in-house, free toppings in any combination, the famously overfilled fry cup, the peanuts while you wait. It is the chain that competes on abundance — and abundance is exactly what you should price in, because its burgers sit at the top of the category's calorie and cost range, and for all that quality reputation we could find no Canadian sourcing disclosure for its beef at all. The best patty on the block, and the least paperwork: quality real, disclosure thin, wallet warned. Fatburger — seventy-ish Canadian locations run under licence from an American brand group. And South St. Burger, the small Canadian one — founded in 2005 by Jay Gould, the same entrepreneur who built New York Fries — worth knowing exists when you want the homegrown premium option. None of the three publishes enough Canadian sourcing detail to grade seriously, which, one last time, is the point of the whole video: they are not required to, so most don't.

Now the two numbers to carry out of tonight, because they apply at every single counter. First: sodium. Health Canada's ceiling is 2,300 milligrams a day, the average Canadian already eats about 2,760 — fifty-eight percent of us exceed the limit, and among young men it is more than ninety percent — and the flagship burgers on this list run from roughly 730 to over 1,100 milligrams, before fries, before the drink. When CBC's Marketplace tested restaurant meals in its Sodium Shock investigation, single "healthy-sounding" plates blew past the entire daily limit — even a build-your-own grilled chicken burger with steamed vegetables, engineered on camera to be the low-sodium option, came out over eleven hundred milligrams — and the program pointedly noted that New York City requires salt-shaker warning icons on high-sodium menu items while Canadian menus require no sodium disclosure at all. Nothing on a menu board in this country has to warn you about any of it, and outside Ontario, nothing has to tell you the calories either. Second: price. Beef is up twenty-five percent in a year at the grocery store, restaurant prices rose faster than inflation in both 2024 and 2025 — and yet every chain on this list spent those same years running four-dollar burger promos, which tells you the margin was always there. The burger did not get more expensive to make. Your patience got priced.

And one genuinely proud Canadian fact before the final count, because this video is not anti-beef — it is anti-vagueness. Canadian beef itself grades among the best in the world: under the national system — Canada Prime, AAA, AA, A — more than ninety-five percent of graded young cattle hit the A-grades or better, and Canada Prime is the direct equivalent of USDA Prime. The raw material these chains are buying is excellent, which is exactly why "where is it from and what did you add" are fair questions with affordable answers. The chains that answer them are not doing anything heroic. They are doing the minimum that the beef deserves.

The final count, then. Passing on the patty: almost everyone — the Guelph DNA study means the beef in Canadian chain burgers is actually beef, and that is a genuinely good national headline. Passing on disclosure: McDonald's, with its twenty-year Canadian sourcing record; Wendy's, with a fresh-and-Canadian claim made in writing; and Harvey's, the quiet winner — honest about its seasoning, public with its nutrition, first to the finish line on Canadian hormone-free Angus, and the only counter where you watch the build. Mixed: A&W — a real Canadian company with a real hormone-free achievement and a Canadian-beef promise that its own FAQ admits is not done; order the Teen Burger with clear eyes, and maybe ask them, politely, how the journey is going. Failing on disclosure: Burger King, the Toronto-owned chain that tells Canadians the least, along with Dairy Queen and the challengers, who tell us almost nothing because nothing makes them. So here is the whole night as one habit: before you trust a chain's story, spend ten seconds on its website looking for two things — where the beef is from, and what is in the patty. The chains that answer both put it in writing years ago. The chains that answer neither are not hiding it by accident — under Canadian law, silence is free. And add the two questions that cost nothing at the counter: in Ontario, the calories are legally on the board, so actually read them once — the number posted next to your usual order is the most honest sentence in the restaurant; everywhere in Canada, the combo is where the sodium and the margin both hide, so price the burger alone against the combo and decide whether the fries are worth what they actually cost you, in both currencies. None of this takes a minute. All of it was true the whole time, printed or posted or deliberately absent, waiting for somebody to look.
