If you've linked your Tims app to Triangle Rewards since September, Canadian Tire says you can earn up to five percent in Canadian Tire Money on eligible Tims purchases. Read the terms, and three of those five percentage points depend on one thing in your wallet. We'll get to it at number one. In February, Canadian Tire pleaded guilty in a Quebec court to seventy-four counts over regular prices it advertised on five products in 2021. Canadian Tire says no customers were overcharged and the matter is now concluded. So we went through Canadian Tire's own filings, its own terms, provincial rules and the news record. Because the truth is not always on the menu.

Here's the plan. Nine things to know before your next trip to Canadian Tire, counted down from nine to one. Then three things it genuinely gets right, including a perk for Triangle members. And if you're in Ontario, one sign worth looking for before you hand over your car keys. Some of these are about price tags. Some are about your data. And one is about the money sitting in your Canadian Tire account.

Number nine. The regular price on the tag. You've seen it a hundred times. A knife set or a drill in the flyer, a big regular price, a bigger red sale price underneath. And you've probably done the math in the aisle, working out how much you're saving. In Quebec, the consumer protection office decided to check those regular prices. Over six months in 2021, it followed a handful of products in Canadian Tire flyers, on its website, and in stores around Montreal. In February 2026, Canadian Tire pleaded guilty to seventy-four counts under Quebec's Consumer Protection Act, for falsely stating a regular or reference price. It covered five products: Henckels and Cuisinart knife sets, Lagostina and Heritage cookware, and a DeWalt cordless drill. The fines and costs came to just under one point three million dollars. The office said its review of sales data showed those products sold at the regular price in only a very small proportion of cases. Canadian Tire's own pricing page defines it this way: regular prices shown reflect the prices at which the products have been sold by Canadian Tire as of the date of issuance indicated. Here's Canadian Tire's side on the plea, word for word. The charges relate to five products over a six-month period five years ago. Importantly, no customers were overcharged and the matter is now concluded. So keep it in proportion. One province, five products, 2021, and a guilty plea. But it tells you something useful about any regular price, anywhere. The fix: judge a sale by the price you pay today, not by the number it's crossed out from. Compare the same model number at another store before you decide it's a deal. And keep that DeWalt drill in mind, because we'll come back to DeWalt in the protect section.

Quick honesty check, because the title needs it. Canadian Tire's own figures show comparable sales at its Canadian Tire stores down in both the first and second quarters of this year. The company points to weather and seasonal categories. SportChek and Mark's grew. That's not a boycott, and we won't call it one. Every key claim in this video gets a label.

Number eight. The name on the door isn't the company's. You walk into a Canadian Tire and it feels like one national chain. Same red triangle, same flyer, same app. Legally, it's not that simple. Canadian Tire's annual information form says its five hundred and two stores are run by Dealers, who are independent third parties. They own the inventory, they employ the staff, and they pay the store's operating costs. So the person at the till works for the store owner, not head office. Your local store can also choose what it stocks for its community. Then comes the line that matters for your wallet. Dealers agree to sell at prices not exceeding those set by the company. And Canadian Tire's pricing page adds: Canadian Tire Associate Dealers may sell for less. So the company sets a ceiling, and your local store can go under it. Here's what that looks like. On October 4th, Canadian Tire's own price data showed a three-hundred-and-twenty-five-gram can of WD-40 with a regular price of twelve ninety-nine at stores in Toronto and Calgary. That's about four dollars per hundred grams. In Halifax and Vancouver, the same can's regular price was eleven ninety-nine, about three sixty-nine per hundred grams. Same product, same day, different regular price. And here's a thing a lot of people still assume exists. Canadian Tire's current list of policies has no price-match policy on it, and the price-match web address we tried returns an error page. Its pricing page also says Dealers are under no obligation to match online prices until the sale begins in their region. So when two stores in your city show different prices on the same thing, that's how the model is set up. It isn't a glitch. The fix: treat your Canadian Tire as its own store. Check that store's price on the app before you drive over, and don't count on a price match at the till. Keep that in mind, because number seven is about the account you may have made to shop online.

Number seven. The account you made to shop online. If you never made an online account, this one isn't about you. But maybe you made it once, to order a barbecue cover. The company said the database covered online accounts at Canadian Tire, SportChek, Mark's and Party City, so one account at any of them counts. On October 14th, 2025, Canadian Tire said it had found a breach in an e-commerce database. It said the data included names, addresses, emails and year of birth, along with encrypted passwords and, in some cases, truncated card numbers. It said that for fewer than one hundred and fifty thousand accounts, the data included a full date of birth. It said the encrypted passwords and partial card numbers can't be used for account access, transactions or purchases. That's the company's statement. And it said the database did not include Canadian Tire Bank information or Triangle Rewards loyalty data. The company also said it reported the matter to the applicable privacy regulators. The breach-tracking service Have I Been Pwned later listed about thirty-eight million email addresses in the data. That's that service's count, not the company's. In July 2026, two law firms announced a proposed class action. It hasn't been certified, and its claims are allegations. Global News reported that Canadian Tire offered credit monitoring through TransUnion. The fix is simple and takes two minutes. If you reused that password anywhere, change it there. And when an email about your account arrives, don't click. Open the app or type the website yourself. That one was about your data. The next one is about your money, after you bring something back.

Number six. The refund that comes with an expiry date. You return something without the right paperwork, and instead of your money back, you're handed a Refund Card. Here's the line in Canadian Tire's own terms. Refund Cards will expire one year after the date of issue, except for those issued in Saskatchewan. After expiry, any remaining balance will not be returned to you. Compare that with a Canadian Tire gift card, which the same policy says has no expiry date. A Refund Card also can't be used online, and the part of a purchase you pay with it doesn't earn Canadian Tire Money. Now the return window itself. If you paid by card and lost the receipt, the policy offers a receipt look-up for card purchases within ninety days. Unopened items with a receipt get ninety days, but the policy shortens that for some categories, including thirty days for electronics. And opened or damaged items may not be eligible at all. Some things can't come back at any time. The policy's non-refundable list includes tinted paint, products cut to length, and clearance, final sale or as-is merchandise. Valid photo ID may also be required for a return. To be fair, ninety days is a decent headline window, and Triangle members can return items without a paper receipt. The fix: whenever you can, ask for the refund back to your original card. If you do take a Refund Card, put a reminder in your phone for eleven months from today. Easy to miss, easy to fix. Number five comes from a privacy commissioner's report.

Number five. What a privacy commissioner found in B.C. This one's from 2023, and it has a clear ending. British Columbia's privacy commissioner investigated Canadian Tire stores using facial recognition cameras. The finding: four B.C. Canadian Tire stores that used the technology between 2018 and 2021 broke the province's privacy law. The commissioner said they didn't properly notify people entering the store, didn't show a reasonable purpose, and didn't get consent. Commissioner Michael McEvoy put it plainly. Retailers would have to present a highly compelling case to show that kind of collection is reasonable, and the stores failed to do so in this case. Twelve B.C. stores were using it, and all twelve removed their systems after the investigation began. It's the only finding by a privacy regulator against Canadian Tire stores that we found. There were no penalties, because the law had no penalty provisions. And Canadian Tire said the company and its Dealers had mutually agreed to prohibit facial recognition in Canadian Tire stores. So the ending is the fix, and the company made it. It's still worth knowing who decided, because it goes back to number eight. Those were Dealer-run stores. If you only keep one fix so far, keep the Refund Card one. That's the halfway point. And the five percent from the start of this video comes back at number one.

Number four. The price tool with a name. It's called DaiVID. On a call with analysts in August, CEO Greg Hicks said Canadian Tire used its DaiVID AI analysis to drop prices on more than five thousand products. He said customers responded strongly to lower prices on essentials like cleaning and storage. So if you've noticed cheaper basics in those aisles, that's the company's explanation. Global News then asked the obvious question. A company spokesperson answered: we do not use DaiVID for algorithmic pricing or real-time dynamic pricing. It's a longer-term pricing tool. Its chief operating officer, TJ Flood, described it this way: most of our price changes are deliberate, to try to provide value where we think Canadians want value. He added that at times, they do watch competitive activity and react to it. Global also noted the company didn't say whether the same tools could help plan price increases. The company's filing also says its refreshed stores are getting electronic shelf labels. On its own, that's a fact about price tags, not evidence of anything else. So here's what we can say, and what we can't. There's no evidence that Canadian Tire changes your price based on who you are, and we're not going to claim it. What we did see in its own data is that sales don't end on the same day everywhere. A twenty-four pack of Duracell AA batteries was on sale for nineteen ninety-nine in all four cities we checked, about eighty-three cents a battery. In Toronto and Vancouver the sale ran to October 8th. In Calgary and Halifax it ended a day earlier. The fix: if the sale price matters to you, check the end date for your store on the app, not the one your cousin saw in another province. Four to go, and the next one is about where the box on the shelf comes from.

Number three. Where the box on the shelf comes from. A lot of people shop here partly because it feels Canadian. Here's what the company's own filing says. In 2025, about half of Canadian Tire Retail's inventory purchases were sourced directly from vendors outside Canada, primarily from Asia. Separately, in February 2025, Greg Hicks said about fifteen percent of the company's goods come from the U.S. Those two numbers measure different things, so don't add them together. When tariffs were first threatened, Hicks said the company was reviewing products and U.S. suppliers, and assessing alternatives to the inflationary pressure those tariffs would bring. The Canadian Press reported he said Canadian suppliers could be found for between a quarter and a third of what it buys from the U.S. He also said that for anything that goes into a bag or a bottle, it's likely a Canadian supplier. And this August, he told analysts consumer sentiment remained soft, with shoppers living under the threat of trade wars and tariffs. In 2025, the company also acquired the Hudson's Bay brand marks, with Hicks saying some things are just meant to stay Canadian. So Canadian ownership and Canadian products are two different questions. The company is Canadian. A lot of what's on its shelves isn't, and it says so in its own filing. Picture the storage bins, the batteries, the extension cords. The flag on the store tells you who owns the building. The small print on the box tells you where the product came from. The fix: read the origin line on the box, not the feeling you get from the red triangle.

Before the last two, which touch your wallet most directly, one quick thing. If you want the next Canadian name checked against the record like this, take a second to subscribe. Canadian Counter reads the fine print so you don't have to. Now, the service bay.

Number two. The service bay. Canadian Tire's filing says its stores house thousands of automotive service bays, and the company says auto service hit a record one billion dollars in annual sales in late 2025. It also says its automotive sales have grown for twenty-four quarters in a row. So a lot of Canadians hand their keys over a Canadian Tire counter. Picture the November rush, when everyone wants winter tires on the same Saturday. Here's what you're agreeing to when you sign. The auto service terms say you can approve an estimate later in writing, orally or by electronic communication. So a quick yes on the phone counts. The same terms say shop supplies are calculated at a rate of ten percent of labour sales. That line shows up on the bill, so expect it. And if you store your tires there, the terms say they'll be kept up to eight months from the invoice date. Tires left more than thirty days past that term will be disposed of, without any liability to you or obligation to return them. And remember that sign. In Ontario, the Consumer Protection Act gives you the right to a written estimate. The repairer may not charge more than ten percent above that estimate, and may not charge for work you didn't authorize. Ontario shops must also post a sign that tells you about any commissions they receive for selling parts, and how that commission is calculated. That's Ontario law. Other provinces have their own rules, so check yours. And to be clear, we found no regulator finding against Canadian Tire's auto service. This is about knowing your rights at any shop. If you buy tires there, you'll also be offered a paid add-on called Replacement Advantage, priced per tire depending on the tire's price. Read what it covers before you say yes. The fix: ask for the estimate in writing, read that sign, and say no to anything you didn't approve. One left, and it's the one we promised at the very start.

Number one. The Canadian Tire Money rule. Back to the five percent. When you scan your Triangle Rewards card, Canadian Tire's own terms say the base rate is zero point four percent. That's forty cents on a hundred-dollar purchase before tax. Pay with a Triangle Mastercard, and its own data shows four percent at its stores. That's the difference the card makes, and the same card shows up in the Tims deal. Here are the Tims terms, word for word from the September release. Scan for Tims Rewards, earn two percent. Pay with a Triangle credit card, earn an additional two percent. Use a Triangle credit card in the Tims app's Scan and Pay, earn an additional one percent. So three of the five points need the Canadian Tire Bank card. Without it, the most you can earn on Tims is two percent. On a ten-dollar coffee order, that's twenty cents, or roughly fifty cents with the card. And you can't spend that Canadian Tire Money at Tims. The terms also say not all Tim Hortons locations participate, so check yours. And the Tims link isn't the only one. Canadian Tire has also linked Triangle with RBC, WestJet and Petro-Canada, so there are more and more ways to earn. A few more rules most people never read. Online, you can't use Canadian Tire Money on tires and wheels. Some charges, like environmental fees and tire disposal fees, don't earn it at all. Canadian Tire Money in your account may expire after eighteen months with no activity. And paper Canadian Tire Money stopped being issued in March 2020, though you can still spend old bills at Canadian Tire stores. Then there's the paid tier, Triangle Select. It costs eighty-nine dollars a year, auto-renews in most provinces, and outside Quebec the fee isn't refundable if you cancel mid-term. Its everyday bonus is an extra four percent in Canadian Tire Money. By our arithmetic, you'd need to spend about two thousand two hundred dollars a year in its stores for that bonus alone to cover the fee. The company says engaged Triangle members spend more than twice as much as non-members. The fix: if you carry a balance, interest can cost more than the rewards earn. Decide on the card first, then decide on the perks.

How to protect yourself. First, judge every sale by today's price on the same model number, and watch the environmental fee added on top. On October 4th, a Duracell twenty-four pack in Toronto showed a two eighty-eight fee on top of its nineteen ninety-nine sale price. On October 4th, a DeWalt drill and impact driver kit was on sale at Canadian Tire for one ninety-nine ninety-nine, down from a regular two ninety-nine ninety-nine. Home Depot and Amazon Canada both listed the same model at one ninety-eight that day. Second, at the service counter, get a written estimate. In Ontario, the final bill can't go more than ten percent over it, and that commissions sign has to be posted. Outside Ontario, check your own province's consumer protection office before the work starts. Third, keep your Canadian Tire Money moving. If you link Tims, know that two percent is what you get without the card. Scan every trip, spend it before eighteen months of inactivity, and use a Refund Card within the year. Fourth, protect your account. Change any reused password, turn on two-step sign-in where it's offered, and never log in through an emailed link.

None of this is about boycotting Canadian Tire. Plenty of Canadians still rely on it every week, and some of that loyalty is earned. It's about walking in with your eyes open. And it's only fair to say it gets some things right.

First, the Triangle perk we promised. This year, Canadian Tire extended Triangle Rewards member benefits to include free ship-to-home on Canadian Tire online orders. Members can also return items without a paper receipt at Canadian Tire, Mark's, SportChek and Atmosphere, because the purchase is on their account. Neither needs the credit card. Just the free membership. And if you hold RBC Avion or WestJet points, the linked programs say you can convert them into Canadian Tire Money.

Second, Jumpstart. It's Canadian Tire's charity for kids' sports and recreation. Its audited 2025 statements show about thirty million dollars paid out to programs. Jumpstart says Canadian Tire Corporation covers its administration costs, so customer donations go to kids. The catch: its 2024 annual report shows customers gave more of the money than the corporation did. And its own FAQ says that as of 2026, individual grants are capped at three hundred dollars per child per year. Jumpstart's own site says it has invested more than three hundred and twenty-nine million dollars since 2005. Still, the program is real, it's audited, and it's large.

Third, it really is Canadian. The company started from a single garage in 1922, founded by brothers A.J. and J.W. Billes, and its filing says the family still controls about sixty-one percent of the voting shares. In a Harris Poll in February 2025, reported by Strategy magazine, Canadian Tire was the brand Canadians named most often as the most Canadian. And in Leger's 2026 reputation ranking, it still placed in the top ten. Just remember number three. Canadian ownership doesn't mean Canadian-made.

That's the full picture. The price tag, the Dealer, your account, the Refund Card, the cameras, the price tool, the box, the service bay and the money. Nine things worth knowing, three worth using.
