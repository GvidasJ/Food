# SCRIPT — CANNED SOUP EPISODE

TITLE: We Investigated 8 "Canadian" Soup Brands (Only 3 Are ACTUALLY Canadian)

- The New Warning Symbol Is All Over The Soup Aisle (We Investigated 8 Brands)

Go open your pantry and bring back a can of soup. Any can. I'll wait — this video is about that exact can. Got it? Good. Now look at the front. If that can was made this year, there's a good chance it's wearing something no soup can in Canadian history ever wore before: a small black-and-white magnifying glass that says "High in sodium — Health Canada." That symbol became mandatory on January the first of this year, and the soup aisle is where it landed harder than almost anywhere else in the store. And if your can DOESN'T have the symbol — same soup, same label, no magnifying glass — that's legal too, and by the end of this video you'll know exactly why both cans are sitting on the same shelf.

But the symbol is only the second-biggest thing that's happened to this aisle. Here's the biggest, and most Canadians have no idea: the famous soup left the country. Not one drop of Campbell's soup has been made in Canada since 2019 — and that includes Habitant. Yes — Habitant. The big yellow can. Quebec's own pea soup, born in a Montreal grocery store in 1918, is now made in the United States by a company in New Jersey. Meanwhile — and this is the part we love — the cans in this aisle that ARE still made in Canada are the ones nobody brags about: the store brand, the dollar-and-a-quarter condensed can, and a soup company in Saint-Hyacinthe, Quebec you've probably never heard of whose own slogan is "The Brand behind the Brand." By the end of this video you'll know which three of these eight brands are actually Canadian, why a soup can holds exactly 284 millilitres, and why the federal government — this is real — officially recommends you keep canned food and a manual can opener in your house. So does your grandmother. They're both right.

One piece of law before the brands, because it frames everything. We went looking for the legal standard for soup — the way bread, ketchup and even tea have standards in Canadian food law. There isn't one we could find. The federal compositional standards run nineteen volumes — dairy, meat, vinegar, salt, tea — and soup appears in none of them. Remember the bread episode, where the law forces raisin bread to carry raisins equal to half the weight of the flour? Chicken noodle soup, legally, owes you no particular amount of chicken. The one law every can must obey is quieter and better: the Food and Drug Regulations say, word for word, "No person shall sell a low-acid food packaged in a hermetically sealed container unless the food is commercially sterile." That's the rule — heat, sealed can, nothing alive inside — that turns soup into a thing that waits patiently in your pantry for years. It's one of the oldest food-safety ideas in the world, it works, and it's why the can is the most trustworthy package in the store even when the label is playing games. We read the labels so you know which is which. If you've already got your can in hand, hit the like button — and subscribe if you want to know what the fine print on your groceries actually says. Now, most popular first.

Brand one: Campbell's. The red-and-white can — market researchers describe condensed soup in this country as a duopoly: Campbell's, and everyone's store brand. The Canadian story starts in 1931, when Campbell's opened a plant in New Toronto — now Etobicoke — and, the story goes, advertised for workers by writing help-wanted notices in chalk on the sidewalks of Lakeshore Boulevard. For eighty-seven years, Canadian Campbell's was made on Birmingham Street. Then, January 24, 2018: closure announced. About three hundred and eighty jobs. The company said the plant — its smallest and oldest — couldn't be retrofitted competitively, and Campbell Canada's president said, "Today is a tough day." Production moved to Maxton, North Carolina; Napoleon, Ohio; and Paris, Texas. The factory came down in 2021. So when you hold the classic can today, the fine print says Campbell Company of Canada, Mississauga — that's an office. The soup is American. We'll read the origin line on camera so you can see exactly how the can phrases it. Two footnotes on the company itself. In November 2024, shareholders voted to rename the Campbell Soup Company — after 155 years, it's now officially "The Campbell's Company," because, the company says, it's about more than soup these days; they were careful to add the change "does not impact the Campbell's soup brand or packaging." And there's one small, telling exception to the exodus: Campbell's own website advertises its Roasted Garlic Chicken Broth as — their words — "prepared right here in Canada." One broth. When a company's marketing goes out of its way to say ONE product is made here, you've learned something about all the others. We'll check that can's fine print too.

While we're reading: the condensed Tomato, in full, from Campbell's Canadian site: "Water, tomato paste, sugars (glucose-fructose), wheat flour, salt, soybean or canola oil, citric acid, flavour (contains celery, garlic), spice extracts." Old friends everywhere — glucose-fructose, Canada's legal name for high-fructose corn syrup, which regulars of this channel could spot blindfolded by now, and notice that the first ingredient of a CONDENSED tomato soup is water. They removed the water, and water is still first. Dorrance would be proud. As for the monosodium glutamate on the chicken noodle label — that's a legal, declared ingredient in Canada, full stop, and we'll let Campbell's own FAQ speak for itself: the company notes MSG "contains only 13 percent sodium compared to 39 percent sodium in table salt," and says it makes "around 130 different varieties" without added MSG. Their words, their math, on the record. We read labels; we don't referee seasoning.

Now flip it over, because the label on this American-made Canadian classic is a masterclass. Condensed Chicken Noodle, from Campbell's own Canadian website, in full: "Chicken broth (water, chicken stock), noodles (wheat flour, whole egg), seasoned chicken (soy), salt, chicken fat, monosodium glutamate, corn starch, flavour, onion powder, yeast extract, spice extracts, beta carotene, dehydrated garlic." Every one of those is legal and declared exactly as Canadian law requires — MSG by name, "flavour" as its permitted one-word self. And the number that matters in 2026: eight hundred milligrams of sodium per two-hundred-fifty-millilitre bowl. The magnifying-glass symbol triggers at fifteen percent of the daily value — three hundred and forty-five milligrams a bowl. Chicken noodle prints thirty-three percent. More than double the trigger. Tomato: thirty-three percent. Cream of Mushroom, the casserole workhorse: thirty-seven. That's why the new symbol found the soup aisle first — we're doing arithmetic on the government's own thresholds here, nothing more; whether that matters to you is between you and your bowl. And this didn't come out of nowhere: Health Canada spent over a decade asking nicely first. In 2012 it published voluntary sodium-reduction targets for ninety-four categories of processed food — soup got its own category — and when it checked the results, its own page says the voluntary approach "yielded modest results." Campbell's, in fairness, genuinely moved: back in 2010 the company announced it had cut its tomato soup to four hundred and eighty milligrams a serving — "a nearly 50 per cent reduction over seven years" — and said it had removed, their phrase, "the equivalent of more than 30 million teaspoons of salt" from its Canadian products since 2003. Thirty million teaspoons. And here's the punchline of the decade-long story: even that reformulated flagship, at four-eighty a bowl, still sits at twenty-one percent of the daily value — above the fifteen-percent line. Voluntary asks, modest results, and then in 2022 the government stopped asking and printed a magnifying glass. Two other details we can't resist. Health Canada's own industry guide uses a soup can as its worked example — figure 8.4 is literally "creamy tomato soup with the symbol for high in sat fat, sugars and sodium," a can wearing the triple crown. And because a can is round, the regulations had to define where the front of a cylinder even IS: the guide says forty percent of the can's curved surface, excluding the top and bottom, counts as the display panel. There are public servants in Ottawa who measured the curve of a soup can so a magnifying glass would have a legal address. We find that genuinely delightful.

And the "condensed" concept deserves its own decode, because it's a 129-year-old shipping trick that still shapes your serving math. In 1897 a chemist at Campbell's named John Dorrance figured out that if you removed the water, you could ship half the weight and let the customer add the water back at home — condensed soup sold for a dime and built the company. The catch, in 2026: the label says "mix soup plus one can water," which turns your 284-millilitre can into roughly two and a quarter servings — and every number in the Nutrition Facts table is per ONE serving. The sodium figure you just read is less than half the can. If you eat the whole can as lunch — and be honest — double everything. That's not a scandal; it's printed right there. It's just printed in a way most people never multiply.

Brand two, same company, bigger can: Chunky. Ready to serve, no water needed, "big pieces of tender chicken" — and the 515-millilitre can is a bit over two servings by its own label: eight hundred and thirty milligrams a serving, thirty-six percent of the daily value, call it seventy-two percent per can if you drink it alone from the pot like a normal person. Its ingredient list runs about nineteen items, including — all verbatim, all legal — "monosodium glutamate," "hydrolyzed protein (soy, corn, wheat)," and "caramel colour." Hold that count of nineteen. A little later, a yellow can is going to beat it with seven.

Brand three — collectively maybe the real number one: the store brands. No Name. Selection. Great Value. Compliments. PC. Here's this episode's detective story, and it's the same trick we used on the bread aisle: nobody tells you who actually cans the store brands — the label just says "Prepared for Loblaws" or "Prepared for Metro." But there's a company in Saint-Hyacinthe, Quebec called BCI Foods — the old Baxters Canada — that describes itself, in writing, as "the largest supplier of private label canned soup in Canada." Their corporate slogan, I promise this is real, is "The Brand behind the Brand." And in March 2023, a recall notice for swollen cans did what no marketing page ever would: the recalling company for both No Name AND Selection condensed chicken soups was Les Aliments BCI. One Quebec plant, two retailers' labels, on the public record. And here's the twist that makes the soup aisle the mirror image of the bread aisle: Metro's Selection condensed chicken noodle is listed as a PRODUCT OF CANADA at a dollar twenty-five — while the famous American-made brand sits beside it. On flyer week, mind you, Campbell's drops to eighty-eight cents as a loss leader — the imported icon undercutting the Canadian-made store brand. That's not a conspiracy; that's how flyers work. But if "made in Canada" is what you're shopping for in this aisle, the cheap can is often the Canadian one. A few more store-brand notes from our shelf walk. Walmart's Great Value tomato is named — look closely — "Tomato FLAVOURED Condensed Soup." That word "flavoured" is doing legal work; when it shows up in the product's actual name, the naming rules are telling you something about what's inside, so read the ingredient list. Compliments' condensed chicken noodle reads nearly beat-for-beat like the national brand's, monosodium glutamate and all — the store brand isn't a different philosophy, it's a different logo. Loblaws even stocks Tim Hortons-branded canned soup in this aisle now, because in Canada everything eventually becomes Tim Hortons. And a genuine myth-bust for the Costco members: there is no Kirkland canned soup. The Kirkland chicken noodle everyone mentions is a refrigerated deli tub; Costco's actual canned-soup shelf is Campbell's multipacks. The one store brand that famously names its real maker on other products doesn't play in this aisle at all.

Brand four. Okay, Quebec — sit down. Habitant. The big yellow can of soupe aux pois. It started in 1918 at Philias Morin's grocery in Outremont, where his daughter Marie-Blanche cooked the family recipe — yellow peas, pork fat, savory. It became the taste of home for generations, reportedly still moving millions of cans a year, most of them in Quebec. Read the label today and it's genuinely beautiful in its honesty — seven ingredients, the shortest list in the entire aisle: "Water, yellow peas, lard, modified corn starch, salt, onion powder, spice." Lard — which is why the vegetarian version exists, and why your grandmother's version tasted the way it did. Now the ownership story. Habitant passed to Catelli in the fifties, and in 1989 the soup business was bought by Campbell's. For thirty years that still meant made-in-Toronto. But when Etobicoke closed, Habitant went south with everything else. Campbell's own website today says — read the wording carefully — Habitant soups "have been made using traditional homemade recipes since 1918." The recipes since 1918. Not the location. We're going to put a current can on camera and read the origin fine print together, because where this can says it comes from now is something every Quebecer deserves to see with their own eyes. Quebec's soup, made in America, by New Jersey's soup company. Only in Canada? Pity. And one bit of arithmetic while the yellow can is out: the Nutrition Facts print eight hundred milligrams — thirty-five percent of the daily value — per two-hundred-fifty-millilitre serving. The 796-millilitre can is three point two servings. Nobody in the history of Quebec has divided a can of soupe aux pois into three point two 250-millilitre portions across three sittings; that can is Saturday lunch for two, and by the printed math, the whole can runs about a hundred and twelve percent of the sodium daily value. Again: their numbers, their serving sizes, simple multiplication. The label isn't lying to you. It's just counting on you not owning a calculator.

Brand five — and now the redemption arc: Aylmer. The name has been on Canadian cans since June 16, 1881 — a canning company in Aylmer, Ontario, that grew into Canadian Canners Limited, once a colossus of the canning world. The trail runs through Del Monte in 1956, a sale to Scotland's Baxters in 2007 — and then, in June 2018, something that almost never happens in these episodes: the money came home. A Quebec consortium — the Fonds de solidarité FTQ, Fondaction, Investissement Québec — bought Baxters Canada and renamed it BCI Foods. Yes: the same "Brand behind the Brand" plant from the store-brand story. Today the Aylmer soup website says, plainly: "We make 100% of our soups in Saint Hyacinthe, Quebec," and — their words — "We are a small Canadian soup company." A century and a half after the name went on a can, Aylmer soup is Canadian-owned and Canadian-made again. Two footnotes worth your attention. First: Aylmer canned TOMATOES belong to ConAgra, an American giant — same 1881 name, two different owners depending on which shelf you're standing in; read the can. Second: Primo soups — the Italian-Canadian brand — are also made by BCI under license, and Primo's own FAQ states "There is no MSG added in our Primo soups." The Primo pasta and beans, meanwhile, belong to Sun-Brite in Kingsville, Ontario — the tomato family from our ketchup episode. Small country, same cast.

Brand six, briefly: St-Hubert. The rotisserie-sauce soups. Owned since 2016 by Recipe Unlimited — the Toronto restaurant group controlled by Fairfax Financial, which paid five hundred and thirty-seven million for Groupe St-Hubert. But the soup is still made in Quebec — Boisbriand and Blainville — it's certified by Aliments du Québec, and Metro lists the chicken noodle as a product of Canada. Canadian-owned, Quebec-made. It counts.

And the beans, because the bean shelf is where Quebec gets its revenge. Clark. Founded — the company's own history — in 1877, when William Clark launched his beans on St-Antoine Street in Montreal. Clark fed Canadian households through two world wars, and in the 1960s it was bought by a Quebec company that never let go: Aliments Ouimet-Cordon Bleu of Anjou, Montreal, which still makes Clark beans and Clark pea soup at its Montreal factory, with Aliments du Québec certification on the can. So hold the two cans side by side: Habitant, the icon, American-owned and American-made. Clark, the quiet one, Montreal-owned and Montreal-made since before Confederation had grey hair. And one more inversion for the road: Heinz beans — the American brand — are canned in Leamington, Ontario by Highbury Canco, the hometown company from our ketchup episode, under a deal running to the end of 2027. In this aisle, the American cans are Canadian and the Canadian cans are American. Read. The. Fine. Print.

Now the law-and-history round, because canned soup has the best of it. Why is a soup can 284 millilitres? Because it isn't metric — it's ten imperial fluid ounces wearing a metric costume. The 540 is nineteen ounces. The 796 is twenty-eight. And this isn't just habit: there is a federal document, in force today, that prescribes standard container sizes — 284, 398, 540, 796 millilitres and up — for canned vegetables. Soup itself isn't on the list, but the sizes carried over from the same imperial ancestors, and Statistics Canada literally prices the nation's soup by the "canned soup, 284 millilitres" — a can that cost eighty-eight cents on average in January 2017 and a dollar fifty-six as of this summer. Up seventy-seven percent. Next: the date stamped on your can. Under Canadian law, a best-before date is only required on food that lasts ninety days or less. Canned soup lasts years — which means the date on the can is voluntary. It's a quality promise and a stock-rotation tool, and the CFIA itself says a durable life date "is not an indicator of food safety, neither before nor after the date." The can in the back of your cupboard is almost certainly fine; check the seams, not the stamp. And the trade war found this aisle too: in March 2025, Ottawa's counter-tariff list included — line item 2104.10.00 — "soups and broths," a twenty-five percent surtax on American soup that ran until September. And in the round taking effect September 8 of THIS year — days after this video — the fifty percent tariff list includes item 7310.21.00: "cans which are to be closed by soldering or crimping." The empty can itself. Which is fitting, because the can was born a weapon of war: Nicolas Appert invented preserved food for Napoleon's army and collected his twelve-thousand-franc prize in 1810; Peter Durand patented the tin can under George the Third the same year. The can opener? 1858. For forty-eight years, cans carried the instruction — verbatim — "Cut round the top near the outer edge with a chisel and hammer." Which brings us full circle: the Government of Canada's official emergency-preparedness page tells every household to keep enough supplies for seventy-two hours, food that "won't spoil" — and, quote, "include a manual can opener." Two hundred and sixteen years on, the can still needs its key. Your grandmother's pantry was policy. She'd remember why, too: during the Second World War the can was so strategically valuable it was effectively conscripted — canned fruits went on the ration in 1943, and canned salmon and tuna all but vanished from Canadian grocery shelves, diverted overseas to feed the war effort. And the can's quiet law still earns its keep today: this past June, a lid defect on a run of Amy's lentil soup — about a hundred and eighty thousand cans — was caught and recalled across Canada and the US before any illness was reported, because in canning, the seam is the whole ballgame and the system watches it. That's also why soup is what food banks ask for: canned soup and stew sit permanently on the most-needed lists, they work for people without full kitchens, and Food Banks Canada itself manufactured over six hundred thousand packets of soup "using Canadian crops" for food banks last year. The most humble shelf in the store does the most work in the country. Oh — and if you make pea soup from actual canned peas and want to brag about the pea content, CFIA guidance says the percentage must be calculated on the drained weight of the peas: the canning water doesn't count as pea. Somewhere in Ottawa, someone wrote that down, and we thank them.

So — who wins the soup aisle? If you want Canadian-owned AND Canadian-made, there are exactly three in this video. Aylmer — and its BCI stablemates — a hundred per cent made in Saint-Hyacinthe by the Quebec-owned company that also cans the store brands; usually one of the cheapest name-brand cans on the shelf. St-Hubert — Canadian-owned, Quebec-made, certified. And on the bean shelf, Clark — Montreal since 1877, still Montreal. Honourable mentions with asterisks: Heinz beans, American name but Leamington hands. The store-brand condensed cans marked "Product of Canada" — the recall paperwork suggests you can thank Saint-Hyacinthe. And Habitant — look, the recipe is still seven honest ingredients, and nobody can take 1918 away from it; just know what the fine print on the bottom of that yellow can is going to tell you. One last thing before the comments. In 1962, Andy Warhol painted thirty-two Campbell's soup cans and hung them in a row like a grocery shelf, and it became one of the most famous artworks of the century — the Museum of Modern Art paid a reported fifteen million for it. The most famous painting of a grocery item on Earth is a soup-can label. And as of this January, in Canada, that label legally has to make room for a magnifying glass.
