You've probably got two or three tins of tomatoes sitting in your cupboard right now. And why wouldn't you? It's one of the most basic, humble products in the entire Canadian grocery run. A few tomatoes, a bit of juice, a can. What could possibly go wrong? As it turns out, quite a lot, and almost all of it is written on the label, in words nobody has ever taught you to read.

Before we name a single brand, here are three things about that tin that most Canadians have never noticed.

First. There is a federal law that defines what canned tomatoes are, and a federal grading system printed right on the can. Under Canada's food compositional standards, anything sold as canned tomatoes must contain not less than 50 percent drained tomato solids, measured by an official government method. And on top of that floor sits a grading ladder almost nobody can decode: Canada Fancy means at least 65 percent drained solids with, in the regulation's own words, a practically uniform good red colour. Canada Choice means at least 60 percent. And Canada Standard, the bottom rung, means the can only has to be half tomato by drained weight, and the legal definition of its colour requirement, reasonably good red colour, allows, quote, not more than half of the units showing a greenish or yellowish hue. Half the tomatoes in a Canada Standard tin are allowed to be greenish. That is the actual regulation. And here's the part that makes this useful instead of just outrageous: for canned tomatoes that cross a border or a provincial line, printing the grade is mandatory. The word Fancy, Choice or Standard on your tin is legally load-bearing. You've been walking past a government quality report on every can, your whole life. And the depth of these rules is genuinely wonderful. The drained-solids test is specified down to the hardware: the tomatoes are drained for exactly half a minute over a screen with two meshes per inch, made of wire one millimetre thick. The defect rules are measured in fractions of a square inch of skin per 20-ounce can, in tenths of an ounce of core material, in counted black spots. Somebody in the federal government wrote down precisely how much tomato skin is allowed in your Fancy tin, and nobody ever told you. One more thing while we're here: the words diced and stewed are federally defined terms with legal requirements attached. The word crushed is not a standardized name at all. Diced is law. Crushed is marketing.

Second. Almost all of the actually-Canadian tins on that shelf come from just two small Ontario towns, and the brand names will not help you guess which ones. We'll get there, because it's the best part of this story. For now, remember one label trick that settles any store-brand mystery in five seconds. Federal rules require an imported product to say, quote, imported for, or imported by, the Canadian company. So flip the can. If it says Prepared in Canada for the retailer, it was packed here. If it says Imported for the retailer, it wasn't. One line of six-point type is the whole confession.

And third, the uncomfortable one. In December 2024, a BBC investigation commissioned a forensic lab to test 64 tomato purée products sold in the UK, Germany and the United States, and reported that 17 of them appeared to contain tomatoes from Xinjiang, China, a region where international organizations have documented the systematic use of forced labour. Appeared to contain, that's the BBC's own wording, and every supermarket named disputed the findings. None of those products were sold in Canada. But Canada has its own chapter, and it aired on the CBC three years earlier. In 2021, a CBC Marketplace investigation reported that the Italian company making store-brand tomato products for two of Canada's biggest grocers had purchased tomato paste from Xinjiang as recently as May of that year. Connected to, was CBC's careful wording. The Italian supplier said it would stop using Xinjiang paste. And no Canadian regulator has ever found any tin on a Canadian shelf to contain forced-labour tomatoes, which is exactly the problem, because here's what the United States did: in January 2021, US Customs banned tomato products from Xinjiang at the border outright. Canada passed a forced-labour import ban in 2020 too. The publicly reported enforcement record since then, per the Globe and Mail: two shipments ever blocked. Neither was tomatoes. This spring the US Trade Representative wrote, quote, it does not appear that Canada is effectively enforcing its forced labor import prohibition. And one Canadian labelling rule ties this whole section together. Under federal regulation, the country of origin on an imported canned product, quote, refers to the country where the product was packaged. Paste concentrated on the other side of the world, packed in Italy, is lawfully Product of Italy on a Canadian shelf. The label tells you where the can was filled. It does not promise you where the tomato grew.

So that's the game board: a legal floor, a secret grade, a two-town industry, and a label that means less than you think. Now we're going through nine brands, in order of who owns the most shelf, and by the end you'll know which tins are actually Canadian, which are actually Italian, and which are just wearing a costume. If your cupboard has ever contained any of these cans, the like button is how this reaches the next Canadian standing in that aisle, and it genuinely helps more than you know. Let's get into it.

Number one. Unico.

The most Italian-looking name in the aisle. And here is the first inversion of this video: Unico is one of the most Canadian products in the entire grocery store. The brand was founded in Toronto in 1917 by Edward Pasquale to serve the city's growing Italian community, and since 1997 it has been owned by Sun-Brite Foods of Kingsville, Ontario, a family company. The tomatoes are contracted from Southwestern Ontario farms, by the company's own account from Harrow, Chatham, Leamington and Wallaceburg, and canned in Ruthven, Essex County. Italian name, Canadian family, Canadian fields, Canadian cannery. Hold on to the name Sun-Brite, because the story of who owns it is the ending of this video, and it's the best thing in it. At the time of research, a 796 millilitre can of Unico diced ran 2.99 at Metro, about 38 cents per 100 millilitres.

Number two. Aylmer.

Your grandmother's brand. The Aylmer name was registered on June 16th, 1881, by seven businessmen in Aylmer, Ontario, which makes it one of the oldest food brands in the country, older than the light bulb in your kitchen. Today, Conagra Brands of Chicago owns it. American-owned. But read what Conagra's own Canadian website says: field to can in just 24 hours, Aylmer products use tomatoes grown in Ontario, Canada. Ontario tomatoes, canned in Dresden, Ontario, under an American flag of ownership. And then read the next line on that same corporate page, because it's the label test of the whole video, in the company's own words: except for Aylmer Fire Roasted tomatoes and Aylmer Crushed tomatoes, which are grown in California. Same brand, same shelf, two different countries in the can, and the only way to know is the fine print. The heritage sting: the town of Aylmer lost its own canning factory generations ago. The company rolled its last cans there in 1959 and the building was demolished in 1969. The name outlived the place.

Number three. Hunt's.

The big American brand. Except, in Canada, it mostly isn't. Conagra's Canadian pages describe Hunt's tomatoes as grown by real Canadian farmers in open-air farm fields across Southwestern Ontario, packed within 24 hours in Dresden, Ontario, and made in Canada since 1903. Read that again. The tin that says Hunt's, the name Canadians assume is the American option, is by the company's own description one of the most Canadian cans on the shelf. And here's the reveal that reorganizes the whole aisle: Hunt's and Aylmer come out of the same Dresden plant. One factory, about 425 workers in fresh-pack season, processing on the order of 140,000 tons of Ontario tomatoes a year, filling the heritage Canadian brand and the classic American brand side by side on the same lines. You've been standing in the aisle choosing between Canada and America, and the choice, for these two, is between two labels from one building in Chatham-Kent.

Number four. Primo.

The other pillar of the Italian-Canadian pantry, founded in Toronto in the mid-1950s by Primo Poloniato, passed through Pillsbury, Nabisco and Kraft, and bought back into Canadian family hands in August 2006 by, once again, Sun-Brite of Kingsville. So the two great rival Italian brands of every Canadian childhood, Unico and Primo, are today owned by the same Essex County family. The rivalry on the shelf is a family reunion in the boardroom. And Primo gives us the perfect bridge to the premium end of the aisle, because Primo sells a can labelled San Marzano Type tomatoes, at 2.99 at the time of research. Type. Remember that word, it's about to matter a great deal.

Number five. The store brands. No Name, Compliments, Selection, Great Value.

The yellow can, and its cousins. At the time of research: Selection diced at 1.79, Great Value at 1.97, No Name around 2 dollars, Compliments at 2 dollars. That's 22 to 29 cents per 100 millilitres, against 38 for the name brands and, as you're about to see, up to 94 for the imports. A 4x price range for products governed by the same 50 percent federal floor. Are the cheap ones Canadian? Here's what we can verify: Metro's listing for Selection diced tomatoes says Product of Canada, prepared exclusively from Canadian tomatoes. For the others, use the test from the top of this video: flip the can and read whether it says Prepared in Canada for, or Imported for. We're not going to claim who packs them, because no grocer discloses it, and this channel doesn't guess. What we will say is on the public record from that 2021 CBC Marketplace investigation: the Italian supplier reported to have bought Xinjiang paste was described as making store-brand tomato products for Loblaws and Sobeys at the time. Loblaw responded that it, quote, shares concern about labour conditions throughout the global supply chain and takes these matters very seriously, and the supplier said it would stop using Xinjiang paste. That was paste, then, under those hedged words, and nothing since has been found by any regulator. But it's exactly why the one-line label test matters more on the cheapest cans than anywhere else.

And since we're in the cheap aisle, one absurd little tax fact as a bonus, because this channel collects them. Groceries are tax-free, and canned tomatoes are groceries, no surprise there. But the Canada Revenue Agency's basic-groceries memorandum contains a special carve-out for the classic 540 millilitre can of tomato juice. Single-serving drinks under 600 millilitres are normally taxable, but the CRA specifically ruled the steel can of tomato juice tax-free anyway, because a can without a built-in opening device, in the agency's reasoning, is not marketed as a single serving. Your tomato juice is tax-exempt because you need a can opener. Somewhere in Ottawa, someone wrote that down, and honestly, they were right.

Number six. Mutti.

The premium Italian, and the one brand the internet agrees on. Mutti is a genuine Italian family company, founded in 1899 outside Parma, run by the family into a fourth generation, and its cans state 100 percent Italian tomatoes. The declared ingredient list on its peeled tomatoes, as captured at the time of research, is two items long: tomatoes and tomato juice. At 4.79 to 5.49 a can, you're paying roughly double the domestic brands, and unlike most premiums in this series, you're at least getting exactly what the label says, from where it says. If you want Italian tomatoes in Canada, this is what actually-Italian looks like. Just know what you're paying for: geography and reputation, priced at 60 to 69 cents per 100 millilitres against Ontario's 25.

Number seven. Cirio.

The other storied Italian name, founded by Francesco Cirio in 1856, and here's its modern twist: since 2004, Cirio belongs to Conserve Italia, a cooperative of more than 14,000 Italian farmers. Not a conglomerate, an actual farmer co-op, one of the largest in Europe. Of all the ownership stories in this video, the fancy Italian import having the most farmer-owned structure on the shelf is not the one anyone guesses.

Number eight. Emma and Aurora.

The Italian-grocer aisle heavyweights, and the mirror image of Unico. Emma is the house brand of Jan K. Overweel Limited, a Canadian importing family business going back to 1953. Aurora was founded in the mid-1950s by a Sicilian immigrant, Nunzio Tumino, who started by helping Italian newcomers find the ingredients that reminded them of home, and today it's owned by the Pulla family of Mississauga, describing itself as the largest Italian dry-food importer in Canada. So hold the two mirrors up: Unico is an Italian name on a fully Canadian tomato. Emma and Aurora are Canadian-owned companies selling fully Italian tomatoes. The flag on the label and the flag on the ownership papers point in opposite directions, in both directions at once. In this aisle, the branding tells you nothing. Only the fine print does.

Number nine. The San Marzano tier.

The prestige cans, 6.29 to 7.49 at the time of research, up to 94 cents per 100 millilitres, nearly four times the price of a store brand. San Marzano is supposed to mean something precise: a protected designation of origin, DOP, for tomatoes grown in the volcanic soil of the Agro Sarnese-Nocerino, between Naples and Salerno, protected since 1996. Genuine DOP cans carry the consortium seals and an individual certification number on the tin. And the rules say certified San Marzano comes whole or in fillets only. Which hands you a detector you can use in the aisle today: a San Marzano that is diced, crushed or puréed cannot be DOP. It's the name without the certificate. Back in 2011, the president of the San Marzano consortium himself said that, at maximum, 5 percent of tomatoes sold in the US as San Marzanos were real San Marzanos. His claim, about that market, back then, but the consortium seals exist precisely because the name gets borrowed. Right now, on Canadian shelves, you'll find genuine DOP cans, Pastene, whose site notes each can is individually stamped by the consortium, Allessia, Favuzzi, Emma's DOP line, sitting near cans that just say San Marzano, and Primo's honestly-labelled San Marzano Type at 2.99. The premium for the certified name over the Type is more than double. Check for the seal and the serial number, or save the four dollars.

Quick reality check before the ending, because this video owes you the fair verdicts too. We searched the federal recall database and couldn't find a single recall of a plain tin of tomatoes. Not one. The tin itself has about the cleanest record in the grocery store; the games in this aisle are played on labels and ownership papers, not in the food. There has, in fact, been exactly one canned-tomato conviction in recent Canadian history, and it's the exception that proves where the games are played: in 2017, Thomas Canning of Maidstone, Ontario was fined 40,000 dollars under the Food and Drugs Act after the CFIA determined it had, quote, falsely labelled some of its regular canned tomato products as organic canned tomato products. Not bad tomatoes. A false word on a label. That company entered receivership the same year. The label, once again, is the entire battlefield. And the money side: Statistics Canada has tracked the average price of exactly this product, the 796 millilitre can, for years. In 2017 it averaged a dollar thirty-three. This July: two dollars and twenty-seven cents. Up roughly 65 percent, with the single biggest jump in 2023. The humble tin quietly became one of the fastest-climbing staples in the store, and unlike almost everything else this channel covers, it dodged every tariff list: canned tomatoes enter duty-free from both Italy and the US, they're not on Canada's counter-tariff lists, and the new 10 percent canned-vegetable surtax covers corn, peas and beans, not tomatoes. Nobody taxed this price rise into existence. Keep that in mind next time a shelf tag blames somebody. The one tariff that does touch this aisle is sneakier: Sun-Brite has said it imports most of its actual cans, the metal, from the United States, and the company warned publicly that steel and aluminum tariffs could add 25 to 40 cents to a can. The Ontario tomato is safe. The tin it lives in is caught in the trade war.

And the farmers filling those tins have their own untold story. Ontario's processing tomato growers negotiate their prices through a marketing board, and in 2017 that system nearly broke: when the province pushed deregulation and a processor refused to bargain, the agriculture minister fired the growers' board outright and installed a trustee to settle that year's contracts. The rules were rewritten, and today some growers negotiate alone while others still bargain as a group, and this year's tomato price came in about 5 to 6 percent lower than last year's, in the growers' association's own words. A record harvest, a shrinking price, three remaining canneries. That's the math under every 2 dollar tin.

Now the ending, and it's the reason this video exists. Canada grows world-class tomatoes. Last year, Ontario farmers harvested a record crop, roughly 671,000 tons of processing tomatoes worth nearly 100 million dollars at the farm gate, from a belt of land in the country's deep southwest that has been doing this since before your grandparents were born. Canada's first cannery opened in Picton in 1882. Prince Edward County alone once ran 75 canneries. All gone. The company behind Aylmer grew into the world's second-largest fruit and vegetable canner before being sold to Del Monte in 1956. Simcoe, Ontario once made one of every two food cans in this country. Closed, 1991. Heinz left Leamington in 2014 and 740 people lost their jobs in a town whose water tower is shaped like a tomato, a town that had literally built a giant tomato as its tourist booth in 1961 and calls itself the Tomato Capital of Canada. The tomato festival that grew out of the old Heinz company picnics faded away after the company left. But the Big Tomato itself was restored in 2024 for the town's 150th anniversary, which tells you something about what this crop means there: the jobs can leave, the festival can end, and they will still repaint the giant tomato. And what's left, after 140 years of building and 40 years of closing, is essentially three plants: the rescued Heinz site in Leamington, the Conagra plant in Dresden filling Aylmer and Hunt's cans with Ontario tomatoes, and a cannery in Ruthven owned by a man named Onorio Iacobelli. He was an Italian immigrant who met his wife Lina working on a local canning line. In 1973 they bought the plant. In their first season, in the company's own telling, everything was done by hand, we had 30 ladies peeling tomatoes. Then in 1997 they bought Unico, and in 2006 they bought Primo, and today the family that started on the peeling line owns both of the Italian brands every Canadian grew up with, and runs what's described as Canada's largest tomato processor. Somewhere in your street, this month, an Italian-Canadian family is filling a garage with propane burners and beer bottles for tomato day, la giornata della passata, the way thousands of families across this country still do every September. Nearly half a million Italians came to Canada through Pier 21 between 1928 and 1971, and they brought the tradition with them: bushels of Roma tomatoes, the hand-cranked mill, reused beer bottles with a basil leaf sealed into every one. The pantry brands in this video, Unico in 1917, Primo and Aurora in the fifties, exist because those families demanded shelves that tasted like home. That instinct, that the tomato in the jar should be yours, from here, made by people you know, is the most Canadian thing in this entire aisle. The industry almost died. A cannery family from Essex County is most of the reason it didn't.
