Canada's number one tuna brand — the one that's been in your cupboard your whole life — hasn't been Canadian-owned for six years. The man who ran its American sister company went to prison for fixing the price of canned tuna. And as of one minute past midnight this morning, the day this video goes up, a brand-new fifty percent tariff hits some of the steel cans in this aisle — not the fish, the cans. We'll get to exactly which ones, because the details matter.

But here's the number that stopped us. We went looking for the last place in Canada that still puts tuna in a can. We found exactly one. It's not in Toronto. It's not owned by a grocery giant. It's a smokehouse on Vancouver Island that started in a man's backyard in 1961 — and today it's majority-owned by five First Nations.

One famous can on this shelf is stamped "proudly Canadian owned" — and that's true — but flip it over and the fish inside crossed an ocean to get here. Another brand celebrated a hundred years of Canadian canning and then quietly stopped selling tuna altogether. And one store-brand can in this lineup has an extra ingredient the others don't — it's perfectly legal, it's printed right there in the list, and almost nobody has ever read it.

Do you remember when tuna was the most dependable thing in the whole kitchen? The can opener that lived in the same drawer for thirty years. Tuna sandwiches on soft white bread, cut in triangles, at every church basement and curling rink in the country. Tuna casserole with crushed potato chips on top when the month ran long. That can never let you down. It never went bad, it never changed, and it cost less than a stamp costs now. Here's something you maybe never noticed: the Canadian can never even shrank. It's still 170 grams — the same six imperial ounces your mother bought — while the standard American can quietly slid down to five ounces. The can stayed loyal. The question we're asking today is: did the companies?

So today we're going through nine tuna brands you'll find in any Canadian grocery store, in order, starting with the most popular — and for every single can we're asking two questions. Who owns this now? And where was it actually canned? Fair warning: nobody publishes a sales chart for tuna in this country, so we've ordered the store brands by the size of the chains that sell them. We're not doctors and we're not activists. We read every label. That's all we do. If you want more of that, the like button is free, it takes one second, and it genuinely tells YouTube to show this to more Canadians who grew up on these cans. Let's open the cupboard.

**Number one: Clover Leaf.**

If Canada has a tuna brand, it's this one. Clover Leaf's own website says it plainly: quote, "the #1 Seafood brand in Canada." Their label says "Most Trusted Canned Seafood Brand," citing the 2026 BrandSpark Canadian Trust Study — that's their claim, from their survey partner, but honestly, ask your neighbours, it rings true. The company traces its story back to 1871 and a salmon cannery on the Fraser River in British Columbia — that's Clover Leaf's own telling — and for decades it sat inside BC Packers, which the George Weston family bought in 1962. The same Weston name from the bread aisle and the grocery store. For a generation, Canada's fish, Canada's cannery, Canada's grocery family.

Then the passing around started. In the late 1990s Weston sold the brand to an American food company. That company was bought, sold again to management, merged in 2004 with an American tuna giant you may know — Bumble Bee. Then a US private equity firm bought the whole thing, sold it to a British private equity firm, and here's where the story stops being ordinary corporate shuffling.

In May 2017, the United States Department of Justice announced that Bumble Bee Foods — Clover Leaf's American sister company — had agreed to plead guilty to a felony. The charge, in the DOJ's own words: a conspiracy to fix the prices of, quote, "shelf-stable tuna fish, such as canned and pouch tuna, sold in the United States." The fine: twenty-five million US dollars. The next year StarKist agreed to plead guilty too, and in 2019 a judge ordered StarKist to pay one hundred million dollars — the statutory maximum. The third big American brand, Chicken of the Sea, says it blew the whistle and got leniency for cooperating; it was never charged. And in December 2019, a San Francisco jury convicted Bumble Bee's former chief executive, Christopher Lischewski, personally. The court found he was a leader of a conspiracy that affected over six hundred million dollars of canned tuna sales, and it sentenced him to forty months in prison and a hundred-thousand-dollar fine. Prison. For the price of tuna.

Now let's be very careful and very fair, because it matters. Every one of those charges was about tuna sold in the United States, by American companies. Clover Leaf — the Canadian company — was never charged with anything, by anyone. A Canadian class action later claimed the conspiracy reached our shelves, and Ontario courts threw it out, saying the plaintiffs hadn't shown a basis in fact that it extended to Canada — a ruling upheld on appeal in 2024. And we found no record of any Competition Bureau case about canned tuna. That's the whole picture: convictions in America, nothing proven about Canada, in either direction.

But the scandal broke the parent company. Days before its former CEO's conviction, Bumble Bee filed for bankruptcy in the United States — and its Canadian affiliate, Connors Bros. Clover Leaf Seafoods Company, filed for creditor protection here the same day. In January 2020 the whole thing — Bumble Bee, Clover Leaf, all of it — was bought for 928 million US dollars by FCF Co., Ltd. FCF is a seafood conglomerate from Taiwan. It calls itself the largest tuna supplier in the Western Pacific. So the number one tuna brand in Canada, the one with the Fraser River on its heritage page, answers today to Kaohsiung, Taiwan.

The can itself? Genuinely clean. Flaked light tuna in water: "Skipjack tuna, water, sea salt." Three ingredients. 130 milligrams of sodium, six percent of your daily value, per half can. Clover Leaf's website even says every can carries a production code you can trace back to the factory and date it was made — they call it Trace My Catch, and we'll type this can's code into their own tool on camera and see exactly what it tells us, and what it doesn't. Then we'll flip the can and read the "Product of" line out loud, because the website doesn't say where it's canned — and the can, by law, does. Around two and a half dollars at Walmart at the time of research. One footnote from the bankruptcy files that stuck with us: when Bumble Bee filed, reporting said it still owed 17 million dollars of that price-fixing fine to the US Department of Justice. The fine outlived the company that paid it.

**Number two: Ocean's.**

Greenpeace once called Ocean's Canada's second biggest tuna brand, and the company's website says something Clover Leaf can't: "PROUDLY CANADIAN OWNED." And that's true. Ocean Brands of Richmond, BC belongs to the Jim Pattison Group — the Vancouver empire of Jimmy Pattison, the billionaire who started with one car dealership in 1961 and ended up owning Save-On-Foods, Ripley's Believe It or Not, and Guinness World Records. Yes — the tuna and the world's biggest ball of twine, same owner. It's one of the largest private companies in Canada, and it's headquartered here, taxed here, run from here.

So Ocean's wins, right? Almost. Pick up the can. "Skipjack tuna, water, sea salt" — three ingredients, clean as can be, MSC certified, dolphin friendly on the front, and a Certified B Corp badge. But now find the "Product of" line, because the fish is not canned in Canada. Ocean's own website says it sources from North America and Asia, and we'll read the origin line on this can out loud on camera, because the law makes them print it: section 266 of the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations says imported packaged fish must name the foreign country of origin. Canadian-owned, yes — and good for them. Canadian-made? Read the can. Hold that thought, because the reason there's almost nowhere left in Canada to can a fish is coming later in this video, and it involves this same company family. About $2.99 at Save-On-Foods — a Pattison store, buying Pattison tuna — at the time of research.

**Number three: Great Value.**

Walmart's can, and often the cheapest branded thing in the aisle — $1.23 at the time of research. The list is the same honest three: skipjack tuna, water, salt. Here's what the label won't tell you: who actually packed it. It just says imported by Walmart Canada. No cannery name, no company. That's legal and completely normal for store brands — and it's why we always say the quiet part: the recall notice is the only time a store brand introduces you to its real maker. Just this past January, a recalled brand of flavoured tuna revealed its actual importers in the CFIA notice — names you'd never see on a shelf. Nothing like that names Walmart's packer today, so we simply don't know whose factory this came from. What we do know is the fine-print country line, and we'll read it on camera.

And one law you can use tonight: because this tuna is packed in water, Canadian rules require the net quantity to be declared as drained weight — the fish alone, not the water. Tuna packed in oil? Declared with the oil included. Same aisle, two different maths. Once you know that, you'll never read a tuna label the same way.

**Number four: No Name and President's Choice.**

The yellow can. Loblaw is Canada's biggest grocer, so by shelf space alone this may pass through more Canadian kitchens than almost anything else here. Ingredients: "Skipjack tuna, water, salt." $1.29 at No Frills at the time of research — honestly, in 2026, that's one of the last true grocery bargains. StatCan's average price for a 170-gram can of tuna was $1.44 when the series started in January 2017 and $1.84 this past July — up about 28 percent in nine and a half years. Compare that to canned salmon, up more than 75 percent in the same window. Tuna has quietly been the best-behaved price in the canned aisle — and we'll say that plainly, because when a number is good news, you deserve to hear it too. Though we'll note one thing: that $1.84 in July was the highest month ever recorded, a twenty-cent jump in a single month, right as the tariff war heated up. Correlation, not proof. Keep your receipts.

President's Choice sits one shelf up in the same stores, same owner, fancier label. Both cans: imported by Loblaws. Who cans them? Again — not printed, not published, and we won't guess. The most Canadian thing about the biggest-selling tuna in Canada is the retailer's name on the fine print.

While we've got the yellow can in hand, notice something missing: there's no best-before date required on it at all. Under Canada's food regulations, a date is only mandatory when a product's durable life is 90 days or less — and a properly canned tuna sails past that, because federal law says a low-acid canned food may not even be sold unless it's "commercially sterile": heated until it's free of anything that could grow in the can, spores included. That's regulation B.27.002, and it's why the tin in your emergency kit is still fine. The CFIA itself puts it plainly: a durable life date "is not an indicator of food safety, neither before nor after the date." Any date you do see printed on a tuna can is the company being helpful — or careful — not the law talking.

**Number five: Kirkland Signature.**

The Costco flat — eight cans of Solid Light tuna, skipjack, water, sea salt. And "Solid" is not a mood — it's a legal term of art. CFIA's canned tuna standard, as Ottawa last spelled it out, defines it: solid means transverse segments of fish, packed with their cut ends parallel to the ends of the can, no free fragments added. "Chunk" legally means pieces at least 1.2 centimetres in every direction. "Flaked" means smaller particles that still keep the muscle structure. That single word on the front of the can is a promise about what's inside, and a false one would be a misleading label under federal law.

While we're at Costco — the albacore question. In the US, food press has long reported that Kirkland's albacore is packed by Bumble Bee — yes, that Bumble Bee — based on an old Costco interview. That's American reporting about American cans; we couldn't confirm who packs the Canadian ones. But here's the delicious part: on costco.ca, the albacore listing isn't Kirkland at all. It's Ocean's. Even Costco Canada's white tuna shelf leads back to Jim Pattison. And "white tuna," by the way, is another legally loaded phrase — under the Safe Food for Canadians rules, only one species on Earth may be called white tuna in this country: albacore, Thunnus alalunga. The regulation even sets how pale the meat must be — its reflectance is measured against magnesium oxide, basically pure white powder. There is a federal document that defines tuna by how much light it bounces. We read it so you don't have to. One honest, attributed note while we're here: Health Canada's mercury guidance says most Canadians don't need to be concerned about canned tuna, and that its advice for children and pregnant women applies to canned albacore — white — tuna specifically, not canned light tuna. That's Health Canada's advice, not ours; it's on their website, and we'll link it below.

**Number six: Compliments and Selection.**

The Sobeys can and the Metro can. Compliments: skipjack tuna, water, salt — the standard three, with a Dolphin Safe mark. Quick legal aside on that: Canada has no defined federal standard for "dolphin friendly." The claim is policed only under the general ban on false or misleading labels, and the company has to be able to back it up if inspectors ask. It may well be true — just know there's no government checklist behind the logo.

Now Metro's Selection can. Read the list with me, because this is the one: "Skipjack tuna, water, salt, vegetable broth — pea extract, carrot extract." Vegetable broth. In tuna. Now, to be completely fair: that is perfectly legal — the federal compositional standard for prepared fish specifically allows vegetable broth — it's declared right on the label, and broth in tuna is common practice in canneries around the world. This is not a scandal. It's a reading test. Two cans, side by side, same price range: one is fish, water, salt; one is fish, water, salt and a broth you probably never knew you were buying. We just think you should get to choose on purpose. About $1.49 at Metro at the time of research.

And since we're comparing ingredient lists, let's do the one that really tells the story of where this aisle is heading: flavoured tuna. Ocean's makes a SnacKit — a little tuna cup with crackers, ready to eat, 18 grams of protein, says the box. Read the cup's list with me: skipjack tuna, water, vinegar, sugar, modified tapioca starch, soybean oil, egg powder, modified waxy maize starch, sea salt, titanium dioxide, xanthan gum, guar gum. Twelve ingredients — two modified starches, two gums, and titanium dioxide — in the same aisle where the plain can gets by on three. We're not saying a word about any of them; they're all legal and all declared. We're just counting. Three. Twelve. You do the arithmetic on what you're paying for.

Speaking of arithmetic — here's the new law of the aisle. As of January 1st this year, Canada's front-of-package rules are fully in force: a food high in sodium must wear a black-and-white warning symbol right on the front. For a can of tuna the trigger is 15 percent of the daily value — that's 345 milligrams, measured per serving or per the 55-gram reference amount, whichever is greater. Plain tuna in water? About 130 milligrams — 38 percent of the way to the line, nowhere close. Now take Clover Leaf's Spicy Thai Chili flavoured can: 300 milligrams in the little 85-gram tin, printed right on it. Still under the line — no symbol — but that's 87 percent of the way there. Plain tuna strolls past the new law. Flavoured tuna is walking a tightrope over it. And that gap, between the fish and the flavouring, is the entire modern grocery store in one shelf.

**Number seven: Rio Mare.**

The elegant Italian one, in olive oil, and at the time of research about $7.62 for three little 80-gram tins at Walmart — per gram, the most expensive mainstream tuna on the shelf. Rio Mare belongs to Bolton Group of Milan, which says it's the number one tuna in Italy, and its Canadian site claims number one in olive-oil tuna here — their claim. Two things worth knowing. First: remember the drained-weight law from earlier — oil-packed tuna is declared with the oil included, so compare fish to fish, not can to can. Second: Bolton also owns Wild Planet — the earthy, sustainable-looking American brand in the natural foods aisle. The glossy Italian tin and the crunchy eco can: same owner in Milan. Different costumes, one wardrobe.

**Number eight: Gold Seal.**

Here's the one that genuinely surprised us. Gold Seal is the other Pattison seafood brand — a hundred years old in 2019, a name your parents trusted. We went to list their tuna and found something strange: there isn't any. Gold Seal's own product page today shows salmon, mackerel, sardines — and no canned tuna at all. A century-old Canadian canned-fish brand, and tuna has quietly left the lineup. If you've got a Gold Seal tuna can in the cupboard, check the date code — the brand has moved on, and nobody sent a memo.

Sandwich, casserole, or straight from the can? And does anyone out there remember working at a BC cannery — Steveston, Prince Rupert, anywhere? Because here's what happened to canning in this country. A hundred and thirty years ago, Steveston, BC had so many salmon canneries they called it Salmonopolis — the biggest of them ran from 1894 to 1979 and was nicknamed the Monster Cannery; it's a national historic site now. You can visit. That's the point — you can only visit. In 2015, Canfisco — the Pattison fishing company — ended canning at its Prince Rupert plant, once called the world's largest cannery, with more than 500 jobs affected. Their executive was honest about why: labour costs, and competition from Alaska. "It is tough to compete." When that line shut down, industrial fish canning in Canada essentially ended.

Essentially. Not entirely.

**The winner: Raincoast Trading.**

In 1961 — the very same year Jim Pattison bought his first car lot — a man named Armand St. Jean started smoking oysters in his backyard in Nanaimo and selling them to bar patrons in little bags. He called them smudgies. That backyard operation became St. Jean's Cannery and Smokehouse, and today it is, in its own words and everyone else's, the last cannery of its kind left in British Columbia — and by its own brand's account, the only tuna canning operation in Canada. Full stop. One left.

And who owns the last one? Since 2015–16, majority ownership belongs to a partnership of five Nuu-chah-nulth First Nations from the west coast of Vancouver Island. The people who fished these waters first now own the last place in Canada that still cans the catch. Their retail brand in grocery stores is Raincoast Trading — started in 1995 by a multi-generation BC fishing family in Delta, and part of the St. Jean's family since 2013.

Pick up the can. The ingredient list on Raincoast's albacore is the shortest in this entire video: "Albacore tuna, sea salt." Two ingredients. No water, no oil — they cook it once, in the can, in its own juices, so there's nothing to drain and nothing padding the scale. Pole and line caught in the Pacific Northwest, packed by hand, MSC certified. Now, the honest part, because we promised you honest: it's about $9.46 at the time of research — four to seven times the price of the store brands, for a 150-gram can. If the $1.29 yellow can is what the budget allows this week, buy it with your head held high; the label's clean and Health Canada has no quarrel with it. But if you've ever wanted to vote with four dollars for the idea that Canada should still make something — this is the can. This is the last one.

One more thing before we go, because it starts today. At 12:01 this morning — September 8, 2026 — Canada's new counter-tariffs came into force, and steel food cans made in the United States now carry a 50 percent tariff at the border. Be precise with us: that's the empty can, not the fish — the tuna itself, mostly from Thailand and Vietnam, isn't touched by this, and cans made in Asia aren't either. But the earlier break that sheltered food-packaging steel expired in January, and can makers, like the rest of us, pass their costs down the line. Add the quiet math already in the aisle — Thai tuna pays a 7 percent import duty while Vietnamese tuna enters free under the Pacific trade deal, which is exactly why your can's fine print has been drifting from one country to the other — and the humble tuna can is suddenly a little lesson in world economics, sitting next to the crackers.

So: nine brands. The number one brand answers to Taiwan. The proudly-Canadian-owned one imports the fish. The store brands won't say who makes them. The Italian one owns the hippie one. The hundred-year-old one quietly gave up on tuna. And the last can actually made in Canada comes from a backyard smokehouse in Nanaimo, owned by five First Nations, with two ingredients and no apologies. Nobody in this video broke a Canadian law. Almost nobody told you the whole story either. That's why we read the labels — because the truth is printed right there, in very small letters, and small letters are our favourite kind.
