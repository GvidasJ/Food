# SCRIPT — CEREAL

TITLE: We Investigated 10 "Whole Grain" Cereals In Canada (Only 3 Are ACTUALLY Worth Buying)

ALTERNATES:
- Canadians Must AVOID These 10 "Whole Grain" Cereals (Only 3 Are ACTUALLY Worth Buying)
- We Investigated Every Famous Cereal In Canada (One Lists Sugar As Its FIRST Ingredient)
- Your Childhood Cereal Just Got A Warning Label (We Investigated All 10)

---

That box your family grabs every single morning is sweetened with two, three — in one case five — different kinds of sugar. On an American box, that fact gets to hide: each sweetener is listed under its own name, scattered down the ingredient list so no single one catches your eye. On a Canadian box, it isn't allowed to hide anymore — since 2021, the law drags every sugar into one bracket near the top of the list — and the strange thing is, almost nobody knows they're looking at it. So we read every famous cereal box in the country. That's all we did — we read the labels and the regulations behind them — and what we found is going to rearrange your cupboard. We found the childhood cereals that now legally have to wear a black-and-white warning symbol on the front, and the ones that escape it by the math. We found one beloved cereal whose first ingredient — first, ahead of any grain — is the word "Sugars." We found out who quietly bought Tony the Tiger this year, and trust us, you will not guess. And at the end, three cereals actually worth your money — including a hundred-year-old Canadian classic that died, came back from the dead, and is milled today by the oldest flour mill on the continent. Ten boxes, biggest shelf presence first. Pour yourself a bowl of something and let's read.

First, sixty seconds on the one piece of law this whole video runs on, because it's the best label rule you've never heard of. In the United States, a cereal can be sweetened with sugar, corn syrup, molasses and honey, and list each one separately — four small entries instead of one big one, each landing comfortably down the list. Canada shut that down. Since the labelling reform that took full effect in 2021, every sugar-based ingredient must be gathered into one entry: the word "Sugars," followed by brackets naming each one, placed in the list according to their combined weight. One word, one position, no scattering. Want to see it flex? Kellogg's Vector — which, look closely at the box, isn't legally sold as a cereal at all in Canada but as a "meal replacement," complete with label directions prescribing exactly 55 grams of cereal and 200 millilitres of skim milk — lists: "Sugars (sugar, corn syrup, blackstrap molasses, honey, malted barley syrup)." Five sweeteners, one bracket, third ingredient. The gym-bag cereal is the single best demonstration of the sugar-grouping law in the entire store. On an American-style label those five would be spread across the panel like camouflage. On a Canadian label they stand in a police line-up. One more difference while we're here: American panels show an "added sugars" line; Canadian panels show total sugars with a percent daily value. So here's your tool for this whole video — take the sugars number on any box, divide by four, and that's teaspoons per serving. Keep that in your head. We're going in.

Number one. Honey Nut Cheerios — the biggest shelf presence in the Canadian cereal aisle, the yellow box with the bee. Read the Canadian label: whole grain oats first — genuinely — and then, second ingredient: "Sugars (sugar, honey, golden syrup)." There's the bracket. Three sweeteners, one line, silver medal position. The label says twelve grams of sugars per one-cup serving — three teaspoons per bowl, and do the kitchen version of that once in your life: spoon three teaspoons of sugar into a dry bowl and look at it. That's the bee's breakfast, before milk, before the second pour. And there's always a second pour. And here's what that number now triggers: under the front-of-package rules that became fully mandatory this January, a cereal in this density class is judged against a ten-gram line — and because the stated serving is thirty-seven grams, bigger than the reference amount, the full twelve grams counts. Twelve is more than ten. That's the black-and-white magnifying glass — "High in sugars" — legally required on the front of the box. Now slide your eyes one box over, to plain original Cheerios: one gram of sugar. Twelve grams versus one, same brand, same bee-adjacent shelf. And one more thing the two boxes share: read the vitamin list. Iron and four B-vitamins. Now look up the American Cheerios box online: calcium, vitamin C, vitamin A, B12, vitamin D, zinc. Same brand, different chemistry — because Canadian law keeps a closed list of exactly eight nutrients that may be added to breakfast cereal, and vitamins A, C, D and B12 aren't on it. The American box isn't more generous; the Canadian box isn't lazier. It's the law, and almost nobody knows it exists: a federal table lists exactly which foods may have which nutrients added, and for breakfast cereal the permitted list is thiamine, niacin, B6, folic acid, pantothenic acid, magnesium, iron and zinc — eight, full stop. And if you're now wondering why some Canadian boxes do list vitamin D — sharp eyes — that's the fine print of the fine print: Health Canada has been issuing temporary, product-by-product permission slips for vitamin D in cereal since 2012 while it gets around to modernizing the rule. Some boxes carry the permission slip. Others don't. The law is the default; the exceptions are literally temporary. As for where your Cheerios are made — General Mills' own supply-chain story follows oats from a Manitoba farm to a mill in Minneapolis to a plant in Iowa. Canadian oats, American cereal. Check the fine print on your box; we'll come back to that idea at the end.

Number two. Frosted Flakes. Fourteen grams of sugars per cup — three and a half teaspoons — which clears the ten-gram line with room to spare: magnifying glass, by law, on the front. The ingredient list is short and honest in that new Canadian way: milled corn, then "Sugars (sugar, corn and barley malt extract)," salt — and then a lovely detail: "riboflavin (for colour)." The golden glow of a frosted flake is vitamin B2. But the label isn't the story here. The story is who owns the tiger. For a hundred years Kellogg's meant Battle Creek, Michigan — and London, Ontario, where Kellogg's of Canada was born in 1924 and made Corn Flakes for ninety years. That plant made some twenty-seven different cereals at its height, and it closed in December 2014, roughly five hundred jobs gone; a worker slipped a handwritten note into one of the final boxes, and it surfaced months later in a kitchen in Timmins — a message in a bottle from a dying factory, reported across the country by the CBC. The site is an entertainment complex now called 100 Kellogg Lane. Then in 2023, the Kellogg empire split itself in two — snacks one way, North American cereal the other — and this past February, the cereal half was swallowed whole: **Ferrero — the Italian family company behind Nutella — completed its three-point-one-billion-US-dollar purchase of WK Kellogg.** Tony the Tiger works for the Nutella people now. Your Frosted Flakes, Froot Loops, Rice Krispies and Corn Flakes are sold in Canada by a subsidiary of an Italian chocolate company. One genuinely good Canadian note to balance the ledger: Kellogg's Belleville, Ontario plant is not only open — it's being handed more production while American plants close. Your Mini-Wheats are made in Ontario. Hold that thought for number five.

Number three. Froot Loops. We're going to read you the first ingredient of the Canadian box, and we promise we are not editing it: "Sugars (sugar, maltodextrin)." First. Ahead of any flour, any grain, anything. That's the grouping law doing exactly what it was written to do — when the sweeteners are combined, there's nowhere left to hide, and in this box they outweigh everything else. Fourteen grams per cup: magnifying glass, by law. Meanwhile the store listing for this same cereal boasts it's "made with whole grains and is a source of fibre." Both things are printed about one box. You now know which line to believe. But here's the genuinely fascinating part — the colours. The Canadian Froot Loops rainbow comes from concentrated carrot juice, watermelon juice, blueberry juice, huito juice, annatto and turmeric. The American version has used the synthetic dyes — Red 40, Yellow 5 and friends — and here's the twist: those synthetic colours are perfectly legal in Canada. Nobody made Kellogg's do this. The Canadian market got vegetable-juice loops because the company chose it, years before the American arm announced — just this month — that it's stripping the artificial colours out by the end of 2026. For once, the Canadian box was ahead of the law instead of hiding behind it.

If you just checked your own cupboard mid-video — and you did — hit the like button before you pour anything, because it genuinely helps these investigations reach the next Canadian standing in the cereal aisle wondering why there's a magnifying glass on their childhood.

Number four. Lucky Charms. The one box in this video that reads like it time-travelled from before the reform. The transcription we checked lists whole grain oats, then sugar, corn starch, corn syrup, dextrose — plus gelatin, "natural and artificial flavour," and this phrase, printed plainly: "Colour (Includes Tartrazine)." Tartrazine — a synthetic yellow — is legal in Canada, and after the Froot Loops story you now know that's a choice, not a requirement. Twelve grams of sugars per cup — magnifying-glass territory by the same math as the loops and the flakes, though as always, the front of the current box is the referee. And the price is the real magic trick: seven twenty-nine for a three-hundred-gram box at time of filming — two dollars and forty-three cents per hundred grams, the most expensive big-brand cereal we checked, roughly four times the price of Shreddies per gram. The marshmallows, it turns out, are the cheapest thing in the box; the charm was always the markup. One caveat for full honesty: the ingredient rendering we checked for this box looked like it predates the sugar-grouping rule, so read the current carton in-store — if its sugars are still scattered rather than bracketed, we'd genuinely like to see it, because that would be its own story.

Numbers five and six. Mini-Wheats and Two Scoops Raisin Bran — the serious-adult shelf. Mini-Wheats first, with credit where due: whole grain wheat genuinely first, made in Belleville, Ontario, and by the math it escapes the sugar symbol — biscuit-type cereals are judged against a higher fifteen-gram line and Mini-Wheats comes in at eleven. But read the full list, because it's four items long and one of them surprises everyone: whole grain wheat, sugars, gelatin, BHT. Gelatin — in a wheat cereal. It's what makes the frosting stick. Vegetarians have been eating it unaware for decades; the label never hid it, nobody looked. Raisin Bran's surprise is even better. The Canadian label lists its second ingredient as — quote — "Raisins (sugar, palm oil)." The raisins have their own ingredient list. The two scoops come pre-candied, glossed with sugar and palm oil before they ever meet the bran. Fourteen grams of sugars per serving, sitting one gram under the fifteen-gram line — too close for us to call either way, so do what we do: look at the front of the box. As of this January, the box itself has to tell you.

Numbers seven, eight and nine — the Quaker shelf: Life, Harvest Crunch, and the story underneath them. Life is oat flour first, sugar second, ten grams a cup. Harvest Crunch is the one to slow down on — not for the ingredients, which are practically rustic, but for the serving size: the label declares a one-hundred-gram serving, the largest we found in the entire aisle, carrying twenty-three grams of sugars — nearly six teaspoons per stated bowl. Same trick as always: the number is honest, the bowl is bigger than you think. But here's what Quaker gets deeply right, and it's the reason the shelf exists at all: Peterborough, Ontario. Quaker has milled there since 1902 — and in December 1916, a grain-dust explosion tore through that plant and killed twenty-three people, one of the worst industrial disasters in Canadian history. They rebuilt. A hundred and ten years later, PepsiCo still runs two Quaker plants in Ontario — Peterborough and Trenton — and the bulk of Quaker products on Canadian shelves come out of them. Every bag of those oats carries more Canadian manufacturing history than the rest of the aisle combined. For the record, because we report these things whether they're convenient or not: in January 2024 Quaker recalled a range of granola bars and cereals across Canada over possible Salmonella — a Class 2 recall, national, resolved through the public recall system. It's in the federal database, it was handled, and it's exactly the machinery working as designed. Companies with century-old plants still get recalls; the difference is you can look them up.

Number ten. Corn Flakes — and the cheapest lesson in this video. First, the decoder: Canada's food agency sorts cereals into two families, in writing. Rolled oats and cracked wheat "can be described as 'whole grain cereals.'" Corn flakes and puffed cereals, in the agency's own words, "are called 'refined cereals.'" And Health Canada will tell you something even stranger: because the rules let millers remove up to five percent of the kernel, even "whole wheat" flour in Canada isn't necessarily whole grain. "Multigrain" means nothing legally. So when a box of anything says "made with whole grains" — a phrase with no legal minimum — the ingredient list is the only referee. And here's the twist that redeems the retro shelf: the plain old cereals your grandparents poured mostly escape the new warning symbol entirely. Corn Flakes, three grams of sugars a cup. Rice Krispies, three. Special K, four. Plain Cheerios, one. The math flags the mascots and waves the antiques through — the plainer your childhood, the cleaner your label. Now the price lesson. Kellogg's Corn Flakes: milled corn, sugars, salt, riboflavin for colour — six dollars and twenty-nine cents for 340 grams at time of filming. Compliments Corn Flakes, same store, same day: flaked milled corn, sugars, salt, iron, riboflavin — three ninety-nine for 680 grams. That is one dollar eighty-five per hundred grams against fifty-nine cents — **the tiger-adjacent brand costs three point two times more per gram than the store brand with functionally the same recipe.** You are paying the difference in rooster. And as of February, you're paying it to Ferrero.

Before the winners, a word about the most Canadian box in the aisle. Shreddies. Invented in Niagara Falls in 1939 — originally sold under the name "Cubs," renamed Shreddies in 1941 — and still made in that same Niagara Falls plant today, a factory that's been weaving wheat since 1904. Four food ingredients: whole grain wheat, sugars, salt, BHT. Escapes the sugar symbol with ease. And at four seventy-nine for a 550-gram box at time of filming, it's the cheapest big-brand cereal per gram we found — the rare box where the Canadian icon is also the value play. The one asterisk: Shreddies hasn't been Canadian-owned in generations — it's Post Holdings of St. Louis, by way of Nabisco, Kraft and Ralcorp. The wheat is woven here; the profits aren't. In this aisle, that's as close to a love story as it gets.

Now the three actually worth buying.

Two honourable mentions first, for balance: Weetabix — whole grain wheat, a whisper of malt, two grams of sugars, five forty-nine — and plain original Cheerios at one gram, the cleanest label General Mills imports. Both fine choices. Neither is the podium, because the podium has a theme, and the theme is Canada.

Winner number three. Quaker Old Fashioned Large Flake Oats. The complete ingredient list: "Whole Grain Rolled Oats." One ingredient. One gram of naturally occurring sugars. Five twenty-nine for a full kilogram at time of filming — fifty-three cents per hundred grams, the cheapest thing in this entire investigation, from those same Ontario mills with the hundred-and-twenty-year history. Your grandmother's breakfast was one ingredient at a fifth the price of the mascots. She won. It just took the rest of us forty years of label-reading to admit it.

Winner number two. Post Shredded Wheat. The original output of that 1904 Niagara Falls plant, and the purest label in the aisle: whole grain wheat — that's it, with the package material, not the food, carrying the freshness preservative, and the label says so in exactly those words. Zero grams of sugars. Zero sodium. The box that the entire "whole grain" marketing industry is trying to sound like, sitting quietly on the bottom shelf at five forty-nine, made in Ontario for a hundred and twenty years.

And winner number one. Red River Cereal. If you're over fifty, you just felt something. Created in Manitoba in 1924 — cracked wheat, cracked rye, flax, the grains of the Red River valley — it fed Canadian prairie mornings for nearly a century until Smucker, the American owner, quietly discontinued it around 2021, citing low support. And then something happened that never happens in this industry: it came back. Arva Flour Mills — a tiny operation in Arva, Ontario that bills itself as North America's oldest continuously operating commercial flour mill — bought the brand from Smucker in June 2022 and relaunched it with the original cracked-grain recipe. Today the list reads: cracked wheat, cracked rye, cracked and whole flax. Three grains, zero games, seven ninety-nine for a 908-gram bag directly from the mill, and it celebrated its hundredth birthday under Canadian ownership again — created by a Manitoban named Gertrude Skilling in 1924, discontinued by an American conglomerate, and revived by an Ontario mill smaller than the parking lot of the factories in this video. Every other brand in this video was bought by somebody bigger. This one was rescued by somebody smaller. Buy it for the label; keep buying it for what it proves.

Now zoom out, because the whole aisle is telling one last story. Statistics Canada's food data shows the "breakfast food" category peaked in 2005 at about five and a half kilograms per Canadian per year. It's now three — below where it stood in 1960. Canadians have quietly walked away from nearly half their cereal, while the average tracked price climbed 36 percent since 2017 to four twenty for a 400-gram box — and the boxes themselves shrank: CBC documented Post's Honeycomb family size dropping from 595 grams to 525 grams in a taller, slimmer box, spotted not by a regulator but by a shopper in Edmonton who kept her old box. The company never replied to the CBC's questions. Meanwhile the same "family size" Shreddies currently exists in both 725-gram and 635-gram versions on different shelves at the same time — we're not calling that shrinkflation, we're calling it a reason to read the grams before the price tag, every single time. Fewer eaters, higher prices, smaller boxes, and — as of this January — warning symbols on the front of the sweetest survivors. And one more thing about those mascots smiling at your grandchildren: Canada proposed federal restrictions on advertising food to children, then archived the policy; the bill that would have done it died at the 2025 election. Only Quebec actually did it — a ban on all commercial advertising to kids under thirteen, on the books since 1980 and upheld by the Supreme Court. In nine provinces, the cartoon tiger on a sugar-first box is the last unregulated billboard in the store. So here's your checklist, and it fits on the back of a cereal box: find the word "Sugars" and its bracket — the law already gathered them for you; the closer to the top, the more you know. Divide the grams by four for teaspoons. Look for the magnifying glass — it's new, it's legal, and it does the math for you. Remember "whole wheat" isn't legally whole grain, corn flakes are officially a "refined cereal," and "made with whole grains" has no minimum. Compare price per hundred grams — never per box, never per mascot. And if all of it exhausts you, the one-ingredient aisle is right there: oats, shredded wheat, and a bag of resurrected Manitoba history. One last patriotic note to pour on top — and remember those Manitoba oats from the Cheerios story, the ones that ride to Minneapolis to come back as an American cereal? Here's the full picture: Canada is the largest oat exporter on earth — nearly four million tonnes grown last year, almost half of it in Saskatchewan, with 81 percent of our exports going to the United States, where Canadian oats hold a 97 percent market share. Ninety-seven. When America sits down to a healthy breakfast, it is, almost without exception, eating Canadian — it just pays a mascot for the introduction. We just had to read a few hundred labels to remember we could do the same. Tell us in the comments which cereal from your childhood survived this video — and which label genuinely surprised you; for us it was a box of loops whose first ingredient is the word "Sugars." If someone you love still pours the mascots every morning, send them this before the next grocery run. And we'll see you at the next label.
