The name on your morning coffee cup is one of the most Canadian words in the language. The company that owns it says, on its own website, that its principal executive offices are in Miami, Florida. And that's not even the strangest thing we found.

We spent this episode on fifteen restaurant chains that Canadians think of as theirs. What we found is a map almost nobody has seen drawn out. The sit-down restaurants you grew up in — Swiss Chalet, Harvey's, The Keg, St-Hubert, Montana's, East Side Mario's — all belong to one owner. Most of the food court — Thai Express, Mr. Sub, Country Style, and dozens more — rings its sales through one Montreal company. Four of Canada's privacy regulators published a finding that the Tim Hortons app tracked customers' locations as often as every few minutes, even when the app was closed. And thanks to a genuinely wonderful piece of federal tax law, buying one donut is taxed, but buying six is not.

Stick with us to the end, because this isn't a funeral. The last part of this video is the chains that are still genuinely, fully Canadian-owned — and at least one of them is the chain everyone assumes is American. We read every label. That's all we do. Today the labels are menu boards, stock exchange filings, and the fine print on a coffee cup.

The map has three big names on it, and you're going to meet them one at a time: a company called RBI, a company called Recipe, and a company called MTY — plus a fast-rising fourth we'll save for near the end. Count the connections as we go.

Number one: Tim Hortons.

Canada's largest restaurant chain — three thousand eight hundred and seventy-four stores across the country as of the last count in The Canadian Encyclopedia — and the story starts exactly where the legend says: with an actual hockey player. Tim Horton was a Toronto Maple Leafs defenceman, and in May 1964, with a Montreal businessman named Jim Charade — the co-founder history forgot — he opened the first Tim Horton doughnut shop on Ottawa Street North in Hamilton. It's still operating. The third franchisee of that struggling Hamilton store was a local police officer named Ron Joyce, who was already running a Dairy Queen on the side. In December 1966 he became Horton's equal partner for twelve thousand dollars.

Then came February 21, 1974. Horton was driving back to Buffalo after a game against the Leafs when he lost control of his car in a high-speed, single-vehicle crash in St. Catharines. The Canadian Encyclopedia records that an autopsy revealed he had been drinking, that he had struggled with alcohol and sought counselling, and that his father had died just weeks earlier. He had just turned 44. There were thirty-five stores. The following year, Joyce bought out Tim's widow Lori for one million dollars and the company Cadillac, and became sole owner. A police officer from Hamilton then built the thing that ate Canadian breakfast.

Now follow the ownership, because this is the whole episode in one brand. In 1995, Joyce sold Tim Hortons to Wendy's — the American burger chain. In 2006, under shareholder pressure, Wendy's spun it off and Tim's came home to the stock market. And in 2014, Burger King — controlled by the Brazilian investment firm 3G Capital — bought Tim Hortons in a deal reported around eleven and a half billion US dollars, financed in part by three billion dollars from Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway. Remember that name; he's coming back. Canada's Competition Bureau approved the takeover, and the combined company became Restaurant Brands International: Tim Hortons, Burger King, Popeyes, and Firehouse Subs, nearly forty-nine billion dollars in annual system sales. At launch, RBI said the new global company would be based in Canada. Today, RBI's own website says its principal executive offices are in Miami, with the Tim Hortons brand headquartered in Canada — and securities filings show 3G still holds a stake on the order of one-fifth. The double-double is run from Florida.

And the fine print keeps going. In the early 2000s Tim Hortons switched from scratch-baking in every store to what it called Always Fresh: par-baked goods, flash-frozen at a central bakery, finished in store ovens. Franchisees sued over the economics of it — and lost. An Ontario judge dismissed the case, writing that a franchisor must be permitted to introduce new products and new methods of production, and the dismissal held up on appeal. That's not a scandal; that's the court confirming the modern doughnut is a business decision.

But one thing was a formal finding. In June 2022, four privacy regulators — federal, Quebec, B.C. and Alberta — published a joint investigation into the Tim Hortons app. Their findings, in their words: the app would continually, as often as every few minutes, track a user's location, including in the background, including when the app was not open. It could infer where you lived, where you worked, and when you were travelling. The commissioners concluded the company did not collect that data for an appropriate purpose and did not obtain meaningful consent, contravening federal and provincial privacy law, and declared the matter well-founded. Tim Hortons agreed to delete the data and build a privacy program, and a year later the regulator confirmed it had kept those commitments. Separate class actions were settled — and a settlement is not an admission of wrongdoing — with compensation that became instantly famous: one hot beverage worth six dollars and nineteen cents, and one baked good worth two thirty-nine. Tracked every few minutes; compensated with a coffee and a donut.

One more thing while we're holding the cup. The odds printed on Roll Up the Rim — one in six this year — aren't generosity. Federal contest law requires fair disclosure of the number and approximate value of prizes and anything that materially affects your chances. The math quiz when you win? Canadian contest law again. Even the fun is regulated. And yes — double-double has been in the Canadian Oxford Dictionary since 2004. The editors sent researchers to eavesdrop in coffee shops to verify it. While we're settling legends: the missing apostrophe is real, and the reason is the law. The chain's signs originally read Tim Horton's, possessive — and the switch to the pluralized Tim Hortons was made to standardize the signage while satisfying Quebec's Bill 101 rules for commercial signs. The most Canadian brand name in existence lost its apostrophe to the Charter of the French Language. And if you ever wondered what it costs to be on the inside: franchise disclosure documents put the Tim Hortons franchise fee at fifty thousand dollars, with royalties of four and a half to six percent of gross sales plus four percent for advertising — and under Ontario's franchise law, the company must hand every prospective franchisee a document with all material facts fourteen days before they sign, or the franchisee can walk away for up to two years. The fine print protects both directions.

Number two: McDonald's.

Here's the fact that surprises everyone: the first McDonald's outside the United States opened in Richmond, British Columbia, in June 1967. Canada was McDonald's first foreign country. The Eastern rights went to a Chicago lawyer named George Cohon, who borrowed seventy thousand dollars, opened his first store in London, Ontario in 1968, became a Canadian citizen in 1977, and founded Ronald McDonald House Charities Canada in the 1980s. Cohon's obsession went global in the strangest way: he spent fourteen years pushing McDonald's into the Soviet Union, and when the Moscow location opened in January 1990, thirty thousand Muscovites lined up — an opening-day record for the company. A Canadian subsidiary pulled that off; his book is literally called To Russia with Fries. Today McDonald's Canada is a subsidiary of the Chicago parent, with more than fourteen hundred restaurants serving, by the company's count, two and a half million Canadians a day.

And it's the perfect place to read the menu board itself, because in Ontario, that board is a legal document. Since January 1, 2017, any chain with twenty or more Ontario locations must post calories for every standard food item, in type as prominent as the price, on penalty of fines that reach ten thousand dollars per day for a corporation. Even the sentence at the bottom of the board — the one about adults needing an average of two thousand calories a day — is not the restaurant being helpful. It's a word-for-word script written into the regulation. You've been reading government prose with your combo since 2017, and Ontario is the only province where we found a mandatory law like it.

Number three: Subway.

The quiet giant. Subway's first Canadian shop opened in St. John's, Newfoundland in 1986, and today it has more locations in this country than McDonald's — over three thousand, second only to Tim Hortons. It's not Canadian: since 2024 Subway has belonged to Roark Capital, an Atlanta private equity firm, in a deal reported at nine point six billion US dollars.

Now, carefully, the story you half-remember. In 2017, CBC's Marketplace aired a report about Subway's chicken based on lab DNA testing. Subway disputed the testing and sued the CBC for two hundred and ten million dollars. Here is the entire legal outcome, and it matters: an Ontario judge first dismissed the suit under anti-SLAPP law in 2019; the Court of Appeal overturned that in 2021 and let the case proceed; the Supreme Court declined to intervene. What no court has ever done — to this day, as far as the public record shows — is rule on whether the report was true or false. We're not going to do what the courts didn't. What we can tell you is deliciously Canadian: Toronto had its own sub chain, Mr. Sub, at 130 Yorkville Avenue in 1968 — eighteen years before Subway arrived. Remember Mr. Sub. It comes back in the food court.

Number four: A&W. The twist of the whole episode.

Frosted mugs. Car hops. The Burger Family — Papa, Mama, Teen and Baby, with the Grandpa Burger added in Canada. It all looks like pure Americana, and it started in Winnipeg: the first Canadian A&W drive-in opened on Portage Avenue in 1956, eleven years before Canada saw a McDonald's. Then, in 1972, the Canadian operation was sold to Unilever and permanently separated from the American chain. In 1995, its own managers bought it. In 2002 its trademarks went onto the Toronto Stock Exchange in a royalty fund, and in October 2024 the whole thing combined into one public company — A&W Food Services of Canada, ticker A-W, headquartered in North Vancouver. More than a thousand locations, the number two burger chain in the country, and every three percent royalty on every root beer flows through a Canadian company that has had nothing to do with the American A&W for over fifty years. The chain everyone assumes is American is one of the most Canadian companies in this entire video. And your grandchildren should know the soundtrack: the Great Root Bear and his tuba theme date from the 1970s Canadian operation, and the drive-in-era jingle — let's all go to A&W — was playing on Winnipeg radio before most of this list existed.

If you're keeping count of the map so far, drop the number 3 in the comments — for the three companies — and let's keep moving, because the connections are about to accelerate.

Number five: Starbucks. Short and telling: when Starbucks finally left the United States in 1987, the first store it opened in the world was at Vancouver's SeaBus terminal. Canada was the test market again. Nearly fifteen hundred stores today, all pouring for Seattle.

Number six: Dairy Queen. It reached Canada before almost anywhere: Melville, Saskatchewan, in the early 1950s, out of a local quick-freeze plant. A Saskatchewan franchisee named Jim Penny, who opened in Estevan in 1954, put it better than any analyst ever could: it put our children through university, and ultimately my grandchildren. And who owns Dairy Queen now? Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway — bought outright in 1998 for about 585 million US dollars. Told you he'd be back: the same Buffett money that owns your Blizzard helped finance the purchase of your double-double.

Number seven: KFC. The Colonel came north remarkably early — Canada was among the first places outside the US where Sanders franchised his chicken, back in the 1950s, and for a generation of Canadian families the Sunday bucket was an institution. Today KFC Canada answers to Yum Brands of Louisville, Kentucky, which also owns Pizza Hut and Taco Bell.

Number eight: Wendy's. The first international Wendy's opened in September 1975 in — of all places — Hamilton, Ontario. The same city that gave us Tim Hortons in 1964. And in 1995 the two Hamilton milestones actually merged, when Wendy's bought Tim's for a decade. Today Wendy's has nearly 450 Canadian locations, every one franchise-owned, reporting to Dublin, Ohio.

Number nine: Pizza Pizza. Nine-six-seven, eleven-eleven. If you just sang that, you grew up in Ontario. Michael Overs opened the first Pizza Pizza at Parliament and Wellesley in Toronto on New Year's Eve 1967, and built the jingle into the most profitable phone number in Canadian history. Pizza Pizza is still Canadian-owned, still Toronto-run, it owns Alberta's Pizza 73, and here's how the modern version works: a royalty company on the TSX collects six percent of every Pizza Pizza restaurant's system sales — nine percent at Pizza 73 — off the top, before profit. Eight hundred and fourteen restaurants are in that royalty pool. When you buy the slice, six cents of every dollar is spoken for by the stock market — and any Canadian can own a piece of it.

Number ten: Boston Pizza. Started by a Greek immigrant, Gus Agioritis, as Boston Pizza and Spaghetti House in Edmonton in 1964. One of his first franchisees was an RCMP officer named Jim Treliving — yes, the Dragon from Dragons' Den — who opened Penticton in 1968, met an accountant named George Melville, and in 1983 the two of them bought the whole 44-restaurant chain. Today the company calls itself Canada's number one casual dining brand, with more than 365 locations, run from Richmond, B.C., with its own TSX royalty fund collecting five and a half percent of franchise sales. Canadian-owned, coast to coast.

Number eleven: Swiss Chalet — and now the map really starts to draw itself.

The first Swiss Chalet opened on Bloor Street in Toronto in 1954, roasting chickens on spits over open flame, and the sauce became a national ritual. Harvey's followed in Richmond Hill in 1959 — the place where, in the company's own words, guests have topped their burgers just how they love them for sixty-five years, set to a jingle about making your hamburger a beautiful thing. Two separate beloved brands, right? Both were bought in 1977 by a company called Cara — a company that started in 1883 selling newspapers on railway platforms. And Cara kept eating. Montana's, Kelsey's and Milestones in 2002. East Side Mario's in 2013 — in a deal, note the name, done with Fairfax Financial. New York Fries — born at Scarborough Town Centre in 1984 — in 2015. Then in 2016, Quebec's crown jewel: St-Hubert, the rotisserie Hélène and René Léger opened on Saint-Hubert Street in Montreal in 1951, the chain whose fleet of yellow Beetles delivered Canada's first free home delivery in 1952 — sold to Cara for 537 million dollars. In 2018 came The Keg, for 200 million — and pay attention to who founded The Keg in 1971: George Tidball, the very same man who had brought McDonald's to Canada in 1967. Cara renamed itself Recipe Unlimited, and in October 2022, Fairfax Financial — the Toronto holding company built by Prem Watsa — took the whole thing private at a valuation of about 1.2 billion dollars. CBC called it Canada's oldest and largest full-service restaurant chain. So here's your map: Swiss Chalet, Harvey's, St-Hubert, The Keg, Montana's, Kelsey's, East Side Mario's, New York Fries — one owner. Canadian, yes. But when you pick between them for dinner, the same company wins every time — and you can no longer even buy a share of it.

Number twelve: the food court. In 1979, a Hong Kong-born immigrant named Stanley Ma, who had arrived in Canada at eighteen and worked his way up from a dishwashing job, opened a single restaurant in Montreal called Le Paradis du Pacifique. That restaurant grew into MTY Food Group: today, by its own count, more than 80 banners and 7,080 locations, ringing about 5.7 billion dollars a year in system sales. Thai Express? MTY. Jugo Juice? MTY. Mr. Sub — the Yorkville original from 1968? MTY, since 2011. And, most poignantly for this audience: Country Style, the Toronto doughnut chain founded in 1963 that once went toe to toe with Tim Hortons for Canada's coffee break — MTY bought it in 2009 for 16 and a half million dollars, about the price of two downtown houses. The coffee war ended with the winner owned from Miami and the runner-up owned from Montreal. When you shop around the food court to support different businesses, check the fine print: every till may ring for the same company.

Number thirteen: the fourth name, the one still growing. Foodtastic, of Montreal, founded in 2016, now claims over 1,200 restaurants and a billion dollars in sales across 27 brands. It bought Second Cup — the mall coffee icon founded in Toronto in 1975 — for just 14 million dollars in 2021. It bought Freshii for 74 million in 2023. It took Milestones off Recipe's hands. The chains of your memory didn't disappear; they got bought, mostly by three companies — and now a fourth is collecting what's left.

Number fourteen: Little Caesars. If this were an American video, this chain would be the star. In Canada it's the punchline to a different joke: a modest player with a few hundred stores, owned since 1959 by the Ilitch family of Detroit — who also own the Red Wings, which for a Leafs fan may be the most damning ownership fact in this entire episode. Its one genuinely Canadian distinction: when Little Caesars first left the United States in 1969, guess which country it picked. Canada. First. Again.

Which is the pattern hiding under this whole video: the first McDonald's outside the US, the first Starbucks, the first international Wendy's, the first international Domino's — Winnipeg, 1983 — and the first international Little Caesars were all Canadian. For sixty years, when American food empires wanted to test the world, they tested us.

Now, the money, briefly, because the numbers tell an honest story. Statistics Canada's restaurant price index is up about 44 percent since January 2017 — but before anyone blames the drive-thru, groceries are up about 43 over the same window. It's a dead heat; all food outran overall inflation by about twelve points. The real outlier is the bean itself: the coffee price index is up nearly 62 percent, and the average retail price of a 340-gram bag went from four fifty-nine in January 2017 to nine fifty-four this summer — more than doubled. Canadians spent 101 billion dollars at restaurants and bars in 2025, and 2024 was the first normal year in history when fast food out-sold sit-down dining in this country. Two more pieces of law for your wallet: since 2022, the advertised price must legally be a price you can actually pay — the Competition Tribunal hit Cineplex with a 38.9-million-dollar penalty over a mandatory booking fee, upheld on appeal, and the Bureau has now sued DoorDash alleging a decade of mandatory fees stacked at checkout; those are allegations DoorDash is contesting, and nothing there has been proven. And the counter itself is a tax comedy in three acts: one donut is taxed, six donuts to go are tax-free — that's written into the Excise Tax Act — and in Ontario, a coffee-and-snack order of four dollars or less gets the provincial part of the HST rebated at the till. Same counter, three different tax treatments, all perfectly legal, none of it explained to you until now.

So here's the final scoreboard. Foreign-owned: Tim Hortons — Canadian-born, Miami-run; McDonald's, Subway, Starbucks, Wendy's, KFC, Dairy Queen, Little Caesars — American; and the food's made here, but the decisions aren't. Canadian-owned, genuinely: A&W — the great impostor in reverse; Pizza Pizza; Boston Pizza; Mary Brown's, the Newfoundland chicken chain born in St. John's in 1969, still Canadian, now past 280 locations; Booster Juice, born in Sherwood Park, Alberta in 1999. And the great consolidators — Fairfax's Recipe stable, MTY's food court, Foodtastic's collection — Canadian owners too, just fewer of them than the logos suggest. Three companies, plus one rising, behind most of the signs on your strip.

The chain that carries the maple leaf answers to Miami. The chain with the American root beer answers to North Vancouver. In this aisle — and it turns out a drive-thru is just an aisle with a speaker — the flag on the sign tells you nothing. The filings tell you everything. So: did your go-to make the list? And tell us in the comments which one you miss the most — Country Style people, I know you're out there. We read every label. That's all we do. See you tomorrow.
