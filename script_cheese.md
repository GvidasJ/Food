TITLE: Canadians Must AVOID These 12 "Cheese" Brands (8 That Are ACTUALLY Real Cheese)

There is a single word printed on some of the most popular cheese in Canada, and once you see it, you cannot unsee it. The word is "product." Not cheese — cheese *product*. And it is not a style choice. It is a legal confession. Under Canada's federal food standards, cheese has a strict definition: milk, coagulated with the help of bacteria, into a curd — with real minimums for milk fat and real limits on moisture, and a rule most shoppers have never heard of: for cheddar, at least 83 percent of the protein must come from actual fluid milk, not from processed milk derivatives. Below real cheese, the law builds a ladder, and every rung down means less cheese and more water. "Processed cheddar cheese" — melted real cheese. One rung down, "processed cheese food" — legally only 51 percent cheese, with milk ingredients and water making up the rest. Another rung, "processed cheese spread" — same 51 percent floor, up to 60 percent moisture. And then, at the very bottom, there is "processed cheese product" — a name that appears nowhere in the cheese standards at all. It is the name manufacturers reach for when what is in the package cannot legally wear any of the names above it. The word "product" is the industry quietly telling you: this does not qualify. Canadians eat more cheese than ever — over fifteen kilograms per person a year, up two full kilograms in the past decade — and a 500-gram block that cost five dollars and eighty cents in 2017 now averages about seven dollars, with retail cheese spending in this country passing seven billion dollars a year. This is real money, spent mostly on autopilot, on packages designed to be grabbed without reading. Tonight we climbed through the cheese aisle the way we did the freezer aisle in our ice cream video: every claim from the companies' own Canadian product pages, the Canadian Food Inspection Agency's standards, Statistics Canada, and actual court records — because yes, this aisle has been to court, and even across the border in the trunk of a police officer's car. Twelve famous names that are not selling you what the front of the package implies, starting with the most famous of them all. Then eight that are gloriously, verifiably the real thing — most of them Canadian, several of them cheaper per kilogram than you'd guess. By the end, you will sort any cheese shelf in this country in five seconds flat.

Begin with the orange square that built a million grilled cheese sandwiches. Kraft Singles is the best-known "cheese" in North America — and in Canada, the retail listings call it "sliced process cheese," while Kraft's own Canadian website titles the Swiss variety, in Kraft's own words, "Processed Cheese Product Slices." There is the bottom rung of the ladder, on the maker's own page. Now read the ingredient list from Kraft Canada's site: modified milk ingredients first. Water second. Cheese — the actual food this product is named after — third, followed by vegetable oil, corn maltodextrin, sodium citrate, sodium phosphate, cellulose gum, carrageenan. Cheese is an ingredient in this cheese, and it comes behind water. "Modified milk ingredients," if you have always wondered, is the catch-all Canadian label term for altered milk components — casein, caseinates, whey proteins, ultrafiltered milk — the industrial building blocks left when milk has been taken apart. The CFIA is actually consulting on renaming the class "milk-derived ingredients," which tells you even the regulator thinks shoppers don't know what the words mean. Nothing on the Kraft label is hidden: it is all printed, in order, exactly as the law requires. And to be fair to the engineers, the recipe does what it is designed to do — that flawless, never-greasy melt is the emulsifying salts working precisely as intended; real cheddar can split and pool oil under the same heat. The trade was never a secret: you gave up cheese to gain melt. But the front of the pack sells cheese, and the back of the pack lists a construction project. And here is the number that should really change your buying: at Walmart, those slices work out to roughly fourteen dollars a kilogram — while a block of real Black Diamond old cheddar runs about nineteen. The gap between the imitation and the real thing is a few dollars per kilogram. You are not saving what you think you are saving.

Velveeta is the second entry, and it is the boldest label in the aisle. At Canadian retailers it is listed plainly as "Process Cheese Product Slices" and "Processed Cheese Loaf" — that bottom-rung word again, right in the product name. The listed ingredients follow the same script: modified milk ingredients, water, a cheese blend well down the list — and, according to the retailer's published listing, pork gelatin, holding the loaf's famous melt together. Check your own package on that one — but sit with the idea for a moment: gelatin, in cheese. Real cheese holds itself together with casein, the protein cheesemaking is built on. When a recipe needs gelatin to behave like cheese, the label has already told you which rung of the ladder you are standing on.

Cheez Whiz completes the Kraft Heinz trio, and it earns a strange little compliment on the way past. In Canada it is designated "processed cheese spread" — which, remember, legally requires at least 51 percent actual cheese. The Canadian jar's ingredients still open with modified milk ingredients, water, and vegetable oil before any cheese appears — but here is the twist: the American version of Cheez Whiz has been reported to contain essentially no cheese at all, while the Canadian version, held up by Canadian standards, must carry real cheese content. Our rules quietly make even the orange jar more honest than its American twin. That is the theme of tonight, by the way: Canada's cheese law is genuinely strict — which is exactly why the words on the front matter so much. The companies did not choose these awkward names. The law forced the names, and the marketing simply hopes you never read them.

Number four is where it gets personal, because this is the brand that practically invented Canadian cheddar as a brand: Black Diamond, founded in 1933, by its own account the first brand-name cheddar in this country, the black wax wheel your grandparents trusted by name. Walk the aisle and you will find Black Diamond slices sold as — read it slowly — "Cheddar-Style Processed Cheese Slices." Style. The brand that built its name on Canadian cheddar sells slices that legally cannot be called cheddar, only styled after it. The ingredients: modified milk ingredients, water, glucose — sugar, in cheese slices — then cheese, emulsifying salts, carrageenan, soy lecithin. And now the twist that makes this entry the most useful one tonight: the same Black Diamond, on the same shelf, also sells "Natural Cheese Slices" — real medium cheddar, sliced: milk, cream, salt, bacterial culture, enzyme. One logo, two completely different foods, separated by one word on the front and about a dollar. Nobody at Black Diamond will ever explain that difference in a commercial — the label already explains it to anyone who looks. Ownership note while we are here: Black Diamond, Balderson, Cracker Barrel, and Cheestrings all left Kraft in 2019, sold for 1.6 billion dollars to Lactalis — the French dairy giant that also owns Lactantia and Président. The most Canadian names in the cheese aisle answer to Paris now. Hold that thought for the real-cheese list, because it cuts both ways.

Number five is for anyone who thinks we are exaggerating about the ladder, because one company prints the entire ladder as a product catalogue. Saputo's Armstrong foodservice line — the slices that end up in restaurants and cafeterias — comes in three tiers, side by side, on Saputo's own website: "Processed Cheddar Cheese" slices, where cheese is the first ingredient. "Processed Cheese Food" slices — the 51 percent rung. And "Processed Cheese Product" slices — the rung below standards. Same brand, same format, three legally distinct foods, priced accordingly, and the only difference a customer ever sees is two or three words that almost nobody reads. When people ask why this channel is obsessed with label designations, this is the answer: the industry itself uses those words with lawyerly precision. They know exactly what each name means. The bet is that you don't.

The store brands run the same play, faster and cheaper, so let's take them as a group. Great Value at Walmart: "Cheddar-Style Process Cheese Product" — style and product in one name, a double confession; the label scan shows modified milk ingredients, water, cheese, glucose, carrageenan, soy lecithin, modified cellulose. No name cheese slices: modified milk ingredients first, cheese second — with the cheese itself partly built from modified milk ingredients — then water and glucose. Compliments at Sobeys: "Cheddar-Style" slices, same formula family. And the old-fashioned tub of MacLaren's Imperial — the sharp cold-pack spread on holiday cracker boards since your childhood — is listed as "cold-pack cheese food," the 51 percent rung, cut with modified milk ingredients and water. None of these are scandals; all of them are printed exactly as the law demands. The scandal, if there is one, is only this: every one of these packages sits in the dairy case, at eye level, dressed identically to real cheese, at prices that are no longer meaningfully cheaper than real cheese. Because now we reach the most Canadian invention in this entire video: pizza mozzarella. Not mozzarella — pizza mozzarella. In 2008, when Canada tightened its cheese standards, it also wrote a brand-new standardized cheese into the rules, designed openly for the pizza industry. Compare the numbers. Real mozzarella: at most 52 percent moisture, at least 20 percent milk fat. Pizza mozzarella: up to 58 percent moisture, as little as 15 percent fat — and the part-skim version goes to 61 percent moisture and 11 percent fat. And the fluid-milk rule that defines real cheese? Cheddar needs 83 percent of its protein from actual milk; most named cheeses need 95; pizza mozzarella needs just 63 percent — the lowest number in the entire cheese table. It is a real, legal, standardized cheese — engineered to be wetter, leaner, and cheaper, one written rule at a time. And it is everywhere: No Name sells "Pizza Mozzarella Cheese" blocks, and Saputo's Mozzarellissima — the red bag of shreds in half the fridges in Quebec — is pizza mozzarella, with cellulose to stop clumping and natamycin, an antifungal, on the surface, both permitted, both printed. Nothing illegal, nothing dangerous. Just a quiet fact: the "mozzarella" on much of this country's pizza is a different, looser cheese than the one the word made you picture — and until tonight, nobody told you it had its own name. If tonight is the first time you're hearing about pizza mozzarella, hit the like button, honestly — because the CFIA published these numbers seventeen years ago and we could not find a single mainstream Canadian explainer that has ever walked shoppers through them. This is exactly the fine print this channel exists to read. And keep the term in your pocket for the good-news list, because the most surprising redemption of the night is made of it.

Two quick label curiosities finish the avoid side. Kraft 100% Parmesan — the green-lidded shaker — is real cheese by designation, but the "100%" deserves its asterisk: the Canadian ingredient list includes cellulose powder, plant fibre added to keep the grains flowing, plus potassium sorbate to preserve it. One hundred percent Parmesan, plus things that are not Parmesan — the front and the back of the same canister quietly disagreeing. And the processed aisle's history file deserves its own entry, because the industry did not accept these strict Canadian standards gracefully: when the 83-percent fluid-milk rules arrived in 2008, Kraft, Saputo, and Parmalat went to Federal Court to strike them down — arguing, in effect, for the right to build cheese from more processed derivatives and less milk. They lost. The Federal Court of Appeal upheld the standards in 2011, agreeing that swapping milk for derivatives "did adversely affect product taste, texture and consistency," and the Supreme Court refused to hear it. The strict labels you are learning to read tonight exist because the government defended them against the exact companies whose products fill this list. And the border games never stopped, because when a food is this protected — cheese from abroad faces a tariff of 245.5 percent above Canada's small import quota — the gap between cheap foreign product and Canadian prices becomes a business opportunity for anyone with flexible ethics. Which brings us to the strangest true story in Canadian food: the Mozzarella Mafia. In 2012, investigators on both sides of the Niagara border unravelled a smuggling operation moving American cheese into Canada — over two hundred thousand dollars of it, resold to pizzerias at a profit of more than one hundred and sixty-five thousand, with drivers reportedly clearing a thousand or two per trip, because US cheese could be had for as little as a third of the Canadian price. The men charged included a serving Niagara Regional Police constable and a former officer. The constable was convicted on six charges in 2015, sentenced to four months, and resigned from the force. A police officer went to jail, in this country, over contraband mozzarella. That is how valuable real cheese is here — and it is also the dark-mirror version of a system that, on the bright side, keeps Canadian dairy farms alive. The subtler border game was "diafiltered milk": for years, ultra-concentrated American milk protein crossed into Canada tariff-free because the border agency classified it as an ingredient — while the food agency simultaneously counted it as milk in Canadian cheese vats. Two federal agencies, one substance, two opposite answers, and millions in losses that Quebec farmers answered by blockading a Montreal processing plant and rallying thousands on Parliament Hill, until a 2017 pricing fix shut the loophole — a fix Canada then traded away under the new NAFTA, because this aisle never gets to stop fighting. Even this year, the CFIA fined a Fortinos ten thousand dollars in a maple-washing crackdown that news coverage says included a French-made Président cheese spread displayed under a maple-leaf shelf tag. The lesson from every chapter is identical: in the cheese aisle, the flag, the logo, and the front of the pack are marketing. The designation is law.

So here is the five-second check before the good news. Under the brand name, find the designation — the small words that legally define the food. "Cheese" or a variety name like cheddar or mozzarella: real. "Processed cheese": melted real cheese. "Cheese food," "cheese spread": majority rule — 51 percent. "Cheese product": below every standard in the book. Then flip it and read the first ingredient. Real cheese starts with milk. The imitations start with modified milk ingredients and water. Five seconds, two looks, no exceptions in the entire aisle — and it works in every store in the country, on every brand, forever, because these aren't marketing terms that fashion can change. They are legal names, defended in court, and no company can print a better one than its recipe has earned.

Now the eight that are exactly what they claim, and this list is a road trip across the country. First — and this one will genuinely surprise parents — Cheestrings. The lunchbox snack this channel fully expected to put on the avoid list turns out to be real cheese: the label reads "Pizza Mozzarella Cheese Sticks" — honestly declared, blue cow logo, 100 percent Canadian milk, ingredients led by milk and cream with bacterial culture and enzyme. Yes, it is the looser pizza-mozzarella standard you just learned about — but it is real, curd-formed, honestly named cheese, and even the white "cheddar-flavoured" ones admit on the label that they are pizza mozzarella wearing a cheddar costume. The kids' snack passes the test that the grown-ups' sandwich slices fail. Nobody saw that coming, including us — and it is the perfect proof that this channel's method works both ways: we read the label expecting a villain and found real cheese, so real cheese is what we're reporting.

Second, the same-brand redemption: Black Diamond and Cracker Barrel blocks are real cheddar — milk and cream first, cultured, aged, legally cheddar — sitting one shelf from their own brand's "cheddar-style" slices. One honest nuance: some blocks list a share of modified milk ingredients too, which the standard permits as long as at least 83 percent of the protein still comes from real milk — the law holds the line at mostly-milk, always. And if you want cheddar with no modified milk ingredients at all, it exists at the same store: Balderson, aged in Winchester, Ontario — milk, cream, salt, culture, rennet, nothing else — the 280-gram block of old cheddar that makes the case for spending eleven dollars.

Third, OKA — the monk's cheese. Created in 1893 by Trappist Brother Alphonse Juin at the abbey in Oka, Quebec, from a recipe he carried from France, prize-winning within its first year, and still ripened in the same cellars today — now under Agropur, which is not a foreign conglomerate but a co-operative owned by more than two thousand Canadian dairy farm families, so the premium you pay flows back to the farms the milk came from. The label is five ingredients: milk, salt, culture, calcium chloride, enzyme. About eight dollars for a 190-gram wheel — genuinely expensive per kilogram, and worth saying so plainly — but it is the opposite end of the aisle from the slices: every dollar buys cheese, history, and nothing else. A hundred and thirty years, one recipe, zero asterisks.

Fourth, St-Albert — founded in January 1894 by ten Franco-Ontarian farmers and still a co-operative today, a hundred and thirty years on, drawing milk from farms within about fifty kilometres of the village and selling in more than two thousand stores across Ontario and Quebec. This is the cheese under half the honest poutine east of Ottawa, and it is responsible for the squeakiest cheese curds in Confederation. The squeak, by the way, is a freshness instrument: fresh curds squeak because the intact protein network rubs against your teeth, and the squeak dies within a couple of days as the structure softens. A cheese that audibly reports its own freshness — no label required.

Fifth, Bothwell Cheese of New Bothwell, Manitoba — founded by farmers in 1936, and since 2022 owned by Gay Lea, the Ontario dairy co-operative, meaning the prairie cheddar institution is farmer-owned again. Their stated standard: 100 percent fresh Canadian milk, no modified milk ingredients. That sentence on a website is nice; on an ingredient list, it's verifiable — and it verifies.

Sixth, COWS Creamery on Prince Edward Island, whose Avonlea Clothbound Cheddar — an Orkney-inspired recipe made with island milk since 2006, wrapped in cloth and aged the way cheddar was aged a century ago — has taken SuperGold at the World Cheese Awards and grand champion honours at SIAL. Think about that pairing for a second: earlier tonight, a slice that cannot legally say the word cheddar; here, a cheddar from the smallest province in the country beating the world. Both are in Canadian grocery stores. Both say cheddar somewhere on the pack. Only one of them means it, and by now you can tell them apart in the time it takes to pick one up.

Seventh, feta — with a reading lesson attached. Under the Canada–EU trade agreement, "feta" became a protected European name here: long-standing Canadian makers kept the word, newcomers must say "feta-style." Krinos cow and goat feta shows what the real thing reads like — milk, calcium chloride, enzyme, culture, sea salt, no modified milk ingredients — while some big-brand fetas lean on the modified stuff. Same white cube, different first ingredients. You know the drill by now.

And eighth, the honourable mentions that deserve their own videos: Sylvan Star's Grizzly Gouda from Alberta, a repeat national champion; Fromagerie Perron, making cheddar in Quebec since 1890; Fromagerie Boivin in the Saguenay, hand-bagging fresh curds since 1939; Skotidakis feta from a three-thousand-goat farm on the Ontario–Quebec line; and Costco's Kirkland cheddars, which carry the blue cow — the Dairy Farmers of Canada mark certifying 100 percent Canadian milk. The Canadian Cheese Awards, fittingly, will not even accept an entry containing modified milk ingredients. The judges use the same test we taught you tonight.

One last story, because this country's relationship with real cheese deserves it. In 1866, farmers in Ingersoll, Ontario pooled the milk of twenty-four hundred cows and pressed a single 7,300-pound wheel of cheddar — the Mammoth Cheese — and shipped it to fairs in America and England as a declaration that Canadian cheddar could stand against the world. By 1900, one Ontario county alone had ninety-eight cheese factories. That is the inheritance sitting on the real-cheese shelf. And here is the quiet good news hiding in the national statistics: processed cheese's share of Canadian retail is falling — Canadians are already, slowly, trading back up to the real thing. Tonight you just got the vocabulary to join them. And run the money math one last time, because it is the part nobody expects: the famous orange slices cost around fourteen dollars a kilogram. Real Black Diamond cheddar in a block: about nineteen. The gap between the bottom rung of the ladder and the genuine article — aged, cultured, legally cheddar — is roughly the price of a coffee per kilogram, and store-brand real blocks close the gap further still. The processed aisle's entire business model assumes you believe the real thing is a luxury. The shelf tag says otherwise, and now you know to check it. So: open your fridge. Find the slices.
