TITLE: We Tested Chicken From Loblaws, Walmart, Costco, Sobeys & 4 More Canadian Stores (These Are Worth Buying)

Chicken is the meat Canada runs on. We eat more of it than beef and pork — over thirty-five kilograms per person per year — and we are paying more for it than we ever have: Statistics Canada has boneless skinless breast averaging around fourteen and a half dollars a kilogram this year, chicken thighs jumped almost fourteen percent in a single year, and a club pack of boneless thighs at a Toronto Loblaws was recently ringing in at twenty-two dollars. Beef prices have chased families into this aisle, avian flu has squeezed supply, and the result is that the humble chicken breast has quietly become one of the most expensive everyday purchases in the store. For that kind of money, you deserve to know exactly what is in the package. And chicken should be the easiest label in the entire store, because real chicken is one ingredient. One word. Chicken. So tonight we did what we did with the frozen fries: we pulled the actual labels, listings, and nutrition panels for the chicken sold at Loblaws, Real Canadian Superstore, No Frills, Walmart, Costco, Sobeys, FreshCo, and Metro, and we held every one of them against Canada's own federal rules. Because here is what almost nobody knows: Canadian law already draws a bright line through the chicken case. The moment a processor injects a bird with water, salt, and phosphates, the product legally cannot be called just "chicken breast" anymore. The name on the label has to change — to "seasoned" — and it has to print a number beside it: the minimum percentage of actual meat protein you are getting. The law literally makes diluted chicken confess, in writing, on the front. And there is a second confession most shoppers have never read: birds cooled in communal cold-water baths absorb some of that bath, and under the federal water-retention program a chicken can carry several percent of absorbed chill water — up to eight percent on smaller birds — counted right into the weight you pay for. One word and one number, hiding in plain sight, separate the chicken that is all chicken from the chicken that is partly tap water at fourteen dollars a kilo. Tonight, store by store, starting with the biggest grocer in the country. And stay to the end, because this video has real good news in it: some of the cleanest, most honest chicken in the entire case is also close to the cheapest — once you know which word to look for.

Start at Loblaws, Superstore, and No Frills, because more Canadians buy their chicken from Loblaw banners than anywhere else. The centre of the chicken case is the club pack — the big tray of boneless skinless breasts that feeds the week. At Loblaws we found it listed around twenty-one dollars a kilogram, cheaper at Superstore and Wholesale Club. Now read the name on that kind of value pack carefully, because the no name club pack turns up in listings under its full legal name: seasoned boneless chicken breasts. There is the word. Seasoned. Nutrition databases put that product at roughly 250 milligrams of sodium per 100 grams. Hold that against a plain, untouched chicken breast — Exceldor's family pack, whose entire ingredient list is the single word "chicken," runs about 44 milligrams per 100 grams. Five, six times the sodium, before you have salted anything, because the bird arrived pre-loaded with salt water it soaked up at the plant. Nothing illegal — the law made them print the word. But the price per kilogram on the shelf tag does not distinguish chicken from brine. Loblaw has also had a rougher problem with the number on the scale itself. Through 2023, Loblaw sold underweighted meat across roughly eighty stores — packages weighed with the tray included — and publicly apologized, saying, in the company's own words, "We apologize for these errors." Then CBC went back with scales: its investigation reported underweighted chicken at a Toronto Loblaws, and underweighted chicken, pork, and ground beef at a Calgary No Frills — about five dollars of overcharge on one hundred and seven dollars of meat — with follow-up reporting in 2025 and again this year finding the problem resurfacing. And in March of this year, the CFIA fined Fortinos in Etobicoke and a Real Canadian Superstore ten thousand dollars each over products falsely presented as Canadian. None of that is a reason to boycott anybody — but it is a reason to weigh the tray against the label price and to read the name on the pack. And to be fair to Loblaw, hold on for the good-news half, because two of the best buys in this entire video are sitting in the same case, wearing a PC logo.

Walmart is next, because Great Value chicken is the budget anchor of the whole country. Walmart Canada's own listings include products with names like "Great Value Uncooked Seasoned Boneless Skinless Chicken Breasts" — the word is right in the product title — and the frozen Great Value breast shows up in nutrition databases around 280 milligrams of sodium per 100 grams. Same story as the club pack at Loblaw: that is not a chicken-quality number, that is a brine number. Walmart also had one of the stores CBC's meat-weighing investigation flagged — a Richmond, B.C. location where the tested basket came up about seven percent short. And Walmart's rotisserie chicken, the Your Fresh Market barbecue bird at around nine dollars for 800 grams, lists a solution of water, sea salt, chicken broth, and carrageenan — a seaweed-derived gelling agent whose job is holding injected water inside cooked meat. Nothing on that label is hidden — but notice that the cooked counter bird and the raw "seasoned" tray are running the same play with different names. Remember that injection, because at the next store it becomes the centre of an actual lawsuit. Here is the fair summary on Walmart: it is genuinely the cheapest way to buy chicken in most of Canada, and for a family stretching a budget that matters. Just know the two numbers that are doing the work: the price per kilogram on the front, and the sodium per 100 grams on the back. When the second number is high on a raw, plain-looking breast, part of the first number is water.

Costco. The eight-dollar rotisserie chicken is probably the single most beloved grocery item in this country — the bird that ends a thousand busy Tuesdays, the one Costco famously sells at a loss to get you in the door. In Canada it runs about eight to nine dollars for a bird around a kilogram and a bit. Now, the label. It is not called rotisserie chicken. It is called seasoned rotisserie chicken — there is that word again, doing exactly the legal work it is supposed to do — and the Canadian bird's panel shows about 350 milligrams of sodium per 100 grams, with phosphate compounds in the fine print. The bird is injected with a solution before it ever meets the rotisserie: that is why the meat is that juicy, every single time, in every single warehouse. In January of this year, that injection became a court case: a proposed class action filed in British Columbia alleges Costco advertised the bird as having no preservatives while it contained sodium phosphate. Those are allegations — nothing has been proven in court, and food scientists at McGill's Office for Science and Society have publicly argued the suit is overblown. We are not telling you the bird is dangerous; it is not, and at eighteen grams of protein per hundred, it remains honest value. We are telling you what "seasoned" means, because Costco itself tells you when it wants to: flip over Costco's own frozen Kirkland seasoned chicken breasts and the ingredient list is a masterpiece of plain speech — "chicken breast, water, kosher salt." That is the entire recipe of the modern chicken case, printed in six words by the one retailer confident enough to say it. One more piece of rotisserie math while we are standing at this counter, because CBC's Cost of Living once asked the obvious question — how can a cooked chicken cost less than a raw one? — and the answer explains the whole counter: rotisserie birds are smaller than the raw whole birds in the case, they are injected with weight-holding solution, and they are priced as traffic bait, the item the store happily loses margin on because nobody drives to Costco for one chicken and leaves with one chicken. The bird is the advertisement. You are allowed to eat the advertisement — just compare it per cooked gram against the raw whole bird two aisles over before you assume it is the bargain, because later in this video that comparison produces the single best number of the night. If this label-by-label breakdown is the kind of thing that saves your grocery money, take one second and hit like — and subscribe to Canadian Counter, because we do this to one Canadian aisle every single week, no scare stories, just the fine print.

Sobeys is the most instructive store in tonight's video, because both kinds of chicken sit in the same case wearing the same brand. On one side: Compliments Naturally Simple boneless skinless breasts and Compliments air-chilled breasts — genuinely premium specs, and we will explain air-chilled in a moment, because it is the single most meaningful upgrade word in the aisle. On the other side: the Compliments frozen seasoned club pack, four kilograms of boneless breasts whose nutrition panel shows 480 milligrams of sodium per 125-gram serving. Do the math and that is roughly 380 milligrams per 100 grams — which means the raw "seasoned" breast in Sobeys' freezer carries more sodium per hundred grams than Costco's fully cooked, famously salty rotisserie chicken. A raw ingredient, saltier than a finished dish. Same brand, same store, opposite philosophies, separated by one word in the product name and a number on the back. That is the entire thesis of tonight in a single freezer door. CBC's meat-weighing investigation touched this banner too — a Toronto FreshCo came up about seven percent light on the tested basket — and Sobeys' Compliments chicken burgers went through a recall this year over undeclared egg, the kind of allergen slip the recall system exists to catch. But credit where it is due: Sobeys keeps air-chilled, raised-without-antibiotics breasts as a regular shelf item rather than a specialty order, and if you shop there, that specific tray is the one this video exists to point you toward.

Metro rounds out the majors, and it is quietly running a different strategy: instead of competing on the cheapest club pack, its chicken case leans premium. The anchor is Prime raised-without-antibiotics chicken from Sofina — the same company behind Lilydale and Zabiha Halal — alongside an organic program built with Ferme des Voltigeurs in Drummondville, a Quebec family operation, and a halal selection that big-city Metro locations treat as core stock rather than an afterthought. The trade-off is price: Metro's "Mammoth" rotisserie bird runs about fourteen dollars, the most expensive of the birds we compared, and its everyday trays rarely undercut the discounters. There is nothing wrong with that model — it is the honest version of premium, actual production differences at a visible markup — but it makes Metro the store where the decoder section coming up matters most, because when every tray in the case wears an upgrade word, you need to know which words are physical and which are decorative. The pattern across every banner, discount or premium, is identical: every store sells both chickens. The honest one and the brined one, side by side, priced per kilogram as if they were the same food.

And a word on the national brands sitting in every one of those cases, because the tray-pack names have their own hierarchy, and it is really a price ladder with three rungs. The bottom rung, seventeen to twenty-one dollars a kilogram, is the store-brand club pack — and you now know to read its name for the word "seasoned." The middle rung, twenty-three to twenty-seven dollars, is the raised-without-antibiotics tier: Maple Leaf Prime, which states its birds "must never have received any amount of antibiotics, ever," raised on Canadian farms in Alberta and Ontario on a vegetable grain diet; Prime at Metro; PC Free From at Loblaw banners. The top rung, twenty-nine to thirty-two dollars, is certified organic — PC Organics, Yorkshire Valley Farms — where the premium buys outdoor-access standards and organic feed on top of everything below. Each rung is a real, audited claim; the ladder itself is honest. What is not honest is paying middle-rung or top-rung prices for bottom-rung chicken wearing decorative words — and one of Yorkshire Valley's own frozen lines shows how easy that is to miss: it is certified organic and labelled "seasoned," organic chicken with added brine, both things true at once, at thirty dollars a kilogram. The rungs only mean something if you check which one you are actually standing on.

Now the decoder for the premium words, because this is where Canadians quietly overpay. Start with the oldest trick on the shelf: "raised without added hormones." Hormones in Canadian chicken have been illegal since March 4, 1963. Every chicken in this country — the cheapest no name bird, the priciest organic bird — is hormone-free, by law, and has been since before most of us were born. The CFIA's own guidance says a bare "hormone free" claim is not acceptable, and that the honest phrasing must admit the truth — and here is the delightful part: one brand actually does it. Zabiha Halal prints, verbatim, "Raised without the use of added hormones or steroids. Like all chicken in Canada." That last sentence is the most honest sentence in the entire meat department, and exactly one company was willing to print it. Next: "grain-fed." Under CFIA guidance a grain-fed claim needs an eighty-five percent grain diet — which describes essentially every commercially raised chicken in Canada. It is table stakes wearing a medal. Next: "raised without antibiotics." This one is real — it means no antibiotics ever, from hatch to harvest, and it carries a real premium: PC Free From breasts run six to eight dollars a kilogram over the club pack. Worth knowing before you pay it: since May 2014 the Canadian industry has banned preventive use of the antibiotic classes most critical to human medicine, and extended that ban in 2018 — a change that came after a 2011 CBC Marketplace test of a hundred supermarket chickens found two-thirds carrying bacteria resistant to at least one antibiotic. The system got embarrassed, and the system actually moved. Ordinary Canadian chicken today already excludes the preventive antibiotic uses that mattered most. And the little blue "Raised by a Canadian Farmer" logo? It certifies the bird is Canadian, raised under the mandatory national food-safety and animal-care programs that cover all two thousand eight hundred or so chicken farms in the country. Mandatory is the key word — every Canadian chicken qualifies. It is a maple leaf, not a merit badge. The one upgrade word that reliably means something physical is air-chilled: instead of tumbling through a communal cold-water bath — the process that lets a smaller bird legally carry up to eight percent absorbed water into its sale weight — air-chilled birds are cooled individually with cold air, a claim a plant has to document to the CFIA before it can print it. No bath, no absorbed bath water, better skin, better sear. That word is the real one. And when a water-chilled bird does cross the declaration threshold, the label has to say so in one of three exact phrasings the CFIA prescribes — "up to X% water retained," "less than X% water retained," or "up to X% retained water added due to processing." Most shoppers have read that line a hundred times without registering that it is a legally mandated confession that part of the weight on the scale is the chill bath. Here is a kitchen test that proves it, free, tonight: lay a breast in a dry pan on medium heat. An air-chilled or genuinely plain breast sweats lightly and then browns. A bath bird steams aggressively and leaks a puddle within the first minute — that puddle is the water you bought at fourteen dollars a kilogram, leaving. One pan, sixty seconds, and the label's fine print becomes something you can see.

Two last stories from outside the store, because they frame everything on the shelf. First: Canada's chicken market is protected by a tariff wall — imports beyond a set quota face duties in the range of two hundred and thirty-eight to two hundred and forty-nine percent depending on the cut. That wall is why our chicken costs more than American chicken, and it is also why somebody found a hole in it: "spent fowl" — old egg-laying hens — enters Canada tariff-free, and the CFIA says broiler chicken has been deliberately misclassified as spent fowl at the border, with over three hundred and sixty-one million dollars in duties and penalties assessed since 2010, while trade reporting suggests only a small fraction was ever collected. Meaning some of the mystery chicken in this country's food supply wasn't Canadian, wasn't taxed, and wasn't what its paperwork claimed. It got bad enough that the industry funded a university lab to develop a DNA test just to tell a meat chicken from a retired laying hen, because the paperwork could no longer be trusted on its own. You will never see spent fowl labelled at the fresh meat counter — it flows into processing, food service, and products where the species line on the label just says chicken — which is exactly why the blue Raised by a Canadian Farmer logo, ordinary as it is, earns its place on this list after all: it is the one mark that means the bird actually came through the Canadian system, quota, audits and all. Second: labels themselves have a police record here. In 2018, Ontario's Cericola Farms — a supplier to major retailers — pleaded guilty and paid a four hundred thousand dollar fine after some 286,000 kilograms of poultry was sold, in the CFIA's words, in a manner likely to create an erroneous impression of its character — reported by the Globe and Mail as conventional chicken passed off as organic, caught by an employee whistleblower. So when we tell you to trust the ingredient line and the sodium number over the front-of-pack poetry, this is why: the number is audited math; the poetry is aspiration. And one genuine good-news story to balance it: after years of salmonella outbreaks tied to frozen raw breaded chicken — nuggets and strips that looked cooked but weren't — Ottawa forced processors in 2019 to control salmonella before sale, and contamination in those products collapsed from twenty-eight percent to under three. The system can fix things when the label finally has to tell the truth. Researchers estimate that one rule change cut all salmonella illness in Canada by roughly a quarter — one of the quietest public-health wins this country has had, achieved not by banning a product but by finally making the bag's contents match what the picture on the bag implied. It's the closest thing this aisle has to a happy ending, and nobody put out a press release you'd ever see.

So here is what is actually worth buying, and the list is friendlier than tonight probably felt. First choice: any bird or breast whose ingredient list is one word. Exceldor — air-chilled, grain-fed, ingredient list "chicken," 44 milligrams of sodium. Lilydale — Canadian farms, air-chilled, single ingredient. Olymel's air-chilled whole birds. Zabiha Halal — air-chilled, single ingredient, and the only label in Canada honest enough to footnote its own hormone claim. These are not boutique products; they are in the regular case at regular stores. Second choice, and the quiet superstar of this entire video: the whole bird. President's Choice sells an air-chilled whole chicken at about eleven dollars a kilogram — five dollars a pound. Sit with that number, because it is the best one tonight: nearly half the per-kilo price of the club-pack breasts, for a bird that got the premium chilling method the twenty-seven-dollar trays brag about, with zero injected anything. A two-kilogram bird costs about the same as the Costco rotisserie plus a coffee, weighs almost twice as much, and you control every grain of salt that touches it. One Sunday roast gives you dinner, a week of sandwiches, and a carcass worth of real stock that makes boxed broth taste like an apology. Our grandparents bought chicken exactly one way, and it turns out the label-reading, the sodium math, and the lawsuit-avoidance all point straight back to it. Third: if you love the rotisserie ritual, look at the label war we found — Loblaws' PC barbecue bird lists chicken, salt, spices, and vinegar-family acids, no phosphates, about 170 milligrams of sodium per 100 grams, roughly half of Costco's bird — thirteen dollars at Loblaws and reportedly ten at No Frills. You pay more per gram than Costco; you get a shorter ingredient list for it. That is a real choice, honestly priced both ways. And fourth: pay the raised-without-antibiotics or organic premium if that value matters to you — it is a real claim, audited, and the one premium on this shelf that buys an actual difference in how the bird was raised rather than a difference in adjectives. So here is the whole night as a fifteen-second protocol at the meat case. One: read the product name — if the word "seasoned" appears, the bird was injected, and the law put that word there to warn you. Two: read the ingredient line — real chicken is one word long. Three: flip to the panel — raw chicken over about 100 milligrams of sodium per 100 grams is carrying brine, whatever the front says. Four: look for "air-chilled" if you want the bird that never took the bath, and check the price per kilogram, not the price per tray. That is it. Four looks, fifteen seconds, and no word on the front of the package can fool you again. And notice what the protocol never asks you to do: it never asks you to trust a brand, boycott a store, or spend a dollar more than you were already spending. Every store in tonight's video sells chicken that passes all four checks, usually within arm's reach of the chicken that fails them. The system is not hiding the truth from you — Canadian law makes the truth legally printable, in the product name, the ingredient line, and the water-retention statement. The industry is simply betting, package after package, year after year, that you will read the front and never turn it over.
