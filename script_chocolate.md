# SCRIPT — Chocolate

TITLE: Canadians Must AVOID These 8 "Chocolate" Bars (Only 3 Are ACTUALLY Real Chocolate)

ALTERNATES:
- Canadians Must AVOID These 8 "Chocolatey" Bars (The Word on the Wrapper Is a Legal Confession)
- Your Favourite Canadian Chocolate Bar Isn't Legally Chocolate — We Read Every Label

---

Canada has one of the strictest chocolate laws on Earth. Most Canadians have no idea it exists — and almost nobody knows what it quietly did to the candy aisle you grew up with. Here's the law, in one sentence: in this country, anything sold as "chocolate" must be made with cocoa butter — the fat from the cocoa bean — and nothing else. Zero vegetable fat. Not five per cent, like Europe allows. Zero. Swap in palm oil, and by federal rule your product "cannot use the common name chocolate." It has to wear a different word — and the food inspection agency has even blessed the official replacement: "chocolatey." That one letter change, from chocolate to chocolatey, is a legal confession printed right on the wrapper — and once you know to look for it, the aisle splits into two teams. Today I read the label of every famous bar on a Canadian shelf, and I have to warn you: the results are going to hurt. The bar with "the secret." Canada's own coffee-flavoured national treasure. The bar you could only get here after the Americans lost theirs. One by one, the most Canadian chocolate bars in history turn out to be on the wrong team — while some bars you'd never suspect pass with honours, including the one everybody makes fun of. And it matters more this year than ever, because cocoa prices went from twenty-five hundred US dollars a tonne to nearly thirteen thousand at the peak of the great cocoa crisis — your bars have been shrinking on camera, CBC has the receipts — and the cheaper a "chocolate" recipe gets, the more that one little word starts appearing. Eight beloved "chocolate" bars that aren't quite what you remember — starting with the most iconic wrapper in the country — then the three real ones actually worth your money, ending with a hundred-and-fifty-year-old family company in New Brunswick that never once needed the word "chocolatey." Let's get into it.

Number one — Caramilk. The gold wrapper. "What's the secret in Caramilk?" — one of the most famous advertising questions in Canadian history. The bar has been made at the same downtown Toronto factory since 1968 — the plant on Gladstone Avenue that William Neilson started building in 1906, which today turns out roughly half a billion bars a year: every Cadbury product sold in this country comes from that one building. So let's answer a different question: what's the coating on a Caramilk? Here is the ingredient list from Mondelez Canada's own product database, in order: "sugars, milk ingredients, modified palm oil, unsweetened chocolate, palm oil, modified vegetable oil, cocoa butter…" Read that order again. Two palm oils and a modified vegetable oil come before the cocoa butter, which sits seventh on its own label. And how does Mondelez's website describe the bar? Quote: "creamy Cadbury chocolatey goodness." Chocolatey. Their word, not mine. Now, the fairness clause, because this channel doesn't do hit jobs: nothing here is hidden or illegal — the ingredient list is printed for anyone who flips the bar, the caramel secret is intact, and Caramilk still comes off the same line on Gladstone Avenue in Toronto it always has. But the thing wrapped around that caramel is not the thing your memory says it is — and when you're in the store, turn the bar over and read the printed name on the back panel yourself, on camera, because that's where the law makes the confession official. And if that one stung, take a breath. The next bar is the most Canadian chocolate bar ever made — and it's not chocolate either.

Number two — Coffee Crisp. How do you like your coffee? Canadians have been answering "crisp" since 1939. This bar exists nowhere else on the planet — every single one, about a hundred and fifty-three million bars a year, comes from one factory on Sterling Road in Toronto that runs day and night. It is arguably the most Canadian food product in existence. Now here is how Nestlé's own press release — a release celebrating Coffee Crisp for Canada Day, no less — describes it: "crispy wafers and coffee crème covered with a thin chocolatey coating." There's the word again, from the company itself. Flip the bar: sugar first, then wheat flour, modified milk ingredients, palm oil, modified palm and vegetable oils — and cocoa butter sitting sixth, behind two rounds of palm. The wafer bar Canada loves is Canadian-made, Canadian-only, and by its own maker's description, not coated in chocolate. Let that sink in: our national bar can't wear the national word. The history makes it sweeter and sadder at once: Coffee Crisp descends from a 1930s wafer called Biscrisp, evolved through the old Neilson and Rowntree operations into the bar Canada adopted in 1939 — and it's essentially unavailable in the United States except as an import, which is why homesick Canadians have been stuffing suitcases with it for generations. It is a genuine national treasure. It's just a national treasure whose coating, under the very Canadian law that makes our chocolate special, files under a different word. To be fair — Coffee Crisp has tasted like Coffee Crisp your whole life, and nobody's alleging it changed yesterday; the point is what the label has been quietly telling you all along. And before you ask "well, is anything at that factory real?" — yes. The same Sterling Road plant pours genuine milk chocolate — cocoa butter, standard met — onto Kit Kat, into Aero, and inside every Canadian Smartie. Same building, both teams. The difference isn't the factory. It's the recipe — and the price of cocoa butter. Which brings us to the bar Canada kept when America lost it.

Number three — Oh Henry! Here's a piece of trivia that sounds like a win for Canada: the Oh Henry! bar died in the United States. Nestlé's American version was discontinued in 2019, just shy of its hundredth birthday — but ours survived, because in Canada, Oh Henry! belongs to Hershey and always ran on a separate recipe. The bar lives on only here. Now flip our sole-survivor: "sugars — sugar, corn syrup, high fructose corn syrup, sweetened condensed skim milk, dextrose, high maltose corn syrup — peanuts, modified palm oil and modified vegetable oil, modified palm kernel oil, unsweetened chocolate, modified milk ingredients…" Keep reading as long as you like: cocoa butter never appears. Not demoted — absent. Six different sugars, three vegetable-oil entries, zero cocoa butter. The Americans lost Oh Henry!; we kept the version that isn't chocolate. And while we're in Hershey's corner of the aisle, two more notes for the record. Skor fans: the full-size bar involves real milk chocolate — but Skor Minis, the little bag by the checkout, lists no milk chocolate at all; palm oils again. Same brand, different format, different team — the format changes the recipe. And the bigger Hershey story your region already knows: the Smiths Falls, Ontario plant — the Chocolate Shoppe generations toured as kids — was closed in 2008, roughly five hundred jobs, production largely to Mexico. The empty factory became Canopy Growth's cannabis headquarters during the legal-weed boom, and in 2023, in the strangest full-circle in Canadian food, Canopy sold the building back to Hershey for fifty-three million dollars. Hershey says production will return; the town is still waiting for details. Chocolate outlived the weed boom — it just hasn't moved back in yet. And Smiths Falls wasn't alone in that 2007 wave: the same restructuring closed Moirs in Dartmouth, Nova Scotia that December — a candy lineage stretching back to 1815, nearly six hundred workers, production to Mexico. Pot of Gold, the box Moirs introduced in 1928, survives as a Hershey brand; the Maritime factory that invented it does not. When your parents say the chocolate aisle used to be more Canadian, they're not romanticizing. They're remembering. If this video is rearranging your candy memories, a quick like genuinely helps more Canadians find out what that little word means. Because next comes an entire family of childhood bars — from the same company — that never contained cocoa butter at all.

Number four — Mr. Big, Wunderbar, and Crispy Crunch. The Cadbury trio of every Canadian childhood — the biggest bar in the vending machine, the peanut-butter caramel one, the orange one you fought your siblings for. All three come from that same Toronto factory. And all three ingredient lists share one feature: cocoa butter is not on them. Anywhere. Mr. Big: sugars, modified palm oil, modified milk ingredients, rice, modified vegetable oil — plus hydrogenated palm oil further down. Wunderbar: sugars, peanuts, modified palm oil, hydrogenated palm kernel oil. Crispy Crunch: sugars with molasses, peanuts, modified palm oil. There's cocoa and unsweetened chocolate in the mix for flavour — but the coating fat, the thing that makes chocolate chocolate under Canadian law, is palm, full stop. Don't take my word for it: Amazon Canada's own listing sells Crispy Crunch under the title — verbatim — "Cadbury Crispy Crunch Chocolatey Candy Bars." The retailer typed the confession into the product name. Now for the twist this entry deserves, the single funniest fact I found in this entire investigation: Big Turk — the pink Turkish-delight bar, the most mocked chocolate bar in Canada, the one nobody admits to buying — is coated in real chocolate. Cocoa butter, no palm oil, coloured with black carrot concentrate. The bar Canada laughs at passes the law that Canada's favourites fail. Somewhere, a Big Turk enjoyer has earned the right to feel smug forever. And the next entry needs two wrappers and a plane ticket, because the same bar is chocolate in one country and not in ours.

Number five — Crunchie. The golden honeycomb bar. If you or your parents ever brought a Crunchie back from Britain, hold onto that memory, because it's legally a different product. The British Crunchie is dipped in Cadbury milk chocolate — cocoa mass, cocoa butter, the real thing. The Canadian Crunchie, made here, lists: sugars, modified palm oil, modified milk ingredients, modified vegetable oil, cocoa, unsweetened chocolate — no cocoa butter. Same name, same gold wrapper, same honeycomb middle — different coating on each side of the Atlantic, and only one of them would be allowed to call that coating chocolate under our rules. That's the cleanest possible demonstration that "chocolatey" isn't about taste or nostalgia — it's a recipe decision, made bar by bar, market by market. And here's a bonus rule from the same CFIA guidance that will change how you hear an everyday phrase: legally, only a product that is completely chocolate can even be called a "chocolate bar." A bar coated in chocolate is supposed to be a "chocolate coated bar," and a bar coated in palm-oil "chocolatey" is neither. Which means the sentence "Canada's favourite chocolate bars" — the one I've been saying this whole video — is, for half the bars we grew up with, legally wrong twice. And it's a decision under more pressure than ever: with cocoa at two-and-a-half times its old price even after the crisis peak, every manufacturer on Earth is staring at the cocoa butter line in its recipes. The law can't stop a recipe from changing. It can only force the wrapper to admit it. Which is exactly why the last stop on the avoid list is the baking aisle — where the law's two magic words sit side by side on the same shelf.

Number six — the chocolate chip decode. CFIA's rule, verbatim: anything called "chocolate chips" must be real chocolate. So what do you do if you want to sell chips made with palm kernel oil instead of cocoa butter? You invent a new name — and there it is, on Loblaw's own label: no name "chocolatey chips" — in the bracket: "sugar, palm kernel oil, cocoa, dextrose, modified milk ingredients, soy lecithin." The entire law, demonstrated in one bracket: swap the fat, lose the name. One shelf over, Hershey's Chipits say "PURE semi-sweet chocolate chips" — sugar, unsweetened chocolate, cocoa butter — and that word "pure" is doing legal work, not poetry. And the best-ordered ingredient list in the entire mainstream aisle belongs, of all places, to President's Choice: The Decadent chocolate chips read "unsweetened chocolate, sugar, cocoa butter, soy lecithin, vanilla extract" — the chocolate comes before the sugar. Sobeys' Compliments chips are real chocolate too. So in the baking aisle, the store brands beat half the famous candy bars — your grandmother's cookie recipe deserves the two-second bracket check more than anything else in your cart. Three more label rules from the same CFIA guidance while we're here, because they're all little confessions once you know them. "Chocolate shavings" on a cake must be real chocolate — same rule as chips. "No sugar added chocolate" legally isn't chocolate at all — sweeteners like maltitol are excluded from the standard, so it has to be renamed something like "milk chocolate sweetened with maltitol." And my favourite absurd detail in the entire regulation: the definition of a permitted sweetening ingredient specifically excludes icing sugar — meaning icing sugar is the one sugar in Canada you legally cannot make chocolate with. Also, that trendy word "cacao"? Legal on the front of a dark bar as "70% cacao" — but in the ingredient list, the law insists on plain old "cocoa." The wrapper speaks two languages; the bracket always tells the truth. Before the winners, three quick beats for the record. One: the money. For most of a decade cocoa cost about twenty-five hundred US dollars a tonne. Then came the worst supply shortfall in over sixty years — failed harvests, disease and brutal weather across Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana, which together grow about sixty per cent of the world's cocoa — and in mid-December 2024 the price hit its all-time record of just under thirteen thousand dollars a tonne. Today it's back to about sixty-two hundred: half the peak, still two and a half times the old normal. Statistics Canada's own annual review says Canadians paid 7.1 per cent more for confectionery in 2025 — and CBC documented the quieter move: the same $8.99 box of 65 Mars fun-size bars weighed 782 grams in 2024 and 672 grams in 2025 — fourteen per cent less, same price, same bar count — while Hershey's 50-bar pack slimmed 6.2 per cent and the Cadbury Creme Egg has shrunk from 39 grams to 34. The companies say they're delivering value and adjusting assortments; the scale is the only witness. Two: the graveyard. This past year Canada lost two icons — Jersey Milk, discontinued after 101 years, and Cherry Blossom, the Montreal-born cherry cup, both gone as the economics tightened. Three: history rhymes. Long-time viewers will remember the bread price-fixing story — chocolate had the prequel: Hershey Canada pleaded guilty to price-fixing in 2013 and paid four million dollars, charges against Nestlé Canada, Mars Canada, a distributor and three executives were all stayed by 2015 — stayed charges are unproven allegations, and no one but Hershey was convicted — and the class actions settled for twenty-three point two million. Chocolate has always been a business. Here's who's still doing the business right.

Best option number three — Cadbury Dairy Milk. After everything I just said about the Cadbury shelf, fairness demands this entry. Flip a Canadian Dairy Milk: "milk, sugar, cocoa butter, skim milk, whole milk powder, unsweetened chocolate, butter oil…" Milk is the first ingredient. Not sugar — milk. Real cocoa butter, real standard, real milk chocolate under the strictest definition in the world, still made in downtown Toronto at the factory William Neilson started building in 1906 — the plant that turns out half a billion bars a year and that Torontonians rallied around during the trade war. The same company that writes "chocolatey" on the trio keeps Dairy Milk on the right side of the law — proof the two-team split isn't about corporations being evil; it's about which recipe each bar got. Honourable mentions on the real-chocolate team while we're here: Aero, Canadian Smarties, the Kit Kat coating — and yes, Big Turk. The law doesn't care about your nostalgia rankings. It reads brackets. And one more made-in-Ontario surprise most Canadians don't know: every Ferrero Rocher and jar of Nutella for North America comes out of Brantford, Ontario — Ferrero's only plant on the continent, more than thirteen hundred jobs, recently expanded by nearly half a billion dollars. The Italian gold-foil ball is a Brantford product. This aisle is more Canadian-made than its ownership chart suggests — which is exactly why the words on the wrappers deserve the scrutiny.

Best option number two — Laura Secord. The chocolate box of every Canadian holiday since 1913 — founded by Frank O'Connor in one shop on Yonge Street and named for the woman who walked thirty kilometres through the woods to warn the British in 1813. Real chocolate, then and now. But the reason it's on the winners list is the ownership story, because it's the reverse of everything else in this video: Laura Secord spent four decades being passed around — John Labatt in 1969, Britain's Rowntree in 1983, then a Chicago candy company, then liquidators — until 2010, when two brothers from Quebec City, Jean and Jacques Leclerc, bought Canada's chocolate brand back for about twenty million dollars. It's been Canadian-owned and Quebec-made ever since — the only brand in this video that left the country and came back. Full disclosure, as always: the chain has been closing some mall stores as shopping habits shift, and in July 2025 there was a Class II recall of some of its chocolate chips for an undeclared peanut — handled through the normal process, stated here for the record. The brand isn't perfect. It's just ours again — a company named for a woman who out-walked an invasion, bought back by two brothers from Quebec City — and in this aisle, a homecoming turned out to be the rarest story of all.

Best option number one — Ganong. St. Stephen, New Brunswick — a town that smells like chocolate. Ganong Brothers has been making candy there since 1873. It is Canada's oldest candy company, and it has never been sold: five generations of the same family, currently run by Bryana Ganong. The lore is irresistible — by most accounts, it was Arthur Ganong, packing chocolate for a fishing trip, who gave North America its first five-cent chocolate nut bar, and the company introduced the continent's first heart-shaped Valentine's box in 1932. Real chocolate, real cocoa butter, still making Pal-o-Mine, Delecto boxes and the gloriously weird Chicken Bones in the same town after a century and a half. And they're not alone: out west, Purdys of Vancouver — family-run since 1963, Karen Flavelle at the helm — hand-produces eighty thousand Hedgehogs a day on Kingsway. Here's the point of this whole ranking: while the multinationals shaved cocoa butter out of your childhood one recipe at a time, the companies that never needed the word "chocolatey" are the two family shops at opposite ends of the country. A hundred and fifty years of choosing the expensive ingredient — that's what the top of this list looks like.

So here's the whole candy aisle in one test, and it takes five seconds. Turn the bar over and find the fat. If you see cocoa butter near the front of the list — or the words "milk chocolate" with a bracket that starts with milk or sugar and cocoa butter — you're holding chocolate as Canadian law defines it, one of the strictest definitions anywhere: zero vegetable fat allowed, stricter than Europe's. If you see modified palm oil where the cocoa butter should be — and somewhere on the package, that one small word, "chocolatey" — the wrapper has already confessed. That word is not a cute adjective. It's the legally mandated name for a different product, and the food agency's own guidance spells it out. One more label to watch this year: since January 1st, the new black-and-white "High in" symbols are rolling onto the front of these wrappers — most bars qualify for both saturated fat and sugars, at three grams and fifteen grams per forty-gram serving — so the shelf itself now does some of the scanning for you. For scale, a single bar carries roughly half of Health Canada's suggested daily free sugars — a fact, not a scolding. And a farewell to an old friend: the phrase "modified milk ingredients," which Canadians have squinted at for twenty years, is being retired by regulation — by 2030 those words become "milk-derived ingredients." The bracket you learned to read today will literally be rewritten. Nobody here is telling you to stop eating Coffee Crisp — I'd fight for this country's right to its weird coffee wafer — but you deserve to know that the law already sorted your aisle into chocolate and chocolatey, and that the answers have been printed in the brackets all along. Flip the bar in your cupboard tonight. Then send this to the person in your life who swears by Caramilk — because "what's the secret in Caramilk" finally has a second answer, and it's written on the back of the wrapper.
