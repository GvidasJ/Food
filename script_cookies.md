# SCRIPT — COOKIES EPISODE

TITLE: We Investigated 9 Cookie Brands In Canada (Only 3 Are ACTUALLY Canadian)

ALTERNATES:
- Mr. Christie Doesn't Own Your Cookies Anymore (We Read Every Bag)
- The Word "Chocolatey" Is Hiding On Your Cookie Bag (We Investigated 9 Brands)
- We Investigated Canada's Cookie Aisle (Check Your Bag For One Word)

---

Somewhere in your kitchen there's a bag of cookies, and I'd like you to go get it, because today is about one word printed on that bag — a word most Canadians have read a thousand times without ever really seeing it. Before we name names, let me tell you what we found in this aisle. There's a cookie brand that prints "crammed with real chocolate chips" while selling a version whose chips are legally not allowed to be called chocolate. There's a famous cookie whose own official name quietly contains the confession. There's a beloved brand whose jingle every Canadian over forty can sing on command — about a man whose company stopped being Canadian before most of us were born, and whose famous Toronto bakery is now a condo development with towers up to seventy storeys. There's a cookie invented in Montreal in 1900 that Quebec would go to war over, now baked by a family company in Kitchener that changed its own name because nobody could pronounce it. There's a Girl Guide cookie that went from ten cents a bag to six dollars a box — and even IT carries the word we're hunting today. And there's one bag in this aisle — the famous one, from 1988 — whose label beats every national brand in the store, and it's the store's own.

We read every bag, we looked up who owns every brand, and we read the law those labels answer to. And here's the legal punchline up front: in Canadian food law, the cookie itself is undefined — we could find no standard prescribing what a cookie must contain, same as soup. But the CHOCOLATE in the cookie? Defined to the decimal. The federal standards say chocolate must contain "not less than 35% total cocoa solids, of which not less than 18% is cocoa butter." Swap the cocoa butter for palm oil and the CFIA's own guidance says those chips "cannot legally be called chocolate" — the accepted words are, and I quote the regulator, "chocolate flavoured," "chocolate-like" and "chocolaty." So every bag in this aisle had a choice to make, and the word it chose is printed right there. That's today's entire investigation: chocolate, or chocolatey. One letter's worth of law. If you're holding your bag right now, hit the like button before you flip it over — every person who watches this is one more Canadian reading the back of the bag instead of the front, and that's the only thing that has ever changed a recipe. Let's go, most popular first.

Brand one: Chips Ahoy — by the maker's own claim, printed on its Canadian website, "Canada's #1 Chocolate Chip Cookie Brand." And this is the most interesting bag in the store, because the chocolate-versus-chocolatey line runs right through the middle of the brand. Chips Ahoy Original, from the company's own Canadian ingredient list: "Semi-sweet chocolate chips — sugar, unsweetened chocolate, cocoa butter…" Cocoa butter. Real chocolate. The front-of-bag boast, "crammed with real chocolate chips," checks out on the Original. Now pick up the red bag beside it — Chips Ahoy CHEWY, same brand, same shelf — and read its list: not one but two entries called, verbatim, "Chocolatey chips." The first is built on "modified palm oil and/or modified vegetable oil." The second on "hydrogenated palm kernel oil and/or hydrogenated palm oil." All perfectly legal, all correctly declared — the law did its job, the label tells the truth. But here's what made us sit up: at the time of research, the company's own product page for the Chewy still carried the brand's blanket line about "real chocolate chips" — on a page whose own ingredient list says "chocolatey." We'll read both on camera, side by side, and note that the CFIA's guidance on highlighted ingredients says emphasis on an ingredient "should be accompanied by a statement regarding the amount." We're not alleging anything — we're reading a company's website next to its own ingredient list and letting you hear both. That's the whole channel.

Brand two: Oreo. The best-selling cookie on Earth, by most global accounts — and in Canada it's sold under the Christie banner and, on many bags, baked in Canada; check your bag for the line, because it varies by format. Two things on this label, and both are law stories. First: the very first ingredient on the Canadian family-size bag is — verbatim — "Sugars (sugar, glucose-fructose)." Long-time viewers know both halves of that: glucose-fructose is Canada's legal name for high-fructose corn syrup, and the reason "Sugars" leads the list with brackets is a 2016 law, fully in force since December 2021, that forces every sugar-based ingredient to be grouped together and counted as one. Before that law, sugar and glucose-fructose could sit apart in the list, each looking smaller. The recipe didn't change. The law changed what the first word is. Second: the bag's proud claim is "always made with real cocoa." Read that twice. Real COCOA — the powder. Not real CHOCOLATE. Unsweetened chocolate does appear — tenth on the list, behind the baking soda. In an aisle where words are chosen by lawyers, "real cocoa" is a very precisely chosen pair of words. And a bonus for the label nerds: the family-size list includes TBHQ and citric acid; the regular size we checked doesn't — same cookie, different bag, different fine print. Nobody notices. You will now.

Brand three: Fudgee-O — and Canada, this one is ours alone. It isn't sold in the United States except as an import; American expats pay double to have it shipped south, which is the sincerest compliment a cookie can receive. But the word of the day is hiding in plain sight here, in the most charming place possible: the cookie's full legal name, on the company's own site, is "Fudgee-O CHOCOLATEY Creme Filled Sandwich Cookies." The confession is the name. The list backs it up: cocoa, yes; unsweetened chocolate, seventh; no cocoa-butter chocolate anywhere. Nobody's hiding anything — it's printed on the front in that cheerful bubble font, and generations of us read it as a flavour when it was actually a legal classification. Coffee Crisp viewers, you know this move: "chocolatey" is the same word doing the same job it does on that yellow wrapper.

Now — the man himself, because brands one through three all answer to the same company, and that company's Canadian story deserves telling properly. William Mellis Christie arrived from Scotland in 1848, age nineteen, and went to work for a Toronto baker that September. By 1868 he had his name on Christie, Brown and Company; by the time he died in 1900, his firm was — in the words of the Dictionary of Canadian Biography — "widely regarded as the largest such enterprise in Canada," with hundreds of workers. The family sold in the twenties; Nabisco took control in 1928; Kraft swallowed Nabisco in 2000; and when Kraft split in 2012, the cookies went to Mondelez International of Chicago. That's who Mr. Christie is today. The jingle — "Mr. Christie, you make good cookies" — the one you just sang in your head — was still running on Canadian TV in the eighties; the man it names had by then been dead for over eighty years and the company American for over fifty. And then came the hard decade: in November 2012, Mondelez announced the closure of the Mr. Christie bakery at Park Lawn and Lake Shore in Etobicoke — baking there since 1948 — five hundred and fifty jobs. Today that site is the "2150 Lake Shore" master plan: up to fifteen towers, some seventy storeys. In 2017, the Montreal plant on rue Viau followed — four hundred and fifty-four more jobs. So it's all gone, right? Here's the twist we didn't expect to find: no. Mondelez's own corporate page, current this month, lists its East York bakery on Bermondsey Road — a street literally named in 1949 after the London borough where Peek Frean built "Biscuit Town" — as, quote, "the largest Mondelez bakery in terms of volume in Canada," baking Peek Freans and feeding the North American Oreo network, with a second plant in Scarborough baking Dad's oatmeal cookies. Toronto still bakes big-brand cookies — two plants, roughly a thousand jobs — it just does it quietly, without a jingle.

And since we're standing in East York, give Peek Freans its due, because it's the best-kept secret in the aisle. The name is two actual Victorians — James Peek, a tea importer, and George Hender Frean, a miller and ship's-biscuit maker — who started baking in London in 1857 and built a factory so big their neighbourhood was nicknamed Biscuit Town. The London factory closed in 1989. The Canadian one, opened 1949, outlived it — and Mondelez makes a claim about it we have to read verbatim: "The bourbon creme cookie that goes into our Assorted Creme package is the oldest mass-produced sandwich cookie in the world." The oldest sandwich cookie on Earth, by the company's own account, baked in East York. There's even a factory outlet store on O'Connor Drive where Torontonians have been buying seconds for decades. The labels here are a mixed report card, and we'll read both kinds: the plain Digestive lists wheat bran, butter — real butter — and milk; the "Family Digestive" beside it wears a — say it with me — "chocolatey coating," modified palm oil and all. And the Fruit Crème deserves thirty seconds of bracket-reading all by itself: the fruit in the filling is "raspberry juice concentrate," and the pink colour comes from "red cabbage juice concentrate." There is cabbage juice in the fruit cookie. Perfectly legal, perfectly declared, and perfectly wonderful to know. One economics note while the box is up: at the time of research the same 300-gram Peek Freans Digestive was listed at three twenty-nine at one grocery banner and five ninety-nine at another — a near-double spread on the identical biscuit. The aisle you shop in matters as much as the bag you pick.

And Dad's — the oatmeal cookie of every Canadian church basement — is a Christie brand too, baked at the Scarborough plant, and its history is stranger than fiction: it began as a US cookie franchise in 1920s California, the American branches died out, the three Canadian franchisees merged in 1972, Nabisco absorbed it in 1986, and today, per CBC, Canada is the only country on Earth where Dad's is sold. An American cookie that survives only in Canada — the mirror image of Fudgee-O. When Christie discontinued Dad's chocolate chip in 2017, the CBC covered the fan outcry like a state funeral. We keep what we love, even when Chicago owns it. And one myth while we're here: Christie Pits park was officially named for the cookie man in 1983 — but Toronto history researchers now argue Christie STREET was named in 1835 for a completely different Christy, when William was a six-year-old in Scotland. We'll leave that one as an open question for the comment section.

Brand four: President's Choice The Decadent — the 1988 challenger. The story, as the President's Choice history tells it: Dave Nichol set out to beat Chips Ahoy, and Colonial Cookies of Kitchener spent over a year and a hundred-plus test batches building a cookie with — their number — thirty-nine percent chocolate chips by weight, "more than double the leading national brand," made with real butter, priced BELOW the big brand. Within three years, by that same account, it was Canada's best-selling cookie despite being sold only in Loblaw stores. Now read today's label against brand one, because this is the side-by-side of the episode. The Decadent's first ingredient: "Chocolate chips — unsweetened chocolate, sugar, dextrose, cocoa butter, soy lecithin, vanilla extract." Real chocolate, first position. Third ingredient: butter. Then real egg. Vanilla EXTRACT, not artificial flavour. The store brand out-labels the national brand it was built to beat, thirty-eight years later, and at the time of research it cost about the same per hundred grams. One mystery remains, and it's our favourite kind: the bag says only "Prepared for Loblaws Inc." The Kitchener bakery that created it was owned by Weston, sold in 2021 to an American contract giant, which went through bankruptcy and re-emerged last year renamed "Maker's Pride" — who bakes The Decadent today, the bag will not tell you. Some things in Canadian groceries never change.

A quick sweep of the rest of the discount shelf: No Name sells a two-pound family bag for five dollars — cheapest cookies in the store by far — and to its credit, the yellow label doesn't even try the trick: it says, right on the front, "Chocolately Chip Cookies." Not chocolate chip. The no-frills brand is the most honest about the word. And those Two-Bite brownies by the checkout that feel like a little local bakery thing? Give & Go, Toronto — majority-owned by Mondelez since 2020, a billion-dollar deal. The map keeps leading to Chicago.

Brand five — and now the aisle starts giving back: Dare. Kitchener, Ontario. Founded in 1892 by Charles Doerr in what was then called Berlin, Ontario — the city renamed itself Kitchener in 1916 amid wartime anti-German feeling, and in 1945 the company simplified Doerr to "Dare" for the recorded reason that it was easier to pronounce. The history is genuinely dramatic: Doerr died in 1941 and his grandson Carl took over at twenty-four — Carl had been raised by his grandparents after both his parents died in the Spanish flu; two years later a fire burned the factory down and killed the nightwatchman; Carl rebuilt the same year. Five generations on, Dare is still private, still family-run, still in Kitchener — described by the Globe and Mail as Canada's second-largest cookie company. And Dare holds two national treasures. First: the Whippet. Created in Montreal in 1900 by Théophile Viau as the "Empire" biscuit, renamed Whippet in 1927, and passed down through Quebec's Viau and Culinar dynasties until Dare bought the cookie business in 2001 — it's baked in Saint-Lambert, Quebec today, and its coating, per the label, is "Sweet Chocolate — sugar, cocoa butter, unsweetened chocolate…" Real chocolate on the marshmallow dome. Quebec, your cookie passed the test. Second: since 2003, Dare has baked Canada's Girl Guide cookies — the classic sandwich in Kitchener, the mint in Cambridge. Those cookies started in 1927 when a Guide leader in Regina named Christina Riepsamen baked batches so her daughter's company could afford camp, selling them a dozen to a paper bag for ten cents. Today the box is six dollars — the first price increase in over a decade came in 2023 — and here's the detail that proves today's word is everywhere: the official name of the fall cookie is the "Chocolatey Mint." Even the Girl Guides declare it properly. Meanwhile Bear Paws — the peanut-free lunchbox staple — lists real chocolate chips, cocoa butter and all, plus actual liquid whole egg. The family company pays for the cocoa butter. Remember that at the checkout.

Brand six: Leclerc. Quebec City, 1905 — François Leclerc baking his wife Zélia's cookie recipe in a backroom on Arago Street; a fire, a rebuild, and now a fifth generation running eight plants and, by the company's own count, a billion cookies a year. Their flagship is the Celebration — the butter cookie with the milk-chocolate square on top — and its label is the aisle's quiet valedictorian: "Milk chocolate — sugar, cocoa butter, chocolate liquor, milk ingredients…" then flour, sugar, and real butter, with a peanut-free certification. Real chocolate AND real butter, from a Quebec family firm, for about five dollars. When we get to the winners in a minute, you already know two of the three names.

Brand seven: Voortman — the wafer and windmill people from Burlington, Ontario, founded by Dutch immigrant brothers Bill and Harry in 1951. The cookies are still baked in Burlington. The ownership is a decade-long corporate relay race: the family sold to private equity in 2015; Hostess Brands — the Twinkie company — bought Voortman in January 2020 for about three hundred and twenty million US dollars; Smucker, the jam company, swallowed Hostess in 2023; and in late 2024 Smucker sold Voortman onward to Second Nature Brands of Michigan — itself owned by a London private-equity firm — for about three hundred and five million. Follow the arithmetic: bought for 320, sold for 305, four years and two parents later. The windmill on the package never moved. The company behind it moved four times.

Now the law-and-numbers round, because the cookie aisle delivered our favourite legal finding of this entire series. You know the new magnifying-glass symbol — "High in sugars," "High in saturated fat" — mandatory on qualifying packages since January first. The trigger for most foods is fifteen percent of the daily value. But Health Canada's reference amount for cookies is exactly thirty grams — about two cookies — and foods with a reference amount of thirty grams or less get the STRICTER trigger: ten percent. Soup, with its 250-millilitre reference bowl, triggers at fifteen. In other words: Canadian law holds a cookie to a tougher line than a bowl of soup. Ten percent of the sugars daily value is ten grams; ten percent of the saturated-fat value is just two grams — do that math against the Nutrition Facts on the bag in your hand; we'll read real panels on camera rather than quote anyone else's numbers. And the regulation has an anti-dodge clause we admire: the percentage is computed "per serving of stated size or per reference amount, whichever is greater" — so no company can escape the symbol by printing a cuter, smaller serving size. Which matters, because packages themselves keep shrinking: our bags today weigh 258 grams, 271, 303 — nobody designs a 258-gram bag from scratch. The consumer watchdog Edgar Dworsky told CBC that cookies are among the products "downsized multiple times over the years — and I don't think it's going to stop." Canada, unlike Brazil or South Korea, has no law requiring shrinkflation disclosure; the packages can shrink, but the thirty-gram legal yardstick cannot — the law, at least, refuses to downsize. And the receipts: Statistics Canada prices "cookies and sweet biscuits, 300 grams" as an official line item — two thirty-two in January 2017, three thirty-nine as of this summer, up about forty-six percent — with a record cocoa spike in 2024 passing through every chocolate chip along the way. One more from the files: last December a cookie-dough recall for undeclared egg was caught with one reported reaction — and our favourite entry in the federal recall database remains the gluten-free cookies once recalled for containing undeclared gluten. The system reads labels too.

Three quick delights before the winners, because this aisle kept giving. One: look at the front of almost any Canadian cookie package and you'll see the national compromise in two words — "Cookies / Biscuits." Federal law requires both official languages on the label, and the French for cookie is biscuit — which means a box of Peek Freans in Canada is a British biscuit, labelled "cookie" in English and "biscuit" in French, and everyone is somehow correct. The word itself is Latin — bis coctus, "twice baked" — so your grandmother's rusks were the original. Two: the Oreo, king of the aisle, is famously not even the original — the Hydrox sandwich cookie beat it to market in 1908, Oreo arrived in 1912, and the copy buried the original so completely that people now assume it was the knock-off. And three: your grandmother's wartime cookie recipes exist because sugar was rationed in 1942 — about half a pound per week per person under the Wartime Prices and Trade Board — which is why so many Canadian heirloom recipes stretch a cup of sugar with oats, dates and molasses. The cookie tin remembers more history than we do.

So — the winners, and this episode has the happiest podium we've had in weeks. If Canadian-owned is your compass, exactly three of today's nine qualify. Dare — five generations in Kitchener, real chocolate on the Whippet and in the Bear Paw, and the keeper of the Girl Guide cookie. Leclerc — five generations in Quebec City, real chocolate and real butter in the Celebration. And President's Choice The Decadent — owned by a Canadian grocer, born in Kitchener in 1988, still carrying the best ingredient list on the shelf, even if the bag won't say who bakes it. Honourable mentions, honestly given: Peek Freans — American-owned, but baked in East York on a street named for London's Biscuit Town, with real butter in the digestive; and Fudgee-O and Canadian-baked Oreos, which keep about a thousand Toronto bakery jobs alive under the Christie name. And to be fair to the whole aisle: nothing we read today is hidden. "Chocolatey" is printed exactly where the law requires, on every single bag. Most of us just never knew it was a legal term. Now you do — and you can never unsee it.

Look — nobody's blaming you for the cookies in your cupboard. Most of us grew up on half the names in this video, and a cookie is supposed to be a small, uncomplicated pleasure. What changed isn't you; it's that the words on the bag became legal instruments while the pictures stayed nostalgic. So here's your five-second cookie-aisle routine: find the chip in the ingredient list — "chocolate" with cocoa butter, or "chocolatey" with palm oil; check whether "Sugars" leads the list and read what's in the brackets; glance at the grams, because the bag is smaller than your memory says; and if the magnifying glass is on the front, now you know exactly which math put it there.
