First, sixty seconds of cottage cheese law. Since December 2024, the legal definition has lived in section 7.4.5 of the Canadian Food Inspection Agency's food compositional standards, and the Food and Drug Regulations make it binding. It says cottage cheese is the product, in the form of discrete curd particles, prepared from skim milk, evaporated skim milk or skim milk powder and harmless acid-producing bacterial cultures. It may hold no more than 80 percent moisture. And it may contain milk, cream, milk powder, salt, added lactose, relishes, fruits and vegetables. That is the whole list. Section 7.4.6 adds one more name, creamed cottage cheese, at not less than 4 percent milk fat, and the milk fat number must be printed on the front. The regulations say a standardized food must contain only the ingredients set out in its standard, and the next section lets in substances the regulations permit elsewhere. That is the door the additives use. Gums, acids and enzymes are not in the standard at all. Until December 17th, 2024, the old regulation capped stabilizing agent at 0.5 per cent inside the definition itself. Now those additives are allowed through Health Canada's separate lists of permitted food additives, and the Canadian Dairy Commission's own page says stabilizing agents can be added at levels up to half a percent. All of that is legal. Now the part that matters. The standard contains two names. Cottage cheese. Creamed cottage cheese. Search the standard and the regulations for the words cottage cheese product, and you get nothing. And there is no Canada grade for cottage cheese, the way there is for butter and cheddar, so the name and the list are all you get. One more rule. Ingredients are listed by weight, heaviest first, but food additives may be shown at the end in any order. So we hold every tub to two things. The name on the front, and the list on the back, read against the list in the law. We are not saying any tub breaks a rule. We are showing you where the front and the back part ways.

How to protect yourself. First, read the name on the front. It should say cottage cheese, or creamed cottage cheese at 4 percent milk fat or more. Any other word in that name, product included, is the company's choice, and the milk fat number beside it is there because the law requires it. Second, read the whole ingredient line. The standard's own list is skim milk and cultures, and it may add milk, cream, milk powder, salt and lactose. Everything else comes in through a separate list. Count the items, and remember that additives can sit at the end in any order. Third, look for the owner and the milk. The label must name the company responsible, which for a store brand is often the store, not the dairy. The blue cow means, in Dairy Farmers of Canada's words, made with 100 percent Canadian milk and milk ingredients. It does not tell you who owns the brand. And under the food agency's origin guidelines, a dairy product can say product of Canada when the cow is milked in Canada. Fourth, compare the price per 100 grams on the shelf tag, never the price per tub. On September 30th, every short-list tub cost more per 100 grams than the 2% tubs we compared it with.

Nine brands, six owners, one lesson, and none of it needs anyone to break a rule. The law already tells you what cottage cheese is. The companies decide how loudly the label says it.
