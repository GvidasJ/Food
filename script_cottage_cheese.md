First, sixty seconds of cottage cheese law. Since December 2024, the legal definition has lived in section 7.4.5 of the Canadian Food Inspection Agency's food compositional standards, and the Food and Drug Regulations make it binding. It says cottage cheese is the product, in the form of discrete curd particles, prepared from skim milk, evaporated skim milk or skim milk powder and harmless acid-producing bacterial cultures. It may hold no more than 80 percent moisture. And it may contain milk, cream, milk powder, salt, added lactose, relishes, fruits and vegetables. That is the whole list. Section 7.4.6 adds one more name, creamed cottage cheese, at not less than 4 percent milk fat. For both, the regulations require the milk fat number on the front. The regulations also say a standardized food must contain only the ingredients set out in its standard, except substances the regulations permit elsewhere. Gums, acids and enzymes are not in the standard at all. Until December 17th, 2024, the old regulation capped stabilizing agent at 0.5 per cent inside the definition itself. Now food additives come in by a separate route, Health Canada's lists of permitted food additives. Here is the part that matters. The standard contains two names. Cottage cheese. Creamed cottage cheese. Search the standard and the regulations for the words cottage cheese product, and you get nothing. There is no Canada grade for cottage cheese, the way there is for butter and cheddar, so the name and the list are what you get. One more rule. Ingredients are listed by weight, heaviest first, but food additives may be shown at the end in any order. So we hold every tub to two things. The name on the front, and the list on the back, read against the list in the law. We are not saying any tub breaks a rule. We are showing you where the front and the back part ways.

How to protect yourself. First, read the name on the front. It should say cottage cheese, or creamed cottage cheese at 4 percent milk fat or more. Descriptive words like dry curd, pressed or low fat can sit around it. Watch for the word product, which is not one of the standard's two names. Second, read the whole ingredient line. The standard's own list is skim milk and cultures, and it may add milk, cream, milk powder, salt, lactose, relishes, fruits and vegetables. Additives come in through Health Canada's separate lists. Count the items, and remember that additives can sit at the end in any order. Third, look for the owner and the milk. The label must name the company responsible, which for a store brand is often the store, not the dairy. The blue cow means, in Dairy Farmers of Canada's words, made with 100 percent Canadian milk and milk ingredients. Some makers, Saputo among them, print their own Canadian milk mark instead, and the food agency says any 100 percent Canadian milk claim must meet its criteria, logo or not. Fourth, compare the price per 100 grams on the shelf tag, never the price per tub. On September 30th, every short-list tub cost more per 100 grams than the cheapest 2% tub in the same store.

Nine brands, six owners, one lesson, and none of it needs anyone to break a rule. The law already tells you what cottage cheese is. The label tells you what is in the tub. Read all of it. APPENDIX B — DO NOT SAY (on air, lower-thirds, description, pinned comment or thumbnail)

- Any health, nutrition, protein, "high-protein", "protein craze", calorie, fat-as-health, sodium, "no salt added", gut, probiotic or "live culture" line. The shortage stories all carry protein framing; use none of it.
- Any characterisation of an ingredient: filler, cheap, chemical, synthetic, industrial, lab-made, engineered, ultra-processed, "gums to fake thickness", "bulk", "water weight". Titanium dioxide, polysorbate 80, carrageenan and modified corn starch are read only as words inside a verbatim list, or not at all. Never single one out.
- That any tub "fails", "breaks", "violates" or "does not meet" the standard, or is "not real", "fake" or "not legally cottage cheese". The on-air short-list test is the channel's own test, defined on air. Do not quote CFIA's "artisan-style" best-practice sentence next to the Natrel tub. Do not set "discrete curd particles" against the Nordica Smooth name. Do not describe the Sealtest licence as "borrowed", or "product" as hidden "small print".
- Why Sealtest says "product" and Northumberland does not. No speculation (open item 3).
- That No Name and Beatrice, Compliments and Dairyland/Baxter, Compliments Dry Curd and Dairyland Dry Curd, or Farm Boy and M-C Dairy are made by the same dairy. Only "the lists match word for word".
