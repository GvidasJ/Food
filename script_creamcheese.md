These two packages are the same brand, from the same store, at the same price. Five forty-nine each. Look closer. The brick says Cream Cheese. The tub says Cream Cheese Product. That word, product, is not decoration. It is a legal confession, printed right on the front, and by the end of this video you'll be able to read it the way an inspector does.

Because here's what we found when we went through nine brands of cream cheese and cheese spreads sold in Canada. The name on the front of a Canadian cheese package is a sworn statement. Cream cheese, cream cheese spread, cheese spread, cold pack cheese food, fresh cheese. Five different names. Five different legal recipes. And once you know the ladder, you can stand in the dairy aisle and know the legal minimum milk fat of everything on the shelf without turning a single package around.

Along the way: the most famous name in cream cheese was invented in New York and named after a city it's never been made in. The French cheese in your fridge is made by a Quebec family fromagerie. The little red tub in your grandmother's icebox is the reason Kraft exists in Canada at all. And one terrible summer, this country lost its two most beloved local schmears in the same season.

We bought the bricks. We bought the tubs. We pulled the federal standards, the ownership records, and the prices. We read every label. That's all we do.

First, the rulebook, because this episode runs on it. Canada has federal compositional standards for cheese, and in December 2024 Ottawa quietly moved them from the Food and Drug Regulations into a new document managed by the food inspection agency. Same rules, new address. Here's what they say. To be called cream cheese, the product must be made by coagulating cream, it must contain at least thirty percent milk fat and no more than fifty-five percent moisture, and the recipe the standard allows is stunningly short: the cream, bacterial culture to set it, more cream to adjust the fat, and salt. That's the whole permitted pantry, apart from a short list of additives the law caps at half of one percent.

And then the ladder goes down. Cream cheese spread: at least fifty-one percent actual cream cheese, but the milk fat floor drops to twenty-four percent and the moisture ceiling rises to sixty. Processed cheese food: at least fifty-one percent cheese, twenty-three percent fat. Processed cheese spread: fifty-one percent cheese, and the fat floor bottoms out at twenty percent. Thirty, twenty-six, twenty-four, twenty-three, twenty. Every step down the ladder, less milk fat is legally required and more water is legally allowed. And federal law says that if you use a standardized name, you must meet that standard. Which means the name is the confession. Nobody chooses to write product on their own package for fun.

Now the countdown. Most popular first, and in this category there is no suspense about where that starts.

Number one: Philadelphia.

The 2026 BrandSpark Most Trusted Awards surveyed more than forty-five thousand Canadian shoppers, and the cream cheese winner was Philadelphia. A competitor's own brand manager admitted in a trade interview that the category, quote, is dominated by one major competitor. It's not close. So let's read the most trusted label in the aisle.

Start with the name itself, because it's the best marketing story in dairy. Philadelphia cream cheese has never been made in Philadelphia. It was developed in the early 1870s by a dairyman named William Lawrence in Chester, New York, who was trying to make a French-style cheese and landed on something richer. In 1880 his distributor slapped the name Philadelphia on it, because back then the Philadelphia area had a reputation for fine dairy, the way Paris has one for perfume. The most famous name in cream cheese is a borrowed address. The brand passed to the Phenix Cheese Company, which merged with Kraft in 1928, and today it belongs to Kraft Heinz, the American giant assembled in 2015 by Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway and the 3G Capital investment firm. Last September Kraft Heinz announced it was splitting itself in two; this February, a new chief executive paused the whole thing. The company that owns your breakfast is having a complicated decade.

Now the two packages from our cold open. The brick, the one in the foil, is legally Cream Cheese. Its ingredients: milk, cream, skim milk powder, salt, bacterial culture, guar gum and or carob bean gum, sorbic acid. The printed numbers, per two tablespoons: 125 milligrams of sodium, five percent of your daily value, and six grams of saturated fat plus a third of a gram of trans, thirty-two percent. The tub beside it swaps in something called modified milk ingredients, and that one change moves it outside the cream cheese standard's little recipe box. So the front of the tub says Cream Cheese Product. Same logo. Same shelf. Different laws. Kraft isn't hiding it — it's printed in plain sight, and now you'll never not see it.

Here's the part most Canadians don't know. Your Philadelphia is not American. Every brick for this country comes out of Kraft Heinz's Mont-Royal plant in Montreal, over a thousand workers, and this March the company announced a two hundred and fifty million dollar modernization of that exact factory. This is also why December 2021 played so differently on the two sides of the border. You may remember headlines about the great cream cheese shortage: that was an American crisis, centred on a strained New York plant and a cyberattack on a US supplier, and it got so bad Kraft offered eighteen thousand Americans twenty dollars each not to bake cheesecake. That twenty-dollar offer was US-only. We found no reports of Canadian shelves running dry. While New York panicked, Montreal kept schmearing.

And if you're over forty-five, you already know the soundtrack to this brand. The angels. Five commercials between 1994 and 1997, made for Canada, starring Canadian actress Linda Kash in the clouds: when it's Philadelphia, it's heaven. One of the most successful ad campaigns in Canadian history, and it sold a New York invention named after Pennsylvania, made in Montreal, owned in Chicago. That sentence is the whole dairy aisle in miniature.

Number two: the store brands. Compliments, President's Choice, No Name, Selection, Great Value, Western Family.

This is where your money quietly goes, and the price gap is the story. The Compliments brick at Sobeys' own online store: four twenty-nine for 250 grams, a dollar seventy-two per hundred grams, and its ingredient list is four items long — milk ingredients, bacterial culture, salt, carob bean gum — with 90 milligrams of sodium per serving printed on the panel. That is the shortest mainstream list we found, on the cheapest verified brick we found. Philadelphia, same store, is five forty-nine. The name on the Compliments package, by the way: Cream Cheese, no asterisks, full standard.

So who actually cultures and packs the store-brand cheese? Here is the honest answer: the label won't tell you, and neither could we. Store brands carry a prepared-for line — prepared for Loblaws, prepared for Sobeys — and the packer's name is not disclosed anywhere public. Walmart's Great Value brick lists almost the same four ingredients as the Compliments brick. Is it the same vat? That is a fair question to ask on camera, and an unfair thing to claim without proof, so we won't. What we can tell you is who owns the brands: Loblaw and its No Name yellow — launched 1978 under Dave Nichol, your fridge probably wore it — belong to the Weston family's empire, Canadian since 1882. Compliments is Sobeys, out of Stellarton, Nova Scotia. Selection is Metro, Montreal. Western Family is the Jim Pattison Group, British Columbia. And Great Value is Walmart, Arkansas. One fine-print flag for your western viewers: the Western Family listing describes a Canadian-prepared product made from domestic and imported ingredients. Read that phrase on the pack. It means prepared here; it does not promise the milk is all Canadian. We'll come back to the logo that does.

Number three: Cheez Whiz.

The jar. Be honest, your family had one, and it went on celery, on toast, on crackers at every card night your parents ever hosted. Cheez Whiz came out of Kraft's labs in the early fifties — one of the food scientists on that team, Edwin Traisman, later developed the process McDonald's used to freeze its fries, a fact food historians love and we now do too. Two things about the Canadian jar. First, it's ours: Cheez Whiz for this market is certified as prepared in Quebec. Second, it is not the same recipe as the American jar — food writers who've compared them report the US version dropped cheese from the formula entirely, while the Canadian one still contains it. Where, exactly? Read the list with us: modified milk ingredients, water, vegetable oil, and then cheese, in fourth place. Sodium, printed: 410 milligrams per two tablespoons, eighteen percent of your daily value — next to the Philadelphia brick's 125. Remember the ladder from the top of the video: anything sold as a processed cheese spread must by law be at least fifty-one percent cheese with at least twenty percent milk fat. Kraft's own Canadian page titles this product, simply, Cheese Spread. We bought a jar, and on camera we're going to read exactly what the fine print on the label calls it. And no, Cheez Whiz was never banned in Canada — that's an internet myth. What Canada never got was the spray can. Some borders defend themselves.

Number four: The Laughing Cow.

April 1921, France: cheesemaker Léon Bel registers a trademark of a laughing red cow, drawn by the illustrator Benjamin Rabier. On his wife's advice, the cow got earrings — and look closely at them, because they are tiny boxes of Laughing Cow, each with a cow wearing earrings of her own. Your grandchildren would call that recursion. The brand reached Canada in 1956, by Bel's own account, and here's the beat nobody expects: since August 2007, nearly every wedge sold in this country has been made in St-Nicolas, Quebec, by Fromagerie Bergeron, a family cheesemaker working under contract for Bel — over a billion individual portions from one hundred percent Canadian milk, by the company's own count. French brand, Quebec cheese. But flip the boxes in the display, because one brand is hiding three passports: the regular wedges are from Quebec, the lactose-free version is imported from Slovakia, and the Cheese Dippers come from France. Same cow, three continents' worth of supply chain. The legal name on the Canadian pack, per the store listings: Processed Cheese Product. And one arithmetic trap worth filming: the sixteen-wedge box works out to two eighty-one per hundred grams, while the eight-wedge box of the identical wedges runs four fifty — sixty percent more per gram for the smaller cow.

Number five: Boursin.

The premium puck on the holiday tray. François Boursin launched his dairy in Normandy in 1957, and the brand's own history says its most famous flavour began as a newspaper mistake: a paper announced a Boursin with garlic that didn't exist, France demanded it, and by 1963 garlic and fine herbs was real. Unilever owned it for years and sold it to France's Bel Group — the Laughing Cow people — in 2007 for four hundred million euros. So the two Frenchest things in your fridge share an owner. And both of them are made in Quebec. Since January 2011, the Boursin sold here has been produced in Saint-Hyacinthe by Agropur — a Canadian dairy farmers' co-operative, making the French luxury cheese under licence, from Canadian milk, with the blue cow logo to prove it. The label reads like a kitchen, not a lab: pasteurized milk and cream, dried garlic, herbs, salt, white pepper, bacterial culture, potassium sorbate. The printed panel is also the steepest in this entire episode: eight grams of saturated fat per two tablespoons, forty-two percent of your daily value, printed right there. And it is the most expensive item we verified, five dollars and fifty-three cents per hundred grams — more than three times the Compliments brick. The most expensive spread on the shelf is Canadian milk in a French costume. Its legal name in the dairy registry, believe it or not: Fresh Cheese.

Number six: MacLaren's Imperial.

The little red tub of sharp cold-pack cheddar, and the oldest story in this video. In 1891, Alexander MacLaren of Stratford, Ontario founded a cheese export company, and about a year later introduced MacLaren's Imperial — ground cheddar in small porcelain jars that the Dictionary of Canadian Biography calls, carefully, evidently one of the first soft, processed cheeses to be commercially distributed. Within a decade this Stratford operation had offices in London, New York, Chicago and China. Then, in 1920, it was bought by a Chicago outfit called J.L. Kraft and Brothers — and that purchase was Kraft's entry into Canada. Read that again. The red tub is the reason Kraft is here. The founder of Kraft, James Lewis Kraft, was himself born near Fort Erie, Ontario — an Ontario farm boy who built America's cheese empire and then bought his way back home. MacLaren's has now been American-owned for a hundred and five of its hundred and thirty-five years. On today's shelf it runs three sixty-nine per hundred grams, and here's a label puzzle for the shoot: Kraft's own Canadian page calls it a Cold Pack Cheddar Cheese Product, while a grocery listing for the identical tub calls it Cold-Pack Cheese Food — which is a different rung of the ladder, with its own standard. Two names, one tub. The package in our hands will be the referee, on camera.

Number seven: Lactantia.

The challenger brick, and behind it, the biggest cheese deal in recent Canadian history. In 2018, Kraft Heinz sold its Canadian natural cheese business — Cracker Barrel, P'tit Québec, and the Ingleside, Ontario plant with about four hundred workers — to Parmalat for one point six two billion dollars, while keeping Philadelphia, Cheez Whiz and Kraft Singles for itself. Parmalat's Canadian arm then became Lactalis Canada, owned by the Lactalis Group of Laval, France. So when Lactantia launched a cream cheese brick, the French-owned company that bought Kraft's old Canadian cheese plants started attacking Kraft's crown jewel — with a brick made in Toronto, wearing the blue cow, priced at four forty-nine against Philadelphia's five forty-nine in the same store. And we have to salute what's printed on the front: thirty-one percent milk fat, fifty-five percent moisture. That is the legal standard for cream cheese, quoted on the package as a selling point. Somebody in that marketing department read the regulation and put it on the label. This episode approves. Note the family split here too: Lactantia's 250-gram brick is Cream Cheese; its 227-gram tub is a Cream Cheese Product. The brick-and-tub rule follows you across brands.

Number eight: Tre Stelle.

The Toronto brand with the Italian name, born of the National Cheese Company, a Toronto family business from 1960. Since 2004 it has belonged to Arla Foods — a Danish and Swedish farmer co-operative — which makes Tre Stelle the Scandinavian-owned Italian-Toronto cheese brand, a sentence that could only exist in Canada. In 2022 Tre Stelle went straight at the champion with its own cream cheese line, aimed at holiday cheesecake bakers. And its label hands us the perfect final exam for everything you've learned. The ingredient list is three items: pasteurized cream and skim milk, salt, bacterial culture. Three. The shortest list in the entire aisle. And the name on the package is Cream Cheese Product. How can the cleanest list wear the product name while seven-ingredient bricks get to say cream cheese? Go back to the standard: cream cheese must be made by coagulating cream, and beyond the culture, the standard's pantry allows only added cream and salt. Skim milk is not on that list. The name doesn't count ingredients; it tracks the legal recipe and the fat-and-moisture box. The lesson to take to the store: product doesn't mean worse, and cream cheese doesn't mean simpler. The name tells you which law the package lives under — the label tells you the rest.

Number nine: the Canadian-owned corner — and two goodbyes.

After eight segments of American, French, and Danish owners, here is what's actually Canadian-owned in this category. Organic Meadow, the organic pioneer with co-operative roots going back to Ontario farmers in 1989 — it went through creditor protection in 2015 and was reborn under Agrifoods, a western Canadian co-operative — still sells an organic cream cheese, and the name on it is the full, unmodified Cream Cheese. Gay Lea Foods, the co-op owned by more than fourteen hundred dairy farm families across Ontario and Manitoba, bought Toronto's Western Creamery in 2019 — the North York creamery whose kosher cream cheese spreads were a staple of the city's Jewish community for generations.

Which brings us to the sad part, and if you're from Montreal, put the bagel down. In 1936, the Kaporovsky family founded a dairy on the Plateau called Liberté. For over eighty years, Liberté cream cheese was the schmear of Montreal — the one the bagel shops swore by. A food historian put it simply: there was nothing else like it, and it was ingrained in the bagel culture of the city. In August 2022, its American owner, General Mills, discontinued it. The manager at St-Viateur Bagel — the shop founded in 1957 by Myer Lewkowicz, a Holocaust survivor who said that at Buchenwald all he dreamt of was a piece of bread — told the CBC that customers still walk in asking, where's my Liberté? It's a better cheese, he said. And that same month, Toronto's Western Creamery cream cheese quietly stopped appearing on shelves too — the trade press reported the line discontinued, and we could not find it in any store we checked. One summer. Both of Canada's beloved local schmears, gone. The bagels of Fairmount — baking since 1919 — carry on with what's left.

Before the verdict, three label rules to take shopping, and the arithmetic behind the aisle.

Rule one: the name is the law. Cream cheese, thirty percent milk fat minimum. Spread, twenty-four, and at least half real cream cheese. Processed cheese spread, twenty. Cold-pack cheese food, twenty-three. Product means the recipe stepped outside a standard — read on to see how. You now shop with an inspector's eyes.

Rule two: the magnifying glass is coming to this aisle, selectively. Canada's new front-of-package warning symbol became mandatory on new labels this January. For a cheese serving of thirty grams, the trigger is ten percent of your daily value — 230 milligrams of sodium, or two grams of saturated fat. Here's the twist written into the regulation: cheese can be exempt from the saturated fat symbol, but only if it delivers at least ten percent of your daily calcium per serving. Cheddar can pass that test. Cream cheese, at around two percent calcium per serving on the labels we read, fails it — so watch new cream cheese stock for the magnifying glass, while the cheddar next to it goes without. Meanwhile on sodium, most cream cheese sits far under the trigger at 90 to 150 milligrams; the printed 410 on the Cheez Whiz jar is another story. Same aisle, three different fates, all decided by arithmetic.

Rule three: the blue cow. That little logo is a certification from Dairy Farmers of Canada that the product is made with one hundred percent Canadian milk and milk ingredients. It's free for any company that qualifies. We found it on Lactantia and Boursin. Hunt for it on camera, package by package — because thanks to supply management, imported cheese pays a tariff of 245 and a half percent once the import quotas fill, which is the real reason nearly everything in this episode, whoever owns the brand, is made in Canada. Ownership went abroad. The vats stayed home: Bergeron makes the Laughing Cow, Agropur makes the Boursin, Mont-Royal makes the Philadelphia, Toronto makes the Lactantia.

And the prices: our verified range ran from a dollar seventy-two per hundred grams for the Compliments brick to five fifty-three for Boursin — a three-times gap on one shelf, and both ends of it are Canadian milk. Official inflation numbers hold a quiet surprise here too: Statistics Canada's cheese price index is up about twenty-four percent since early 2017, while food overall is up about forty-three. Cheese inflation ran at close to half the pace of your groceries. And Canada now produces roughly ten thousand tonnes of cream cheese every quarter — with the biggest quarter, every single year, being the holiday-cheesecake quarter. The statistics can see your baking.

So, the scoreboard. Philadelphia: American, made in Montreal. Cheez Whiz: American, made in Quebec. MacLaren's Imperial: American since 1920 — and the reason Kraft is here at all. The Laughing Cow and Boursin: French, made in Quebec. Lactantia: French, made in Toronto. Tre Stelle: Danish co-op, Toronto roots. Great Value: American retailer. Canadian-owned: your grocers' own labels, the co-ops — Organic Meadow, Gay Lea — and the memory of Liberté. In yesterday's episode, the ham aisle turned out to be secretly Canadian. The cheese-spread aisle is the mirror image: foreign flags on the front, Canadian milk in the vat, and the whole truth printed in the one place nobody looks — the name.
