# SCRIPT — COFFEE CREAMERS

You've been whitening your coffee your whole life, and I'm willing to bet you've never once read the jar. Seventy-one percent of Canadians drank coffee yesterday — it's the most consumed beverage in this country, ahead of tap water — and millions of those cups got a pour from a bottle or a canister with the word "creamer" somewhere on it. So we read every label in that aisle — the powders, the flavoured bottles, the little diner cups, the oat ones, and every cream carton beside them. That's all we did — we read the labels and the regulations behind them, from the federal definition of the word "cream" down to the French fine print — and here's what we found. The best-selling "creamer" in Canada contains no cream. Not less cream, not cheap cream — none. And its own packaging admits something even stranger, in two places at once, that we'll show you word for word. We found a store-brand jar whose French legal name translates literally as "coffee colouring." We found one Canadian company quietly making both the fakest product in this aisle and the realest one, at the same time. And we found the twist that makes this whole video worth your time: when you do the math per millilitre, real Canadian cream — the actual dairy product, from actual Canadian farms — costs the same as the fake stuff, and sometimes less. You've possibly been paying extra to avoid cream. Seven brands, biggest first, then the three moves that fix your coffee for good. Flip the jars over with us.

Number one. Coffee-Mate. Nestlé's own Canadian trade site calls it "Canada's preferred coffee whitener" — their words. The whole category started in 1952 with a powder called Pream, the first mass-market coffee creamer, but Pream had a problem: it still contained too much actual milk, and milk protein clumps in hot coffee. The breakthrough came in 1961 from the Carnation company — the evaporated-milk people — whose chemists figured out that if you replaced most of the milk with vegetable fat and sugar, the powder would dissolve beautifully. Read that sentence again: the innovation that created this billion-dollar category was removing the dairy from the dairy substitute. Nestlé bought Carnation in a deal announced in 1984 for roughly three billion US dollars — at the time, the biggest non-oil takeover in history. That's how seriously the food industry took powdered coffee whitener. Now read the canister with us. Coffee-Mate Original powder, first ingredient: glucose syrup. That's sugar. Second ingredient: hydrogenated palm kernel oil. Then polydextrose, dipotassium phosphate, modified milk ingredients, and down through the mono- and diglycerides to the colours — turmeric and annatto, which are there to make sugar and palm oil look cream-coloured. There is no cream. There is no milk, as you'd recognize it. And now the strangest thing in this entire video, and it's sitting on Nestlé's own product page in plain sight: the same page that describes this as a non-dairy, lactose-free whitener also carries the allergen statement — "Contains: Milk." Non-dairy. Contains milk. Both, at once, about the same jar. Here's the explanation, and it's the key to the whole aisle: those "modified milk ingredients" are casein — milk protein, the stuff that makes milk white — stripped out of milk and added back into a product built on sugar and palm oil. Milk protein makes it look and swirl like cream; no lactose means it can say lactose-free; and because milk protein is a legal allergen, federal law forces the word "milk" onto the label. And what does Canada's food agency say about calling something like that non-dairy? Their guidance, word for word: the claim "non-dairy" or "dairy-free" "cannot be used for products containing milk derivatives such as caseins or sodium caseinate," and — their words — "it is misleading" to claim it. Read your own canister on camera with us and see exactly which words Nestlé prints on the retail label. The liquid version, by the way — those little cups at every diner — is water, coconut oil, and sugar, with the same milk protein, engineered to sit unrefrigerated for nine months. And yes, Coffee-Mate now sells Coffee Crisp and KIT KAT flavoured versions — Nestlé crossing its own candy brands into your coffee — and if you watched our chocolate investigation, you already know what "chocolatey" means on a Nestlé label. There's even a product in the Canadian lineup called "Double Double-Mate," which is a remarkable piece of branding when you think it through: a product with no cream in it, named after a drink defined in the dictionary as coffee with double cream. One more number from the official page before we move on: the powder proudly advertises a two-year shelf life with no refrigeration necessary. Real cream lasts about two weeks. Nothing that behaves like cream for two years is cream, and the label has been telling you that the whole time — in the one place nobody looks.

Before we go to number two, thirty seconds on what the word "cream" legally means in this country, because everything else in this video hangs on it. Canada's food standards define cream in one sentence: "the fatty liquid prepared from milk by separating the milk constituents in such a manner as to increase the milk fat content." From milk. That's the whole test. And here's the companion definition that decides the entire aisle: under federal law, a dairy product is a food derived from milk "that contains no oil and no fat other than that of milk." The moment palm or coconut oil goes in, a product is — by definition, not by our opinion — not dairy. Here's a detail that surprised even us: the federal standard sets no minimum fat percentage for cream at all. The classes you know come from provincial law and tradition — in Ontario, half-and-half must be at least 10 percent milk fat, table cream between 16 and 32, whipping cream 32 to 40 — but the number itself is federally mandatory: cream must declare its milk fat right on the front, "M.F.", by federal regulation. That's why a cream carton always tells you exactly what it is, in a number you can compare across brands — and why the products in this video use every word except the ones with legal definitions. "Whitener." "Enhancer." "Creamer." Not one of those words appears in Canada's food standards. "Cream" does, and that's precisely why they don't use it.

Number two. International Delight. The flavour empire — French Vanilla, Hazelnut, Cinnabon, even Maple Latte. Start with who makes it, because this one's a matryoshka doll. The brand belongs to WhiteWave, which was bought by Danone of Paris in 2017. But flip the bottle and it boasts it's "proudly prepared in Canada" — and it is: securities filings show it's manufactured and sold in Canada by Saputo, the Montreal dairy giant. A French-owned American brand, made by a Canadian dairy company. Hold onto that fact — Saputo comes back at the end of this video in a way you won't expect. Now the label. French Vanilla, first three ingredients: water, sugar, palm oil. Then sodium caseinate — the milk protein again, with the site's own footnote rushing to add it "is not a source of lactose." Then the stabilizer stack, and finally "natural and artificial flavours." Notice what's not there. Cream isn't there. And vanilla — the actual bean, the thing "French Vanilla" is named for — is not named anywhere on that list. Five grams of sugars per fifteen-millilitre tablespoon, which means a two-tablespoon pour is ten grams — a tenth of the entire daily value the new nutrition labels are built on, in your morning cup, before breakfast. At five to six dollars a bottle at time of filming, you're paying cream prices for flavoured sugar-water with palm oil. Remember that number; the winners section is going to embarrass it. The flavour catalogue, meanwhile, is a genuine cultural document: Chocolate Chip Cookie, Pumpkin Spice, Pistachio, Cinnabon — an actual licensed cinnamon-bun flavour — Southern Butter Pecan, Vanilla Toffee Caramel, and, for us specifically, Maple Latte, which is a palm-oil product wearing our national tree. There are Zero Sugar versions and Fat Free versions, which is an interesting pair of claims for a product whose first three ingredients are water, sugar and fat. And the strangest part: International Delight's own iced-coffee line, one shelf over, advertises itself as "made with real milk, cream and cane sugar." Same brand, same fridge. They know exactly how to make it with real cream. They just know you'll pay the same either way.

If you've got one of these bottles in your fridge door right now — and most Canadian fridges have at least one — do us a favour and hit the like button. It genuinely helps these investigations reach the next person standing in that aisle. Now the store brands, because this is where it gets funny.

Numbers three, four and five. no name, Great Value, and Compliments coffee whiteners. Read all three and you realize they're the same product in different jackets — and the same product as the big brands, at that. no name: sugars, then coconut oil, then sodium caseinate — milk. Compliments: glucose solids, coconut oil, sodium caseinate. Great Value: corn syrup solids, hydrogenated coconut oil, sodium caseinate — plus, deep in the list, dimethylpolysiloxane, an anti-foaming agent you might remember from our soy sauce investigation. It's the same recipe everywhere: a sugar, a tropical oil, and milk protein to make it act like cream — plus silicon dioxide, which is powdered silica, there to keep the jar from clumping. Five dollars for a 450-gram jar at time of filming, and to be fair to the store brands, the powder genuinely is the one format cheaper than real cream — about two cents a cup versus fifteen. If pure economy is the goal, the jar wins. Just call it what the French side calls it. But the best part of the no name jar is the French side. Canadian labels are bilingual, and the French legal name printed on that jar is "colorant à café." Translate it. Coffee colouring. Not creamer, not whitener — colouring. Somewhere in a Loblaw office, a regulatory translator looked at powdered sugar-oil and told the absolute truth in one official language. The English side says "coffee whitener." The French side says what it is. Read both sides of everything, always — sometimes the honesty is hiding in the other official language.

Numbers six and seven. The plant-based "creamers" — Silk and Earth's Own. And we're going to be fair here, because these two are playing a slightly different game. They contain no dairy and, unlike everything above, they don't sneak milk protein in the back door — no caseinate, no milk allergen line. Earth's Own oat creamer is even made in Canada with one gram of sugar per tablespoon, a fraction of International Delight's five. So why are they on this list at all? Two reasons. First, the name game runs in both directions: in Canada, the word "milk" legally belongs to — and we quote the standard — "the normal lacteal secretion obtained from the mammary gland of the cow, genus Bos." That's why Silk's shelf label reads, in full legal splendour, "Vanilla Oat-Based Coffee Enhancer." Coffee enhancer. The law wouldn't let them say milk, and "creamer" is doing exactly the work you think it's doing. Second: the base is still filtered water, oat flour, vegetable oil and cane sugar at five fifty to six fifty a bottle — you're paying more per millilitre than real cream costs, for oats and water. If dairy-free is genuinely your goal, these are the honest choice in this video. Just know what the words on the front are — and aren't — allowed to say. And if you want proof the naming rules matter: this March, a Toronto company's "dairy-free" vegan ice cream was recalled across Ontario because it contained undeclared milk. The rules you learned in this video are the rules that catch that.

Now, a quick word about your grandmother, because she got this right. Carnation evaporated milk — the can she poured into her coffee — is still on Canadian shelves, and here's the double twist. One: in Canada, Carnation isn't even Nestlé anymore; Nestlé sold the Canadian canned-milk business to Smucker in 2007, and the label now reads "Carnation is a trademark of Société des Produits Nestlé, used under license." Two: unlike everything we've covered so far, the can is real milk — "milk with about half the water removed," as they put it. Which means the old-fashioned generation whitening coffee from a can of evaporated milk was closer to real dairy than almost anyone buying "creamer" today. The aisle didn't evolve. It devolved, profitably. And if you want to know how seriously this country once took the line between dairy and vegetable oil, here's a piece of history most Canadians have never heard: until June the first, 2004 — not 1954, 2004 — an Ontario statute called the Edible Oil Products Act effectively banned blending dairy with vegetable oil in that province. Western provinces actually fought Ontario over it as a trade barrier, and a trade panel ruled against Ontario before the law finally came off the books. Within living memory, the products in the first half of this video existed on the far side of a legal wall Ontario built specifically to keep oil from impersonating dairy. The wall came down. The impersonation, as you've now seen, moved in.

One more piece of honesty before the winners, about the new warning labels. You've heard us talk about the black-and-white magnifying-glass symbols now required on packages. Here's the counterintuitive part, and we did this math ourselves from federal nutrition data. The symbols are judged per serving, and cream's official reference serving is one tablespoon — fifteen millilitres. At that tiny serving, the flavoured creamer's five grams of sugars sits under the ten-gram trigger — no symbol. Meanwhile real whipping cream, 35 percent milk fat, crosses the saturated-fat trigger — symbol. So in the aisle we've just walked through, the January rules will flag the realest product and miss the fakest one. That's not a scandal; it's just what per-spoonful math does. But it's the best proof we can offer you that the magnifying glass can't do all your thinking. The ingredient list is still the only place the whole truth lives.

And now the Tim Hortons pivot, because no Canadian coffee video is complete without it. "Double-double" is literally in the Canadian Oxford Dictionary — added in 2004, in the edition published on the hundredth anniversary of Oxford's Canadian offices, after editors confirmed the word appeared everywhere from the Globe and Mail to the movie Men with Brooms. In 2023, Tims put grocery creamers on shelves — Double Double, Vanilla Dip Donut — and credit where due: the label leads with milk, sugar, cream. Real dairy, no vegetable oils, exactly what this video has been asking for. One catch: seven forty-nine for 750 millilitres at time of filming. That's a dollar per hundred millilitres — the most expensive dairy in this entire investigation, double the price of plain cream. It's real. It's also a branding tax. And in case you're wondering what Tims actually pours in the restaurant when you order a double-double — so were we, and here's the honest answer: Tim Hortons doesn't publicly specify the fat percentage of its in-store cream anywhere we could verify, and the carton in the grocery aisle doesn't say who manufactures it. For a drink that's literally in the dictionary, the details are remarkably off the record. Which brings us, finally, to what you should actually buy.

Winner number three: Lactantia. Ten percent half-and-half, about five dollars a litre at time of filming; eighteen percent table cream around five seventy to six. Ingredients: milk, cream, then a short stabilizer set — sodium citrate, carrageenan — the kind of thing dairies use so the carton pours the same in December as July. Now do the math we promised in the title. International Delight: five dollars for 946 millilitres — 53 cents per hundred mils. Coffee-Mate liquid: about 48. Lactantia half-and-half: 50 cents per hundred mils. Real cream — actual Canadian dairy — costs the same per millilitre as sugar-water with palm oil, and undercuts most of the flavoured bottles. The premium you thought real cream commanded doesn't exist. And it is Canadian, structurally: above a tiny quota, imported cream faces a 292.5 percent tariff, so the cream in Canadian fridges comes, in practice, from Canadian farms. Every whitener in this video is a multinational's recipe. Every cream carton is a Canadian farm's.

Winner number two: the two-ingredient cartons. In the East, Natrel Fine-Filtered half-and-half — ingredient list: "fine-filtered skim milk, cream." Two entries, no stabilizers, made by Agropur, a co-operative owned by around 2,700 Canadian dairy farm families. In the West, Dairyland 10% Creamo, whose complete ingredient list is the shortest we have ever read on this channel: "Milk, Cream." Two words. And wherever you are, look for Gay Lea table cream too — a co-operative owned by about twelve hundred Ontario and Manitoba farm families since 1958, the same people behind the Nordica and Ivanhoe names your parents knew. The pattern across the winners is the point: the real creams in this country are disproportionately made by farmer co-operatives — thousands of Canadian farm families — while every single product on the avoid list belongs to a multinational headquartered in Vevey, Paris, or a licensing deal between them. And here's the payoff we promised at number two: Dairyland belongs to Saputo — the same Montreal company that manufactures International Delight. One company, both ends of this entire video. The fakest bottle in the aisle and the purest carton in the country, same corporate parent, different fonts. Companies will sell you whatever you reach for. So reach carefully.

And winner number one — make your own. If what you actually love is French Vanilla, hear this: real vanilla and real cream is a thing you can just… combine. A litre of 18 percent cream, a spoonful of genuine maple syrup — the thing Canada makes better than anywhere on earth — and a splash of vanilla extract. Shake. That's a French Vanilla creamer with actual cream and actual vanilla, for roughly the same money as the bottle whose ingredient list contains neither. It takes ninety seconds, it keeps for a week and a half in the fridge, and every ingredient in it has a name your grandmother would recognize — which, after the aisle we've just walked, might be the highest compliment a label can earn. Run the numbers on it: a litre of table cream at about six dollars plus a few spoonfuls of maple syrup makes well over a litre of flavoured creamer for roughly seven dollars — against seven forty-nine for Tim Hortons' 750-millilitre bottle, or ten dollars' worth of International Delight for the same volume. Cheaper than the branded real one, cheaper per millilitre than the fake one, and you control the sugar — use half the syrup and you've made something no shelf in that aisle sells at any price: a lightly sweetened creamer that's actually cream. Vanilla-hazelnut, cinnamon, a dash of cocoa if you miss the chocolatey ones — the flavour wall is your spice rack now.

Last thing, and it's a hopeful one. Statistics Canada tracks the national average price of a litre of cream — six dollars and eighteen cents as of June — and its food data shows something quietly wonderful happening around that number: whipping cream consumption per Canadian is up around 21 percent since 2011, table cream is up too, and even whole milk is growing again after decades of decline. Without any campaign telling them to, Canadians have been drifting back toward the real thing for a decade. And because of how this country's dairy system works, every litre of that shift lands on a Canadian farm — the thousands of co-op farm families you met in the winners section are the entire supply chain. The whitener aisle is betting you won't read the jar. The numbers say more and more of you already are. So here's the checklist, and it fits on a coffee sleeve: the first two ingredients should be milk and cream — if the first two are a sugar and an oil, it's not cream, whatever the front implies. "Non-dairy" plus "Contains: Milk" on the same package tells you everything about how much the front panel respects you. Compare price per hundred millilitres, never per bottle — the real thing is not more expensive. And the M.F. percentage on a cream carton is federal law doing you a favour — use it. One more label note for the road: the phrase "modified milk ingredients" you've now learned to spot is itself being renamed — by 2030 those words become "milk-derived ingredients." The vocabulary changes. The game doesn't. Keep reading.
