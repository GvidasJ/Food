# SCRIPT — 5 Chicken Chains in Canada That Got Too Greedy and Paid the Price

TITLE: 5 Chicken Chains in Canada That Got Too Greedy and Paid the Price

TITLE NOTE: The working title was "7 Fried Chicken Chains". Research supported five chains with a dated, documented record, not seven, so the count is five. "Fried" was dropped because two of the five (St-Hubert and Swiss Chalet) are rotisserie chains; keeping "Fried" in the title would hand the comment section an easy correction. Same construction as the February video (52.6K views, 5.8x median): countdown, dated events, company quotes, "How to protect yourself", tagline. Order basis is stated on screen: ranked by Canadian locations, fewest to most, using each company's own filings, releases and store locators as of September 28, 2026.

ALTERNATES: "5 Fried Chicken Chains in Canada That Got Too Greedy and Paid the Price" (only if the producer accepts the rotisserie inaccuracy) / "5 Chicken Chains That Got Too Greedy and Paid the Price (Canada)" / "5 Canadian Chicken Chains That Got Too Greedy and Paid the Price"

Every single day, Canadians line up for chicken expecting a fair piece at a fair price. But at some of the best-known chicken chains in Canada, the record shows something else happening. They wanted more. Greedy, in this video, means one of three documented things: prices pushed up, growth chased past what the counter could carry, or a franchisor fighting its own franchisees. Then the bill came due. In October 2025, an Ontario court put fourteen Popeyes franchise companies under an interim receiver. In October 2024, St-Hubert froze the price of every main dish, and the same day its own president admitted the dining rooms had lost double-digit traffic. Five chains, not more, because only these five have a dated record in filings, court files and named reports. Two of the five roast rather than fry; the test is that chicken is the main product. Ranked from the fewest Canadian locations to the most, using each company's own filings, releases and store locators, or its parent's latest Canadian count, as of September 2026. Where something is an allegation, we say so. Because the truth is not always on the menu.

Number five, Dixie Lee. The company's own franchising page says the first Dixie Lee opened in Belleville, Ontario in 1964. Today there are two franchisors. Dixie Lee Fried Chicken, whose website names Maria Struik as president, covers Ontario and the West. Dixie Lee Maritimes Limited covers the Atlantic provinces and Quebec. For a brief period, Dixie Lee was a public company. In July 2007, a Nevada shell company called Wood Products filed with the United States Securities and Exchange Commission that it had acquired Dixie Lee Food Systems U.S.A. and renamed itself Dixie Lee International Industries. In April 2008, the same company told the SEC it had signed a letter of intent under which a British area franchisee would open 70 stores in the United Kingdom within twelve months of incorporation. In June 2008, it told the SEC that its Canadian arm was the franchisor of approximately 31 Dixie Lee franchises in Canada. Then the filings stopped. The SEC's own index shows the last document was filed in August 2008, and no audited financial statement for the Dixie Lee business was ever filed there. In April 2023, Radio-Canada reported a month-long shortage of the chain's signature brown sauce across New Brunswick and the Gaspésie. The owner of the Caraquet restaurant told Radio-Canada that the Ontario plant making the sauce for the franchise had run short of one of its raw materials. As of September 28, 2026, the company's homepage still says Dixie Lee continues to expand throughout all of North America and 60 Foreign Countries. On the same day, the Ontario franchisor's own store locator listed five restaurants. Three in Ontario, one of them marked opening soon, one in Alberta, and one in Ogdensburg, New York. From approximately 31 Canadian franchises in a 2008 securities filing, which did not say whether it counted the Maritimes stores, to four in Canada on the Ontario franchisor's own locator eighteen years later. To be fair, the Maritimes franchisor publishes no count, so nobody outside the company knows the full number. Even at a hundred, Dixie Lee would still sit below St-Hubert's 120. And a sauce shortage is a supply problem, not a pricing decision. But the homepage says sixty countries and the store locator shows four Canadian towns. Both are the company's own pages, captured on the same day. Next, a Quebec institution that put its prices in reverse. Number four, with more than 120 rotisseries by its own count.

Number four, St-Hubert. The company's 75th anniversary release from September 2026 says the first rotisserie opened in 1951, and that its more than 120 rotisseries in Quebec, Ontario and New Brunswick serve more than 22.4 million meals a year. In 2016 it was bought for about 537 million dollars, per the Globe and Mail, by Cara, the Vaughan, Ontario company that became Recipe Unlimited. Recipe itself was taken private in October 2022 by Fairfax Financial at 20.73 dollars a share, according to Fairfax's 2022 annual report. In August 2022, the Globe and Mail reported that Recipe had been raising some menu prices, as much of the industry was. Two years later, St-Hubert's president was describing a very different picture. On October 9, 2024, St-Hubert issued a release announcing a freeze on the price of every main dish, price cuts averaging about eleven percent on roughly one hundred menu items, and new lunch plates starting at twelve dollars. The same day, president Richard Scofield told La Presse that dining-room traffic had fallen, in his words as we translate them, by double digits. Quebecers who once visited four or five times a year now came two or three times, he said. And, in translation: we saw far fewer purchases of starters and desserts. People were depriving themselves. In the release, Scofield put it this way, in translation: inflation can hurt Quebecers, and many families now have to make difficult choices. Later in the same statement: we want to revisit our value equation. Then came the shrinking. In April 2025, he told La Presse new restaurants would be built for 175 seats instead of 300, saying, in translation, our restaurants, we are making them smaller. And the closures. The boulevard Laurier restaurant in Quebec City, reported closed by Radio-Canada in January 2026, where the franchisee said roadwork had cost it thirty percent of its customers and they never came back; a replacement is planned for early 2027. Salaberry-de-Valleyfield closed in March 2026 and reopened in April under head office, not a franchisee, according to Néomédia. In July 2026, Radio-Canada reported that headcount had gone from 10,000 to about 7,200 since the ownership change, while the company's own releases say more than 6,000. The same report said the Sainte-Catherine Street rotisserie in Montreal's Village, open since 1977, would close ahead of four years of municipal roadwork. La Presse counted 124 rotisseries in October 2024 and 121 in April 2025; the company's own releases have said more than 120 throughout. Flat on paper, smaller in the room. In fairness, Fairfax's annual reports say Recipe posted record system sales in 2023 and four percent same-restaurant sales growth in 2025. And in November 2025, BrandSpark's survey of more than 45,000 Canadian shoppers named St-Hubert the most trusted casual restaurant for family dining in Quebec. But a company does not cut a hundred prices and freeze the rest because business is good. It does it because, in its own president's words, people were depriving themselves. Its sister chain in English Canada shares the owner but has its own count, its own franchisees and its own court file, so it holds its own slot. Number three, with almost 200 locations by the company's own count.

Number three, Swiss Chalet. Founded in 1954, according to the company. We covered its retreat from British Columbia in our February video. Swiss Chalet is a registered trademark of Recipe Unlimited Corporation, which now issues its releases as Recipe Restaurant Group, the same Fairfax-owned company that owns St-Hubert and Harvey's. One correction to our February video: The Keg left Recipe in September 2025, according to Fairfax's 2025 annual report. Start with the footprint, in the company's own words. In June 2023, a Swiss Chalet release said more than 200 locations across Canada and announced five openings. In March 2025, the Windsor Star reported that the company's own website listed 180. On September 28, 2026, three of the company's own pages said over 190, over 195 and almost 200. The provinces listed on the franchising page are Ontario, Alberta, Manitoba and the four Atlantic provinces. No British Columbia, no Saskatchewan, no Quebec. Then the franchisee fight. In mid-December 2023, less than two weeks before Christmas, Recipe locked the franchisees out of the combined Harvey's and Swiss Chalet in Napanee, Ontario, putting 26 employees out of work, according to Global News. The franchisee alleged that he had deducted the cost of repairing the building's heating and cooling unit from his rent with Recipe's approval. Recipe's position, as reported by the Napanee Beaver, was that the franchisee was poorly managing the restaurant. Both sides are allegations. In April 2024, the Ontario Superior Court, in a decision reported as 2024 ONSC 2050, found the first judge's reasons inadequate and ordered a new hearing. According to Law Times, the court stressed Recipe's undertaking not to terminate the franchise pending the injunction motion, and cited the duty of fair dealing in Ontario's Arthur Wishart Act. The restaurant reopened in May 2024 pending trial, and 24 of the 26 staff came back, according to the Napanee Beaver. We found no public final outcome as of September 2026, so the case stands as pending. Meanwhile, more doors closed. In March 2025, the Windsor Star reported that the Walker Road restaurant in Windsor, open about 30 years, would leave by May 31, according to the landlord, and that Recipe did not respond to multiple requests for comment. In July 2026, the Cross Avenue location in Oakville, more than 20 years at the site, closed for redevelopment, according to INsauga. The company's website says it is growing all the time. In fairness, the Oakville restaurant closed as part of an area redevelopment; nothing on the record says that was Recipe's decision. And the same BrandSpark awards named Swiss Chalet the most trusted casual restaurant for family dining in Ontario and Atlantic Canada. But a franchisor locked out its own franchisee in the run-up to Christmas, and the dispute ended up before a judge citing the fair-dealing duty in Ontario franchise law. That is one franchisee, but it is the one on the public record, and the case is still open. Now, from a rotisserie to a chain that went from 230 restaurants to 380 in five years, and what that growth cost. Number two, with 380 restaurants by its parent's count in July 2025, the last Canada-only figure it gave.

Number two, Popeyes. The chicken sandwich that broke the internet arrived in Canada in September 2020, at over 230 restaurants by the company's release. Popeyes is owned by Restaurant Brands International, the Toronto-listed parent of Tim Hortons and Burger King, which bought it in March 2017 per its annual report. Canadian restaurants are franchised directly to individual operators. The growth was fast. In October 2022, the company announced its 300th Canadian restaurant. In July 2025, an RBI spokesperson told the Globe and Mail, we have 380 restaurants in Canada. Then the cracks. In January 2025, Popeyes sent default notices to Toronto-area franchise companies run by Irfan Memon. The Globe and Mail reported they cited repeated failures to pay their employees amounts owing when due for more than two months. In May 2025, a former chicken supplier, ADP Direct Poultry of Etobicoke, sued Popeyes, RBI and its Canadian supply cooperative in Ontario Superior Court for about 35 million dollars. The claim alleges breach of contract and that some franchisees bought chicken outside the approved supply chain. Those are allegations. The case is pending, two franchisee companies have filed defences, and RBI told the Globe it found no evidence of the systemic issues they are claiming. On September 26, 2025, employees at Memon's restaurants protested over what they alleged were unpaid wages and bounced cheques, according to the Globe. By September 29, three days later, RBI had terminated the franchise agreements for 13 Toronto-area Popeyes, and the restaurants, shut since the night of the protest, stayed closed, according to the Globe. RBI told the Globe: treating restaurant team members with respect, including paying them on time, is fundamental to our brand's values. On October 10, 2025, the Ontario Superior Court's Commercial List appointed BDO Canada as interim receiver over 14 corporations that each ran one Popeyes, on the application of Meridian Credit Union. That is the thirteen that had closed plus one that stayed open. The Globe and Insolvency Insider reported that the application put the credit union's exposure at about 10.83 million dollars and cited unpaid wages, source deductions and HST. The receivership order is a court order; the amounts are as reported. Then the numbers. RBI reports Popeyes comparable sales for one segment covering the United States and Canada, labelled U.S. in its tables, and publishes no Canadian figure. Every percentage that follows is that segment, not Canada. Comparable sales rose 0.4 percent in 2024, according to RBI's February 2025 release. They fell 2.4 percent in the third quarter of 2025, according to the October 2025 release, and 3.2 percent for the full year, according to the February 2026 release. They fell 6.5 percent in the first quarter of 2026 and 5.1 percent in the second, according to RBI's filings with the SEC. In November 2025, RBI replaced the president of Popeyes for the United States and Canada. On the February 2026 earnings call, chief executive Josh Kobza said franchise profitability had declined to roughly 235,000 dollars, as reported by Nation's Restaurant News. He added that Popeyes' biggest opportunity is improving restaurant level execution and re-engaging with our core guests. In fairness, no filing or report documents a net decline in Canadian Popeyes restaurants; the count went from over 230 to 300 to 380. On its August 2026 earnings call, RBI said, as reported by CNBC, that it expects positive comparable sales again in the second half of 2026. The receivership was at franchisee level, and the wage claims remain allegations. But when 13 restaurants close in a single week, 14 franchise companies land in receivership, and the parent tells investors that franchise profits across its U.S.-and-Canada segment are down, growth has stopped being the whole story.

Number one, KFC. The bucket. Toonie Tuesday. PFK in Quebec. Its own franchise recruitment listing calls it the largest quick service chicken restaurant brand in Canada. The franchisor is Kentucky Fried Chicken Canada Company, owned by Yum! Brands of Louisville, Kentucky, according to Yum's 2025 annual report; the restaurants are run by franchisees. And KFC in Canada has already paid one price, a decade ago. In March 2011, its biggest franchisee, Priszm Income Fund, which ran about 60 percent of Canadian KFCs, filed for creditor protection, according to the Office of the Superintendent of Bankruptcy and the Globe and Mail. Priszm's chairman John Bitove told the Globe, it's not just Priszm, the whole KFC system in Canada has been struggling. By May 2012, the Globe reported about 15 percent of Priszm's 428 outlets abandoned. In December 2019, the last PFK in Quebec City closed, according to Le Soleil, after five other closures in the area within a year; new franchisees began reopening the region in 2023, per the same paper. The chain rebuilt on value. In April 2023, KFC Canada launched Twosdays, two pieces for 2.99, and its release spoke of a time of high inflation and rising food and grocery costs. In March 2024 came a 4.95 Fill Up, Ontario only. In November 2025, a Black Friday bucket deal went out under a headline saying the company's year-round commitment to value continues. In July 2026, it added a deals platform called Good Cluckin' Deals on top of its existing Everyday Value Menu. A senior marketing manager, quoted by Strategy magazine from a company release, said value shouldn't mean compromising on the food you actually want. Four value pushes since 2023 is a company that knows its customers are stretched. The question is what the checkout screen shows once the deal is in the cart. Then, in November 2025, the lawsuit. A Quebec consumer filed an application in Quebec Superior Court to authorize a class action against dozens of restaurant chains and the payment processor Moneris, and Kentucky Fried Chicken Canada Company is the first name in the case title. La Presse reported the example the application gives. It alleges a PFK sandwich shown at 11.29 dollars on the ordering site became 17.04 dollars at checkout once a mandatory delivery fee and taxes were added. It says such fees violate Quebec's Consumer Protection Act, which bars charging more than the advertised price. This is an allegation. It has not been authorized, and KFC is one of many defendants. In a July 2026 court filing, KFC said its franchisees set delivery fees, not head office, and that its updated website and app now show the delivery fee before a restaurant is selected. Then the numbers. Yum reports KFC Canada's system sales growth every quarter: up 6 percent in 2024. Up 7 percent in 2025. Then, in the first quarter of 2026, down 1 percent, according to Yum's April 2026 filing with the SEC. Down 1 percent again in the second quarter, according to the July 2026 filing. In the nineteen quarterly filings from February 2022 to July 2026, that is the first time the Canada line fell two quarters in a row. Those are system-wide sales, which include new restaurants; the company's own boilerplate moved from more than 600 to more than 650 locations in late 2025. In fairness, the store count is growing, the class action is an allegation, and KFC is contesting it. But this is Canada's biggest chicken chain. It is number one by size, not by sin. It has watched its largest franchisee go into creditor protection once already. Four value pushes since 2023 and two straight quarters of falling sales say the customer is telling it something again.

So where does this leave the Canadian who still wants chicken tonight? Three names whose own filings and releases show growth rather than the pattern above. First, a disclosure. We have no commercial relationship with any chain named in this video, and none of them knew this video was being made.

First, Mary Brown's. Founded in St. John's in 1969, owned since 2007 by Newfoundland entrepreneur Gregory Roberts, and 100 percent Canadian-owned, in the words of its own press releases. The count has gone in one direction. 188 locations in June 2021, according to Retail Insider. 257 in April 2024, according to Franchise Times. Over 300 in June 2026, when the company said it opened its 300th restaurant in New Brunswick. The company claims 19 consecutive years of same-store sales growth. In the court, class action and Competition Bureau databases we could search, we found nothing involving it. The concession: it is private, so those figures are the company's own, and from January 4, 2022 its Big Mary Monday price went up to 4.99, a change the company's own Facebook post tied to rising food costs. In January we told you to avoid this chain, based on customer reviews. This video uses only the documented record, and on that record it is clean.

Second, A&W. The July 2026 results from A&W Food Services of Canada reported 1,105 restaurants, up from 1,082 a year earlier, and same-store sales up 2.1 percent, which chief executive Susan Senecal called the strongest quarterly growth in over two years. In January 2026, it launched a Value Deals menu with items under four dollars each, including the Chicken Buddy Burger and two chicken wraps, according to its release. The concession: the release gives no item prices or end date, and A&W is a burger chain with a chicken line. McDonald's has a chicken line and a one-year five-dollar promise from January 2026 too, but we ranked it in February, so it sits out. A&W's own results show growth, not retreat.

Third, the grocery store. Sobeys, Safeway, IGA, Foodland and FreshCo are all banners of Empire Company of Stellarton, Nova Scotia, run through its subsidiary Sobeys Inc. On September 27, 2026, Empire's Voilà online store listed a hot oven roasted chicken at 13.49 and a one-kilogram BBQ whole chicken at 12.99, with quarter-chicken combos at 5.49 for dark meat and 6.49 for white. Those are online prices for an Ontario service area; in-store prices vary by banner and store, so check yours. Empire's September 2026 quarterly release said its internal food inflation stayed below the consumer price index for food purchased from stores. A whole roasted chicken for about thirteen dollars, online price, is the competitor every chain on this list is really up against. Costco sells one too, but does not post the price on a company page, so we do not quote it.

How to protect yourself. First, vote with your wallet. If a chain raises prices without giving you more, stop going. Nothing changes a menu faster than falling traffic. St-Hubert cut about a hundred prices on the same day its president told La Presse that dining-room traffic had fallen by double digits. Second, check the price before you order. Every chain on this list takes orders online, and the price at your nearest restaurant is in that order screen. Screenshot it, and again next month. Watch every screen before you pay, because delivery fees can appear late in the process, and that is exactly what the proposed Quebec class action alleges. Third, read the filings. RBI disclosed falling comparable sales in the segment that includes Canada, and Yum disclosed KFC Canada's back-to-back quarterly declines, in filings anyone can read for free. Fourth, compare with the grocery store. Statistics Canada's consumer price index shows fast food and takeout prices rose 8.3 percent between August 2024 and August 2026, while food bought from stores rose 6.3 percent.

These five chains have something in common in the record. Their own filings, releases and store maps show customers making a choice. In September 2026, the industry's own trade association, Restaurants Canada, said its annual survey found eight in ten Canadians eating out less often because of the cost of living. When prices go up and the plate does not, people find somewhere else to go. Thank you for watching.
