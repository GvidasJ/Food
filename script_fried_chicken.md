# SCRIPT — 5 Chicken Chains in Canada That Got Too Greedy and Paid the Price

TITLE: 5 Chicken Chains in Canada That Got Too Greedy and Paid the Price

TITLE NOTE: The working title was "7 Fried Chicken Chains". Research supported five chains with a dated, documented record, not seven, so the count is five. "Fried" was dropped because two of the five (St-Hubert and Swiss Chalet) are rotisserie chains; keeping "Fried" in the title would hand the comment section an easy correction. Same construction as the February video (52.6K views, 5.8x median): countdown, dated events, company quotes, "How to protect yourself", tagline. Order basis is stated on screen: ranked by Canadian locations, fewest to most, using each company's own filings, releases and store locators as of September 28, 2026.

ALTERNATES: "5 Fried Chicken Chains in Canada That Got Too Greedy and Paid the Price" (only if the producer accepts the rotisserie inaccuracy) / "5 Chicken Chains That Got Too Greedy and Paid the Price (Canada)" / "5 Canadian Chicken Chains That Got Too Greedy and Paid the Price"

Every single day, millions of Canadians line up for chicken. You hand over your money and you expect a fair piece of chicken at a fair price. But some of the biggest chicken chains in Canada decided that was not enough. They wanted more. They raised prices, they chased growth, and they fought the people who run their own restaurants. And then the bill came due. In October 2025, an Ontario court placed fourteen Popeyes franchise companies into receivership. In October 2024, St-Hubert froze the price of every main dish after its own president admitted the dining rooms had lost double-digit traffic. Those are two of the five stories on this list. Five chains, ranked from the fewest Canadian locations to the most, using each company's own filings, press releases and store locators as of September 2026. Every event has a date and a source. Where something is an allegation, we call it an allegation. Because the truth is not always on the menu.

Number five, Dixie Lee. If you grew up in small-town Ontario, northern New Brunswick or the Gaspésie, you know the red and white box. The company's own franchising page says the first Dixie Lee opened in Belleville, Ontario in 1964. Today there are two separate franchisors. Dixie Lee Fried Chicken, whose website names Maria Struik as president, covers Ontario and the West. Dixie Lee Maritimes Limited of Campbellton, New Brunswick covers the Atlantic provinces and Quebec. Neither publishes financial statements. For a brief period, though, Dixie Lee was a public company. In July 2007, a Nevada shell company called Wood Products filed with the United States Securities and Exchange Commission that it had merged with Dixie Lee Food Systems and renamed itself Dixie Lee International Industries. In June 2008, the same company told the SEC that its Canadian arm operated approximately 31 Dixie Lee franchises in Canada, and that a British area franchisee would open 70 stores in the United Kingdom within twelve months. Then the filings stopped. The SEC's own index shows the last document of any kind was filed in August 2008, and no audited financial statement for the Dixie Lee business ever appeared. In April 2023, Radio-Canada reported a month-long shortage of the chain's signature brown sauce across New Brunswick and the Gaspésie. The owner of the Caraquet restaurant told Radio-Canada that the Ontario plant making the sauce for the franchise had run out of potato starch. In July 2024, the Ontario franchisor announced on its own blog that a firm called Timms Holding Limited had been commissioned to expand the brand into the Middle East, Far East Asia and the Indian subcontinent. As of September 28, 2026, the company's homepage still says Dixie Lee continues to expand throughout all of North America and 60 Foreign Countries. On the same day, the same company's own store locator listed five restaurants. Three in Ontario, one of them marked opening soon, one in Alberta, and one in Ogdensburg, New York. From approximately 31 Canadian franchises in a 2008 securities filing to four in Canada on its own locator eighteen years later. To be fair, the Maritimes franchisor publishes no count, so nobody outside the company knows the full number. And a sauce shortage is a supply problem, not a pricing decision. But a chain that tells the world it is expanding into sixty countries, while its own map shows four Canadian towns, has a gap between the story and the counter. Next, a Quebec institution that put its prices in reverse. Number four, with more than 120 rotisseries by its own count.

Number four, St-Hubert. The yellow rooster of Quebec. The company's 75th anniversary release from September 2026 says it was founded in 1951 by Hélène and René Léger, and that it serves 22.4 million meals a year across Quebec, Ontario and New Brunswick. It has not been a family company for a decade. In 2016 it was bought for about 537 million dollars, according to the Globe and Mail, by Cara, the Toronto company that became Recipe Unlimited. Recipe itself was taken private in October 2022 by Fairfax Financial at 20.73 dollars a share, according to Fairfax's 2022 annual report. Fairfax's 2025 annual report says Recipe bought out the founding Phelan family that year, leaving Fairfax with 100 percent. In August 2022, the Globe and Mail reported that Recipe had been raising some menu prices across its brands. By 2024, the customers had answered. On October 9, 2024, St-Hubert issued a release announcing a freeze on the price of every main dish, price cuts averaging about eleven percent on roughly one hundred menu items, and new lunch plates starting at twelve dollars. The same day, president Richard Scofield told La Presse that dining-room traffic had fallen, in his words, by double digits, and that Quebecers who once visited four or five times a year now came two or three times. He said, and we translate, we saw far fewer purchases of starters and desserts. People were depriving themselves. The company release put it this way: inflation can hurt Quebecers, and we want to revisit our value equation. Then came the shrinking. In April 2025, he told La Presse that new restaurants would be built for 175 seats instead of 300, saying, in translation, our restaurants, we are making them smaller. In July 2026, Radio-Canada reported that headcount had gone from 10,000 to about 7,200 since the ownership change and that some dining rooms were being replaced with takeout counters. And the closures. The boulevard Laurier restaurant in Quebec City in January 2026, where the franchisee told Radio-Canada that roadwork had cost it thirty percent of its customers and they never came back. Salaberry-de-Valleyfield closed in March 2026 and reopened in April under head office instead of a franchisee, according to Néomédia. And in July 2026, Radio-Canada reported that the Sainte-Catherine Street rotisserie in Montreal's Village, open since 1977, would close ahead of four years of municipal roadwork. The company's own count went from 124 rotisseries in October 2024, per La Presse, to 121 in April 2025, to more than 120 in its 2026 releases. Flat on paper, smaller in the room. In fairness, a price freeze is good news for the customer. Fairfax's annual reports say Recipe posted record system sales in 2023 and four percent same-restaurant sales growth in 2025. And in November 2025, BrandSpark's survey of more than 45,000 Canadian shoppers named St-Hubert the most trusted family casual restaurant in Quebec. But a company does not cut a hundred prices and freeze the rest because business is good. It does it because, in its own president's words, people were depriving themselves. Its sister chain in English Canada took a harder road. Number three, with almost 200 locations by the company's own count.

Number three, Swiss Chalet. Founded in 1954, according to the company. The quarter chicken dinner was a birthday tradition for generations. We covered its retreat from British Columbia in our February video on restaurants that got greedy. Here is what has happened since. Swiss Chalet is a registered trademark of Recipe Unlimited Corporation, the same Fairfax-owned company that owns St-Hubert and Harvey's. One correction to our February video. The Keg is no longer part of Recipe. Fairfax's 2025 annual report says it was separated on September 25, 2025. Start with the footprint, in the company's own words. In June 2023, a Swiss Chalet release said more than 200 locations across Canada and announced five openings. In March 2025, the Windsor Star reported that the company's own website listed 180. On September 28, 2026, the company's franchising page said over 190, its FAQ page said over 195, and its About page said almost 200, all on the same day. The provinces listed on that franchising page are Ontario, Alberta, Manitoba and the four Atlantic provinces. No British Columbia, no Saskatchewan, no Quebec. The last BC restaurant, in Burnaby, closed in August 2022, and its general manager told Burnaby Now the closure was for economic reasons. Then the franchisee fight. On December 13, 2023, twelve days before Christmas, Recipe locked the franchisees out of the combined Harvey's and Swiss Chalet in Napanee, Ontario, and 26 employees lost their jobs, according to Global News. The franchisee alleged that Recipe had agreed he could deduct heating repair costs from his rent. Recipe's position, as reported by the Napanee Beaver, was that the franchisee was poorly managing the restaurant. Both sides are allegations. In April 2024, the Ontario Superior Court, in a decision reported as 2024 ONSC 2050, found the first judge's reasons inadequate and ordered a new hearing. According to Law Times, the court stressed Recipe's undertaking not to terminate the franchise while the injunction motion was decided, and cited the duty of fair dealing in Ontario's Arthur Wishart Act. The restaurant reopened in May 2024 pending trial, and 24 of the 26 staff came back, according to the Napanee Beaver. We found no public final outcome as of September 2026, so the case stands as pending. Meanwhile the closures kept coming. The Walker Road restaurant in Windsor, open about 30 years, left by May 31, 2025, according to the Windsor Star, which said Recipe did not respond to multiple requests for comment. In July 2026, the Cross Avenue location in Oakville, more than 20 years at the site, closed for redevelopment, according to INsauga. And in September 2026, INsauga reported that the Leanne Boulevard restaurant in Mississauga is slated for demolition to make way for townhomes and a ten-storey tower. The company's website says it is growing all the time. The company's own count went from more than 200 to 180 to almost 200 in three years. In fairness, the Oakville and Mississauga closures are redevelopment decisions by landlords and councils, not by Recipe. And BrandSpark's 2026 survey named Swiss Chalet the most trusted family casual restaurant in Ontario, Atlantic Canada and the West. But a franchisor locked out its own franchisee twelve days before Christmas, and the dispute ended up before a judge citing the fair-dealing duty in Ontario franchise law. That is not a chain at peace with the people who run its restaurants. Now, from a rotisserie to the fastest expansion in Canadian fast food, and what it cost. Number two, with 380 restaurants by its parent's count.

Number two, Popeyes. The chicken sandwich that broke the internet arrived in Canada in September 2020, and the company release said it was in over 230 restaurants at 5.99 to 6.49 depending on the province. Popeyes is owned by Restaurant Brands International, the Toronto-listed parent of Tim Hortons and Burger King, which bought the brand in March 2017 according to its annual report. Canadian restaurants are franchised directly to individual operators, and chicken is bought through a supply cooperative called Restaurant Services Canada. The growth was fast. In October 2022, the company announced its 300th Canadian restaurant. In February 2024, RBI told investors it wanted more than 4,200 Popeyes across the United States and Canada by 2028. In July 2025, an RBI spokesperson told the Globe and Mail, we have 380 restaurants in Canada. Then the cracks. In January 2025, Popeyes sent default notices to a group of Toronto-area franchise companies run by Irfan Memon. According to the Globe and Mail's reporting of those notices, they cited repeated failures to pay their employees amounts owing when due for a period of more than two months. In May 2025, a former chicken supplier, ADP Direct Poultry of Etobicoke, sued Popeyes, RBI and the supply cooperative in Ontario Superior Court for about 35 million dollars. The claim alleges breach of contract and that some franchisees bought chicken outside the approved supply chain. Those are allegations. The case is pending, two franchisee companies have filed defences denying them, and RBI told the Globe it found no evidence of the systemic issues they are claiming. On September 26, 2025, employees at Memon's restaurants protested over what they alleged were unpaid wages and bounced cheques, according to the Globe. Three days later, RBI terminated the franchise agreements for 13 Toronto-area Popeyes and the restaurants closed. RBI's statement to the Globe: treating restaurant team members with respect, including paying them on time, is fundamental to our brand's values. On October 10, 2025, the Ontario Superior Court's Commercial List appointed BDO Canada as interim receiver over 14 corporations that each ran one Popeyes, on the application of Meridian Credit Union. The Globe and Insolvency Insider reported the credit union was owed about 10.83 million dollars, alongside unpaid wages, source deductions and HST. That is a court order, not an allegation. Then the numbers. RBI reports Popeyes as one segment for the United States and Canada and publishes no Canadian figure, so treat these as the segment. Comparable sales rose 0.4 percent in 2024, according to RBI's February 2025 release. They fell 2.4 percent in the third quarter of 2025 and 3.2 percent for the full year, according to RBI's February 2026 release. They fell 6.5 percent in the first quarter of 2026 and 5.1 percent in the second, according to RBI's filings with the SEC. On the February 2026 earnings call, chief executive Josh Kobza said franchisee profitability had declined to roughly 235,000 dollars, and that Popeyes' biggest opportunity is improving restaurant level execution and re-engaging with our core guests. In November 2025, RBI replaced the president of Popeyes for the United States and Canada. In fairness, no filing or report documents a net decline in Canadian Popeyes restaurants. The company's own count went from 230 to 300 to 380, and in August 2026 RBI said it expects Popeyes to return to positive comparable sales in the second half of 2026. The receivership was at the franchisee level, and the wage claims against that franchisee remain allegations. But when 13 restaurants close in a single week, 14 franchise companies land in receivership, and the parent tells investors its own franchisees are earning less, growth has stopped being the whole story.

Number one, KFC. The bucket. Toonie Tuesday. PFK in Quebec. Its releases call it the largest quick service chicken restaurant brand in Canada. The franchisor is Kentucky Fried Chicken Canada Company, a Nova Scotia unlimited company owned by Yum! Brands of Louisville, Kentucky, according to Yum's 2025 annual report. The restaurants are run by franchisees. And KFC in Canada has already paid one price. In March 2011, its biggest franchisee, Priszm Income Fund, which ran about 60 percent of Canadian KFCs, filed for creditor protection, according to the Office of the Superintendent of Bankruptcy and the Globe and Mail. Priszm's chairman John Bitove told the Globe, it's not just Priszm, the whole KFC system in Canada has been struggling. By May 2012, the Globe reported that about 15 percent of Priszm's 428 outlets had been abandoned. In December 2019, the last PFK in Quebec City closed, according to Le Soleil, after five other closures in the area within a year. The chain rebuilt on value. In April 2023, KFC Canada launched Twosdays, two pieces for 2.99, and its release spoke of a time of high inflation and rising food and grocery costs. In March 2024 came a 4.95 Fill Up, Ontario only. In November 2025, the company announced what it called its year-round commitment to value. In July 2026, it added an Everyday Value Menu and a platform called Good Cluckin' Deals, with a marketing manager quoted in Strategy magazine saying value shouldn't mean compromising on the food you actually want. Four value launches since 2023 is a company that knows exactly what its customers are feeling. Then, in November 2025, the lawsuit. A Quebec consumer filed an application in Quebec Superior Court to authorize a class action against dozens of restaurant chains and the payment processor Moneris, and Kentucky Fried Chicken Canada Company is the first name in the case title. La Presse reported the example at the heart of it. A PFK sandwich shown at 11.29 dollars on the ordering site became 17.04 dollars at checkout after a five-dollar delivery fee and taxes. The application alleges that mandatory fees added at the end of checkout violate Quebec's Consumer Protection Act, which bars charging more than the advertised price. This is an allegation. It has not been authorized, and KFC is one of many defendants. In a July 2026 court filing, KFC said its franchisees set delivery fees, not head office, and that its updated website and app now show the delivery fee before a restaurant is selected. Then the numbers. Yum reports KFC Canada's system sales growth every quarter. Up 6 percent in 2024. Up 7 percent in 2025. Then, in the first quarter of 2026, down 1 percent, according to Yum's April 2026 filing with the SEC. Down 1 percent again in the second quarter, according to the July 2026 filing. In nineteen quarterly filings going back to early 2022, that is the first time the Canada line fell two quarters in a row. Those are system-wide sales, which include new restaurants, and the company's own boilerplate went from more than 600 to more than 650 locations over the same period. In fairness, the store count is growing, and Yum's chief financial officer listed Canada among the markets with ten or more openings in late 2025. The company and its franchisees say they are investing 30 million dollars in a beverage brand called Kwench. The class action is an allegation, and KFC is contesting it. But this is the biggest chicken chain in Canada. It has the most restaurants, the most customers, and the most to answer for. It has already watched its largest franchisee collapse once. Four value launches since 2023 and two straight quarters of falling sales say the customer is telling it something again.

So where does this leave the Canadian who still wants chicken tonight? There are three names whose public record does not show the pattern above. Before we name them, a disclosure. We have no commercial relationship with any chain named in this video, and none of them knew this video was being made.

First, Mary Brown's. Founded in St. John's in 1969, owned since 2007 by Newfoundland entrepreneur Gregory Roberts, and 100 percent Canadian-owned according to every company release. The count has gone in one direction. 188 locations in June 2021, according to Retail Insider. 257 in April 2024, according to Franchise Times. Over 300 in June 2026, when the company said it opened its 300th restaurant in New Brunswick. The company claims 19 consecutive years of same-store sales growth. We found no court decision, class action or Competition Bureau matter involving it. The concession: it is private, so those figures are the company's own, and in January 2022 it raised its Big Mary Monday price to 4.99, citing rising food costs. We have been critical of this chain before on other grounds. On the record this video is about, it is clean.

Second, A&W. The July 2026 results from A&W Food Services of Canada reported 1,105 restaurants, up from 1,082 a year earlier, and same-store sales up 2.1 percent, which chief executive Susan Senecal called the strongest quarterly growth in over two years. In January 2026, it launched a Value Deals menu with items under four dollars each, including the Chicken Buddy Burger and two chicken wraps, according to its release. The concession: the release gives no individual prices and no end date, and A&W is a burger chain with a chicken line, not a chicken chain. But its record shows growth, not retreat, and no franchisee in receivership.

Third, the grocery store. Sobeys, Safeway, IGA, Foodland and FreshCo are all owned by Empire Company of Stellarton, Nova Scotia. On September 27, 2026, Empire's Voilà online store listed a hot oven roasted chicken at 13.49 and a one-kilogram BBQ whole chicken at 12.99, with quarter-chicken combos at 5.49 for dark meat and 6.49 for white. Those are online prices for an Ontario service area, and in-store hot counter prices vary by banner and store, so check yours. Empire's September 2026 quarterly release said its internal food inflation continued to be below the consumer price index for food purchased from stores. A whole hot chicken for under thirteen dollars, from a company whose filings show no franchisee in receivership, is the competitor every chain on this list is really up against.

How to protect yourself. Here is what you can do. First, vote with your wallet. If a chain raises prices without giving you more, stop going. These companies respond to one thing and one thing only, revenue. St-Hubert cut a hundred prices in the same year its president admitted traffic had fallen by double digits. The company said so itself. Second, check the price before you order. Every chain on this list sells through an app, and the price for your postal code is in it. Screenshot it, then screenshot it again next month. Watch the last screen before you pay, because that is where delivery and service fees appear, and that is exactly what the Quebec class action is about. Third, read the filings. Restaurant Brands International, Yum! Brands and Fairfax Financial publish annual reports and quarterly results, and they are free. They told investors that comparable sales were falling and franchisee profits were down months before anyone saw a closed door. Fourth, compare with the grocery store. Statistics Canada's consumer price index shows fast food and takeout prices rose 8.3 percent between August 2024 and August 2026, while food bought from stores rose 6.3 percent. Every meal you make at home puts the difference back in your pocket.

These five chains had something in common. They all forgot a simple truth. The customer always has a choice, and in September 2026 Restaurants Canada reported that eight in ten Canadians say they are eating out less often because of the cost of living. When you push people too far, they find somewhere else to go. Thank you for watching.
