TITLE: We Tested Frozen Fries From President's Choice, No Name, Great Value and 3 More Canadian Brands (These Are Safe)

A french fry is a potato and some oil. That is the entire recipe, and it has been the entire recipe since long before any of us were born. It is what your mother made on the stove. It is what the chip truck parked by the arena makes. And here is something almost no Canadian knows: our own federal government agrees with you, in writing. Canada has a legal recipe for a frozen french fry. The Canadian Food Inspection Agency's compositional standards define "French Fried Potatoes" as mature, sound potatoes that have been cleaned, sorted, trimmed, cut into strips, blanched, and cooked in vegetable oil — and then the standard lists the only things a manufacturer is permitted to add. There are five. Sodium acid pyrophosphate. Dextrose. Ascorbic acid. Food colours. And an enzyme called asparaginase. That is the whole list. No flour. No batter. No starch coating. No gums. Nothing else.

So tonight we did something simple. We pulled the actual Canadian ingredient panels for the six brands sitting in Canadian freezers right now — McCain, President's Choice, No Name, Compliments, Great Value, and Cavendish Farms — and we held every one of them against that legal recipe. And I am going to give you the answer in the first minute instead of making you wait for it, because this video has good news in it, and good news should not be held hostage. The plain bag — the basic straight cut fry, from almost every brand in this country — passes. It really is potatoes and oil and a handful of things the standard specifically allows. If that is what you have been buying, you have been buying the real thing this whole time.

The engineering is not in the plain bag. It is in the bag beside it. It is in the word "crispy," in the word "seasoned," in the smiling shapes aimed at your grandchildren, and in one company's decision not to publish its ingredients where you can read them before you buy. We are going through all six brands, starting with the biggest name in the country and working down, and by the end you will be able to walk any freezer aisle in Canada and tell in about four seconds which bag is a potato and which bag is a potato wearing a costume.

Let us start with the one that is not just the biggest name in Canada, but the biggest name on Earth. McCain.

Here is the story most Canadians have never been told properly. In 1957, four brothers — Harrison, Wallace, Robert, and Andrew McCain — started a frozen food company in Florenceville, New Brunswick, a small town in the Saint John River valley. Today McCain Foods is the largest maker of frozen french fries in the world, selling in more than one hundred and sixty countries, and it is still a privately held family company. The company itself says that one in every four fries eaten on this planet is a McCain fry — that is McCain's own figure rather than an audited one, so take it as the company's claim, but nobody disputes that they are number one. Florenceville-Bristol calls itself the French Fry Capital of the World, and it has the trademark to prove it. There is a genuine Canadian pride story here, and this channel is not going to pretend otherwise: a family from rural New Brunswick built the biggest potato company on the planet and never sold it. Think about the scale of that for a moment. A village on the Saint John River, a few hundred people, several hours from any major city — and out of it came the company that supplies fries to fast food chains, pubs, and freezers on six continents. Whatever else we say tonight, that is a Canadian story worth knowing, and most Canadians have no idea the brand in their freezer is one of ours.

Now the bag. McCain Superfries straight cut, from McCain's own Canadian website: potatoes, canola oil, sea salt, sodium acid pyrophosphate to retain natural colour, caramel, and annatto. Six things, and every single one of them is expressly permitted under that federal standard. Their regular straight cut line reads nearly identically, with the phosphate written as "sodium phosphate" instead — and there is a small labelling reason for that difference that we will come back to, because it explains something you have probably noticed and assumed was two different chemicals. The verdict on the plain McCain bag is simple: it is a real french fry, made the way the law describes, by a Canadian company. Buy it without a second thought.

Then walk your eyes twelve inches to the right, to Superfries Extra Crispy. Same brand, same freezer, same section. Potatoes, canola oil, corn starch, rice flour, sea salt, rice starch, potato starch, baking powder, sodium acid pyrophosphate, and paprika. Read that again and count what got added: corn starch, rice flour, rice starch, potato starch, and baking powder. That is not a better potato. That is a batter. That crunch you are paying extra for is a coating, and the reason it matters legally is the detail almost nobody catches — flour and starch coatings are not on the list of things the standard permits in French Fried Potatoes. Which is exactly why that bag is not sold to you as French Fried Potatoes. It is sold as "Superfries Extra Crispy." The name changed because the recipe left the standard behind. Once you know that, the freezer aisle reads like a completely different document.

And there is one more McCain product that belongs in this conversation, because it is in a lot of Canadian freezers and it is aimed squarely at the smallest people in your house. McCain Smiles. The ingredient list is potatoes, dehydrated potatoes, vegetable oil, potato starch, sea salt, and the phosphate. Look at the second and fourth entries. Dehydrated potatoes and potato starch. A cut fry is a slice of an actual potato. A Smile is not cut from anything — it is potato that has been dried into flakes, rebuilt into a paste, bound with starch, and pressed into a shape. That is a reformed potato product, and it is legal, and it is disclosed right there on the bag. It is simply not the same food as the fry beside it, and the happy face on the front is not going to be the one to tell you. And notice who that product is designed for. Not for you — for the smallest, least sceptical eaters at your table, the ones who choose food by whether it is smiling. There is nothing wrong with feeding your grandchildren a treat. Just know that the most processed potato in the entire freezer is the one shaped like a face, and that a plain fry from the same company, three feet away, is closer to an actual vegetable and usually costs less per kilogram.

Second on our list is the house brand from the biggest grocer in the country. President's Choice belongs to Loblaw, which controls roughly twenty-nine percent of Canadian grocery, and their frozen potato line is genuinely large — straight cut, organic, seasoned wedges, spicy fried potatoes, sweet potato fries.

And here I have to tell you something straight, because this channel does not guess and it does not fill gaps with confidence. I could not verify a published Canadian ingredient panel for President's Choice plain fries from any source I trust enough to read to you. Every Loblaw website — the main one, Superstore, No Frills — blocks automated access to their ingredient information. That is not an accusation of hiding anything; it is a technical choice about their websites. But notice the practical result: McCain publishes its full Canadian panels openly on its own site. Cavendish Farms publishes its full panels. Sobeys publishes its full panels. If I want to know what is in a President's Choice fry before I drive to the store, I cannot easily find out. So my honest guidance for this brand is the least satisfying advice in the video and also the most reliable: pick up the bag in the store and read the back before it goes in your cart. And know what you are looking for — if the words after "potatoes" and "oil" include flour, starch, or a seasoning blend, you have got yourself a coated product, whatever the front of the bag says.

Third is the yellow bag, and this one has a pleasant surprise in it. No Name is Loblaw's discount label, the plainest packaging in Canadian grocery, and the ingredient information available for their straight cut fried potatoes reads essentially as potatoes, soybean and canola oil, salt, caramel colour, paprika extract, and sodium phosphate. I will flag my own sourcing here — that panel comes from a photograph of a Canadian package in a public food database rather than from Loblaw directly, so treat it as a strong indication rather than gospel, and confirm on the bag. But the shape of it matches every other Canadian house brand exactly, and if it holds, then the cheapest fries in the biggest grocery chain in Canada are a legitimate, standard-compliant french fry. Which is the pattern this channel keeps running into, video after video: the bag with nothing to prove is very often the bag with nothing to hide. The plain yellow package is not cutting corners. It simply never dressed up in the first place.

That is the three biggest names covered. Now, the second-largest grocer in the country.

Compliments is the Sobeys house brand, on shelves at Sobeys, Safeway, Foodland, FreshCo, and IGA — roughly a fifth of Canadian grocery. And I want you to hear this ingredient list in full, because after everything we have just been through, it is almost funny. Compliments straight cut fried potatoes: potatoes, canola and soybean oil, colour, sodium acid pyrophosphate. Four ingredients. That is the shortest list of any bag in this entire comparison, published openly on Sobeys' own website, and it is a textbook match for the federal standard. The one small mark against it is not about the food at all — that listing carries no country of origin statement, so I cannot tell you where those potatoes were grown, and I am not going to imply Canadian just because the brand is. But as a french fry? Four ingredients. The store brand quietly beat several national brands on simplicity.

Fifth is Great Value, the Walmart Canada house label, and it lands in the same position as President's Choice, for the same reason. Walmart Canada's website blocks automated access to its product pages, there is no Canadian ingredient panel for these fries in the public food databases, and the Great Value ingredient lists you will find floating around online are for the American product, which is a different formulation under different rules. So I will not read you a list I cannot stand behind. What I can tell you is the pattern worth noticing: of the six brands we examined, the two whose ingredients are hardest for an ordinary shopper to check before buying are the house brands of the two largest retailers in the country. Meanwhile the manufacturers — the actual potato companies — publish everything. Make of that what you will, and read the bag in the aisle.

And that brings us to the last brand, which is the one I suspect a lot of you have in your freezer without thinking twice about where it comes from. Cavendish Farms.

Cavendish is part of J.D. Irving, the New Brunswick family company, with its head office in Dieppe, New Brunswick, and its original potato plant on Prince Edward Island — the Irvings bought that plant in 1980 and named the company after the P.E.I. community. It is Canadian-owned in the fullest sense of the phrase: a private Canadian family company processing Canadian potatoes, with plants in New Annan on Prince Edward Island, a four hundred and thirty million dollar facility in Lethbridge, Alberta that opened in 2019, a coated-products plant in Wheatley, Ontario, and a twelve and a half million dollar research centre back home on the Island. They also run a plant in North Dakota, so this is a North American company rather than a Canada-only one, and I would rather tell you that than let you find it out later.

The straight cut bag: potatoes, vegetable oil, salt, caramel colour, extractives of paprika, and sodium acid pyrophosphate. Six ingredients, all within the standard, a clean and legitimate french fry. But the reason Cavendish gets the last and best slot tonight is not the ingredient list. It is one line printed underneath it that no other bag in this comparison carries: "Made in Canada from domestic and imported ingredients."

Stop and appreciate what that sentence actually does, because we have talked about it on this channel before and this is the clearest example we have ever found. Under Canadian rules, "Product of Canada" means essentially everything — ingredients, processing, labour — is Canadian, at the ninety-eight percent level. "Made in Canada" means the last substantial transformation happened here, and the law requires it to be followed by a qualifier telling you the ingredients are not all domestic. Cavendish could have printed nothing at all. Origin statements on this kind of product are not mandatory. They chose to tell you the truth in the most specific language available to them: the fries were made in Canada, the potatoes are largely Canadian, and some of what is in the bag — the oils, most likely — came from somewhere else. That is a company being precise with you when silence was an option, and after the last two entries, it is worth noticing.

For contrast, and for fairness, Cavendish sells a coated line too. FlavourCrisp reads: potatoes, vegetable oil, wheat flour, modified corn starch, rice flour, yellow corn meal, salt, baking powder, malted barley flour, guar gum, dextrose, and the phosphate. And underneath that, in bold: contains wheat, contains barley. That is the same swap McCain makes with Extra Crispy — the crunch is a coating, and in this case the coating carries allergens that a plain fry never would. If someone at your table avoids gluten, the difference between those two Cavendish bags is not a preference. It is the whole meal.

Before the chemicals, there is one thing hiding inside that legal definition that deserves its own minute, because millions of Canadians have believed the opposite for thirty years. Go back and read how the standard describes a frozen french fry: potatoes, cleaned, sorted, trimmed, cut into strips, blanched — and processed in vegetable oil. Processed in vegetable oil. Not "may be." That is part of the definition. Every frozen fry in that aisle, including the ones with baking instructions and a photograph of an oven on the front, was already cooked in oil at the factory before it was ever frozen. It is called par-frying. The fry is dropped in hot oil, cooked partway, and frozen mid-cook so that your oven can finish the job.

Now, I want to be fair about what that does and does not mean, because this channel does not trade in outrage. A fry you bake at home genuinely does end up with less fat than one submerged in a deep fryer at a restaurant. That part is true and it stays true. What is not true is the idea a lot of us have carried since the first oven fries appeared in Canadian freezers: that "oven" means the potato has never met oil. It met oil. It met oil in a factory, in oil you never saw and cannot inspect, and the word "oven" was only ever an instruction for the second cook — the one you do. It is not a lie. It is a halo. And while you are looking at that bag, check the serving size the nutrition numbers are built on, because it is often somewhere around eighty-five to one hundred grams — considerably less than the pile most of us actually put on a plate. The friendly number on the front is real. It is just describing a smaller supper than the one you are about to eat.

So let us talk about the chemicals, because I promised no scare stories and I intend to keep that promise by explaining exactly what these things are doing.

Sodium acid pyrophosphate is the one on nearly every bag, and it is there for the least dramatic reason imaginable. Potatoes contain iron. When cooked potato iron meets certain natural compounds in the potato, the fry turns grey. Not spoiled, not unsafe — grey. That additive grabs the iron first so your fries come out gold instead of gunmetal. The Canadian labels literally explain themselves in brackets: "to retain natural colour." And here is the labelling wrinkle I promised you earlier, which explains why McCain's two straight cut bags read differently. Canadian rules allow a whole family of phosphates to be declared under the class name "sodium phosphate" or "sodium phosphates." So one bag naming "sodium acid pyrophosphate" and another naming "sodium phosphate" may well contain the same thing. It is not two different formulas. It is two different ways of writing the same permitted ingredient, both entirely legal.

Dextrose is a sugar, and yes, it is genuinely a little cheeky to find sugar in a savoury bag of potatoes. It is not there for sweetness — you cannot taste it. It is there for colour. Sugar browns under heat, so a light dusting makes a pale, part-cooked fry come out of your oven the exact golden shade shown in the photograph on the front of the bag. You are not tasting it. You are seeing it. And notice where it shows up in tonight's lists: not in the plain bags, but in the coated one.

Caramel, annatto, and paprika extract are colours, all permitted, all declared. Annatto comes from the seeds of the achiote tree, paprika from peppers, caramel from cooked sugar. Their job is consistency — so the bag you buy in February looks like the one you bought in August, regardless of what the potato crop did that year.

None of this is a scandal. All of it is legal, disclosed, and boring in the best possible way. Which is precisely why the coated fries stand out: they are not adding a colour or a stabilizer, they are adding an entire second food on top of the potato, and charging you more for it.

Now, since we are talking about Canadian potatoes, you should know how big this actually is. Canada grows roughly five point eight million tonnes of potatoes a year, and about two-thirds of that crop goes to processing rather than to the produce aisle. The biggest growing provinces are Alberta and Manitoba, followed by Prince Edward Island and New Brunswick, and the fry plants sit right where the potatoes grow. This country exports something in the order of two point seven billion dollars in french fries annually, the overwhelming majority to the United States. We are, in the most literal sense, a french fry superpower — a country that turned a cold-climate root vegetable into one of its great manufacturing exports, employing thousands of people in towns most Canadians could not find on a map. I will not tell you every fry in your freezer is Canadian — Canada still imports well over a hundred million dollars of frozen fries a year, and Cavendish's own label is honest about imported ingredients. But when you buy a bag of Canadian-made fries, the potato in it very likely came out of Canadian soil, and the money moves through Canadian towns.

And that trade is not something to take for granted. In November of 2021, after potato wart was detected in two Prince Edward Island fields, exports of P.E.I. potatoes to the United States were suspended — a decision that shook an entire province built on that crop. Fresh potato exports resumed the following spring, but seed potato exports have remained restricted, and as recently as this past May, American potato groups were publicly pushing Washington to reinstate the ban. This story is still moving as we speak, so check the current status before you repeat it at the coffee shop. Meanwhile the investment has kept coming: McCain announced a six hundred million dollar expansion at Coaldale, Alberta in 2023, which the company described as the largest single investment in its history.

So — which bags are safe? Here is the list, and it costs you nothing.

The plain straight cut bag from McCain is a real french fry: six ingredients, all permitted, from the New Brunswick family company that became the biggest in the world. The plain straight cut bag from Cavendish Farms is a real french fry, Canadian-owned, and it is the only one on the shelf that bothers to tell you where it was made. The Compliments straight cut bag has the shortest ingredient list in this entire comparison at four items, and it is usually among the cheapest. The No Name yellow bag appears to be exactly the same honest formula in the plainest packaging in the country, and it is generally the cheapest of all — confirm the panel on the bag, then buy it happily. And for President's Choice and Great Value, the fries themselves may well be perfectly fine; you simply have to read the package in the aisle, because the companies have not made it easy to check from home.

Then there is the option that beats every one of them on price, which I would be doing you a disservice not to mention. A ten pound bag of Canadian potatoes costs a few dollars. Cut them thick, toss them in a spoonful of oil, roast them until they are gold, and you have made the exact food this entire industry is imitating, for a fraction of the money, with an ingredient list of exactly two items. Twenty minutes of work. Prince Edward Island, New Brunswick, Manitoba, and Alberta all grew that potato for you.

Here is the rule to take to the freezer aisle, and it takes four seconds. Turn the bag over and read the words after "potatoes" and "oil." If what follows is salt, a colour, and a phosphate, you are holding a genuine french fry made to a federal standard, and the price on the front is the only decision left. If what follows includes flour, starch, baking powder, or a seasoning blend, you are holding a coated product — the crunch is batter, not potato, and the bag is not calling itself French Fried Potatoes for a reason. And if the bag is shaped like a smile or a letter or a waffle, it is reformed potato, built from flakes and starch, and it belongs in a different category than the one your memory has it filed under.

That is the whole aisle. The good news is that the honest bag is usually also the cheap bag, the Canadian bag, and the one your family has been buying all along. This is one of the rare corners of the grocery store where the law did its job, the plain products are genuinely plain, and the only thing standing between you and the right choice is four seconds and a willingness to turn the bag over.
