# SCRIPT — FROZEN FOOD EPISODE

If you opened your freezer tonight and read the fine print on every box in there, you would find at least three surprises. Canada's most famous frozen pizza — the one with the slogan every person watching can finish from memory — quietly vanished from this country three years ago, and most people never noticed. The waffle brand you have bought since 1971 has changed owners twice in the last three years, and as of nine months ago it belongs to the same American company that makes M&Ms. And the bag of frozen corn with the jolly green fellow on it is at the centre of a legal fight in Ottawa that started three weeks ago — a fight over whether a Quebec company should be allowed to buy the very brand it has been quietly packing for thirty years.

And one more thing, because this channel owes you the numbers nobody else reads out: the single fastest-rising price in the entire freezer aisle, according to Statistics Canada, is the most Canadian thing in it. We will get there. One of these stories is going to sting.

But let us start at the beginning, because the freezer aisle has the best origin story in the grocery store, and it is ours. Between 1912 and 1917, a young American named Clarence Birdseye was living in Labrador, working the fur trade. He watched Inuit fishermen pull trout through a hole in the ice into air so cold the fish froze almost instantly — and when that fish was thawed and cooked weeks later, it tasted fresh. Slow freezing makes mush. Instant freezing, the kind the Inuit had understood for generations, keeps food whole. Birdseye went home, spent a decade building machines to copy what he saw on that ice, and the modern frozen food industry was born. Every bag in your freezer traces back to a fishing hole in Labrador. Frozen food is not some modern shortcut. It started as one of the most honest ideas in the history of eating — and it started here.

So today we are going through nine frozen food brands in every Canadian grocery store, in order, starting with the most popular, and asking our two questions of every single package. Who owns this now? And where is it actually made? Fair warning before we start: the ownership side of this aisle has been playing musical chairs at a speed nobody could follow — you will hear the words "sold," "renamed," and "discontinued" more times today than in any episode we have made. We are not doctors and we are not activists. We read every label. That's all we do. If that is a service worth having, the like button is free, takes one second, and genuinely tells YouTube to show this to more Canadians who grew up on these freezers. Let's open the door.

Number one: McCain.

Where else could we start. By survey after survey, McCain is the most trusted name in the Canadian freezer — BrandSpark's poll of more than 45,000 Canadian shoppers has handed McCain the frozen potato crown year after year, including this year. And the company itself makes a claim that stops you cold, so to speak: McCain says one in every four french fries eaten anywhere on Earth is a McCain fry. One in four. On the planet. And it all comes from Florenceville, New Brunswick — a town of a few thousand people that officially calls itself the French Fry Capital of the World, where the head office still sits, where there is literally a museum called Potato World off exit 153 on the Trans-Canada.

The story: in the mid-1950s, brothers Wallace and Harrison McCain, with their brothers Robert and Andrew, opened a french fry plant in their hometown. First year sales: $152,000. Today, by the company's own telling, it is the largest maker of frozen fries in the world, sixteen billion dollars a year in revenue, and still owned by the family. Still Canadian. And still betting on Canada — in 2023 McCain announced 600 million dollars to double its Coaldale, Alberta plant, the largest single investment in the company's history.

But no Canadian dynasty is complete without a family war, and this one is legendary. Around 1990, the brothers who built the empire fell out over one question: who takes over. Wallace wanted his son Michael. Harrison said no. The fight went to arbitration in a Fredericton courtroom, with legal fees reported around twenty million dollars, and in 1994 Wallace lost — forced out of the company he co-founded. Years later he said it plainly: "The biggest thing that happened to me in the past 25 years — and in my life — was being unceremoniously dumped from McCain Foods." Now here is the part they should teach in business school. The next year, 1995, Wallace and the Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan bought Maple Leaf Foods. The man who lost the french fry empire took over the hot dog empire — and his son Michael, the young man the whole war was fought over, ran Maple Leaf for almost twenty-five years. Two of the biggest names in your grocery store, run by two halves of one family that stopped speaking. They reconciled, quietly, near the end — Wallace visited Harrison in hospital in 2004, the year Harrison died.

Now pick up the bag. Superfries Straight Cut: potatoes, canola oil, sea salt, sodium acid pyrophosphate to keep the colour, caramel, annatto. Six ingredients, and the label reads 105 milligrams of sodium, five percent of your daily value, per eighteen fries. Hold that number — it matters later. The bag sold at Walmart carries the line "Made in Canada from domestic and imported ingredients," and McCain says its potato products are made from local potatoes at its plants in New Brunswick, Manitoba and Alberta. About $3.32 at Walmart at the time of research. And yes — Deep'n Delicious cakes and Pizza Pockets are McCain too. The cake's first listed ingredient is sugars, and the box says, in capital letters, DO NOT HEAT OR BAKE — the only dessert in the store you are legally instructed to simply thaw. Two Pizza Pockets carry 900 milligrams of sodium, 39 percent of a day — read that beside the 105 in the fries and you understand this entire aisle: the closer frozen food stays to the potato, the shorter the story on the back of the bag.

Number two: Eggo.

Eggo waffles arrived in Canada in 1971, says Kellogg's own anniversary press release, and the slogan arrived the year after — and the company once claimed the majority of Canadians can still finish the phrase "L'eggo my..." without help. Fifty-five years of Canadian breakfasts. So, Canadian? Not for one day of those fifty-five years. And the last three years have been a corporate relay race that nobody at the breakfast table was told about. In 2023, Kellogg split itself in two — the cereals went one way, and Eggo went to a new company called Kellanova. In December 2025, nine months ago, Mars — the chocolate company — completed its purchase of Kellanova. So the waffle in your toaster now answers to the maker of M&Ms. And in a twist for trivia night: the other half of Kellogg, the cereal half with the plant in Belleville, Ontario, was bought by Ferrero of Italy last September. The two halves of the old Kellogg empire now belong to a chocolate company and a hazelnut company, and neither of them is Canadian. Where is the waffle itself made? We searched, and we could not find a single Canadian Eggo plant — the factories we found are in California, Georgia and Tennessee. The box in our freezer will get the final word: we will read its fine-print origin line on camera. Around $2.99 at the time of research, and an ingredient list we will read in full — it runs about fifteen deep, from enriched wheat flour through vegetable and palm oil shortening to soy lecithin.

Number three: High Liner.

The fish stick company. Founded December 1899 in Lunenburg, Nova Scotia, by five Smith brothers — three of them sea captains — as a salt cod business. The High Liner brand itself dates to 1926, it became National Sea Products in 1945, and generations of Canadians were raised by the man in the yellow slicker: Captain High Liner. When the company brought him back to television in 2015 after fifteen years away, its own marketing executive said the research showed, quote, "coast to coast, people still remember, respect and have reverence for the captain." This is a genuinely Canadian company — still headquartered in Lunenburg, still trading on the Toronto Stock Exchange, still the most trusted frozen fish brand in the BrandSpark survey, this year included. Honesty requires the fine print, though: High Liner today runs three plants — one in Lunenburg and two in the United States — and the fish itself is sourced from fisheries around the world. The company that survived the cod collapse of the early 1990s — when quotas fell off a cliff and the East Coast fishery shattered — survived precisely by becoming a marketer of global fish rather than a harvester of ours. The label tells the story: fish sticks, first ingredient "minced fish fillets (pollock or haddock)," 410 milligrams of sodium, 18 percent, per serving, about eleven dollars at the time of research.

And one law you can use tonight, because frozen fish comes wrapped in ice armour. That shiny glaze on frozen fillets is sprayed-on water, and here is what the rules actually say: under Canada's net quantity regulations, glazed fish must declare its weight as the edible contents — the CFIA's own words are that this, quote, "does not include the free liquid or glaze content." The ice does not count. If the bag says 400 grams, the fish must be 400 grams. You may have heard louder claims about glaze in other videos; the regulation is printed, and it is on your side.

Number four: the frozen pizza story — Delissio, and what really happened to it.

Nineteen ninety-nine. McCain — yes, the fry family — brings rising-crust frozen pizza to Canada. Kraft fires back within months using a name it had on the books here since the eighties: Delissio. Same product as America's DiGiorno, different name, and a slogan built to live forever: "It's not delivery. It's Delissio." For twenty years that jingle paid rent in every Canadian head. Kraft sold the brand to Nestlé of Switzerland in 2010. And then, February 2023, Nestlé Canada announced it was winding down its entire frozen meals and pizza business in this country — Delissio, Stouffer's, Lean Cuisine, all of it — noting it did not manufacture any of those products in Canada. CBC wrote the obituary in six words: "It's not delivery, it's discontinued." Check your freezer. It is already gone.

So who won the pizza war? Here is the twist nobody noticed. In 2014, McCain sold its entire North American frozen pizza business to Dr. Oetker — a German family company founded in 1891. In 2015, Dr. Oetker took the old McCain pizzas and renamed them Giuseppe Pizzeria. That Giuseppe rising crust in your cart is, by direct corporate descent, the old McCain pizza wearing an Italian name chosen by a German company. And where is it made? London, Ontario — a plant Dr. Oetker opened in 2014 that turns out an average of 400,000 pizzas a day and, per trade press, has made Dr. Oetker the market leader in thin crust and number two in the whole Canadian pizza aisle. German-owned. Ontario-baked. The exact mirror image of Eggo.

While the box is out, the arithmetic. As of January 1 this year, Canada's front-of-package rules are fully in force: high-sodium foods must wear a black-and-white magnifying-glass symbol on the front. For pizza eaten as a meal, the legal reference amount is 200 grams and the main-dish trigger is 690 milligrams of sodium. The retailer listing for the Giuseppe thin crust shows 610 milligrams per one-fifth of a pizza — scale that to the legal 200-gram serving and you are around 1,270. We will re-read the actual box on camera, but the math explains what you are about to see all over this aisle: the magnifying glass is coming for the dinner end of the freezer.

Number five: Swanson, Hungry-Man, and the TV dinner Canada refused to let die.

September 10, 1953: the Swanson TV Dinner hits stores at 98 cents — turkey, cornbread dressing, peas and sweet potatoes in a three-compartment aluminum tray, dinner in 25 minutes. The legend says it was invented to unload 260 tons of leftover Thanksgiving turkey riding around in refrigerated rail cars — a salesman named Gerry Thomas claimed the idea, the Swanson family disputes it, and the fight over who invented the TV dinner has outlived everyone involved. Ten million sold the first full year. Campbell Soup bought Swanson in 1955, spun the frozen side off decades later, and after more corporate hot-potato it landed with Conagra in 2018. Here is the beautiful part: the Swanson-branded frozen dinner survives today, by all accounts, only in Canada. America invented it; we kept it. Whether that is loyalty or stubbornness we leave to the comments.

But the frozen dinner is also where the new law bites hardest, so let us do one honest, verified example. Michelina's lasagna — the budget classic, whose brand is owned these days by Charoen Pokphand Foods of Thailand and made at a plant in Jackson, Ohio — prints 700 milligrams of sodium, 30 percent of a day, on its own Canadian nutrition panel. The legal reference amount for a frozen lasagna is 300 grams, more than the 255-gram tray, and the law says you assess on whichever is greater — which pushes it to roughly 824 milligrams against a 690 limit. Over the line. That box is exactly what the new magnifying glass was built for; if the one in your store does not wear the symbol yet, you are looking at older stock still working through the system. No lecture from us — just bring your glasses to the freezer aisle, because the government finally printed something big enough to read.

Number six: Green Giant.

Ho ho ho. The valley, the giant, the jingle — American to the bone, born in Minnesota in 1903, "Jolly" since a young adman named Leo Burnett gave him a leafy suit and a smile in 1935, and "Ho ho ho" since 1961. The brand today belongs to B&G Foods of New Jersey — the Canadian website says it right in the footer: trademark of B&G Foods, used under license. So, American. Flip the bag. The banner on the Canadian Niblets package: "PROUDLY GROWN AND PACKED IN CANADA." Both things are true — and the company doing the actual growing and packing is Nortera of Quebec, formerly Bonduelle's American arm, which has packed Green Giant vegetables in Canada for thirty years, including at the old Green Giant plant in Tecumseh, Ontario that has been running since 1931 and is still the town's largest employer. American brand, Quebec packer, Ontario corn.

And right now, this exact bag is the subject of a fight in Ottawa. Last October, Nortera announced it would simply buy the Green Giant brand in Canada from B&G — in its own words, so that these brands, quote, "long produced in Canada, are now also locally owned." Three weeks ago, on August 19, the Competition Bureau asked the Competition Tribunal to block that sale, saying its investigation found the deal, quote, "would weaken competition and likely lead to higher prices and fewer choices for staple items at the grocery store." Nortera disagrees and says its operations continue as usual. Nobody has done anything wrong here — this is a referee's question, not a scandal — but think about where we have arrived: the fight over the most American-looking brand in the freezer is about whether it is allowed to become Canadian.

Meanwhile the label on the plain Niblets bag is one word long. "Whole kernel corn." One ingredient. And under the new front-of-package law, plain frozen vegetables with nothing added are exempt outright — the regulation literally treats the vegetable aisle's plain bags as a protected class. One ingredient, zero paperwork. There is a lesson in that.

Number seven: Cavendish Farms.

The other fry dynasty, and the quiet one. In 1980 the Irving family of New Brunswick — yes, that Irving family — bought a little potato plant on Prince Edward Island and named it after Cavendish Beach. Today Cavendish Farms calls itself the fourth-largest frozen potato processor in North America, with its big plants in New Annan, PEI and a 430-million-dollar facility opened in Lethbridge, Alberta in 2019. Which means Canada's freezer-fry aisle is essentially a two-family province: the McCains versus the Irvings, Florenceville versus PEI. The bag reads almost identically to McCain's — potatoes, vegetable oil, salt, caramel colour, paprika extract, sodium acid pyrophosphate, six ingredients, 150 milligrams of sodium per serving, "Made in Canada from domestic and imported ingredients" printed on the product page — and it was $2.97 at Giant Tiger at the time of research. Fun fact for the label readers: there is literally a legal recipe for this bag. Canada's Standards of Identity define, in law, what "Frozen French Fried Potatoes" is and the short list of things it may contain. The humble fry is a regulated food with a federal identity. The pizza next to it is not.

Number eight: Janes.

The quiet champion. Janes has won the BrandSpark most-trusted award for frozen breaded chicken again and again, this year included, and unlike almost everything else in this aisle it is exactly what it looks like: a Canadian family name, founded in 1969, bought in 2012 by Sofina Foods — a privately owned Canadian company in Markham, Ontario that runs sixteen plants across the country. And the label earns the trust: Janes Pub Style strips list chicken breast as the very first ingredient — not mechanically separated anything, the actual breast — followed by water, wheat crumbs and pantry items, with 380 milligrams of sodium, 17 percent, per two strips, and "Made in Canada" on the listing. If you watched other channels warn you about what hides in frozen chicken, here is the calmer Canadian truth: the label tells you which kind you are holding. First ingredient, first answer.

Number nine: the store brands — and the recall that told the truth.

President's Choice and No Name are Loblaw. Compliments is Sobeys. Great Value is Walmart. Great Value's straight cut fries were as low as about $2.50 to $3.64 at the time of research — the cheapest bag in the aisle — and nobody will tell you who actually makes them; the packer's name is not printed, not published, and we won't guess. But sometimes the fine print gets subpoenaed by events. In October 2024, an American company called TreeHouse Foods voluntarily recalled frozen waffles made at its plant in Brantford, Ontario, as a precaution over possible Listeria — and the recall paperwork revealed what no label ever advertised: that one Brantford factory was making the waffles for Great Value, for Selection, for Compliments AND for No Name. Four rival grocers, four different boxes, one production line. A precautionary recall, handled and done — but it settled our oldest question by accident. The store brands are not competitors in the freezer. They are the same waffle in four costumes. And it was made in Ontario.

Now the rules, because this aisle rewards exactly three seconds of reading. One: find the origin line — "Product of Canada" is the strictest legal claim in the store, "Made in Canada from imported ingredients" means assembled here, and the country name on an imported bag of vegetables legally means where it was packaged. Two: count the ingredients — this aisle runs from one to twenty-eight, and the count is the story. Three: look for the grade — frozen peas, corn and fries sold across provincial lines must carry it, and the words to look for are "Canada A." Fancy is for cans; A is for freezers. And four: from now on, look for the little magnifying glass. The plain end of the aisle will never wear one. The dinner end often will. That symbol is the entire episode, printed by law on the front of the box.

The winners. Four of our nine are genuinely, fully Canadian — owned here, and making food here. McCain: family-owned in Florenceville since 1957, fries from New Brunswick, Manitoba and Alberta, still doubling down with the biggest investment in its history. Cavendish Farms: the Irvings' PEI answer, fourth-largest on the continent by its own count. High Liner: 127 years out of Lunenburg, with the honest footnote that the fish sees more of the world than we do. And Janes: Markham-owned, sixteen Canadian plants, chicken breast first on the list. One number to keep you honest on the way out, because we promised the sting: Statistics Canada's price for a 750-gram bag of frozen fries has gone from $1.98 in January 2017 to $3.53 this July — up 78 percent, the steepest climb in the freezer aisle, steeper than pizza, steeper than peas. The most Canadian product in the case is also the one whose price has run hardest. Owning the freezer, it turns out, is not the same as owning the price tag. Frozen food was born on Canadian ice, the biggest fry company on Earth still answers to a town of 1,500 in New Brunswick, and the rest of the aisle changes owners so fast the boxes cannot keep up. Nobody here broke a law. But nobody sent you a memo either — and the memo was printed on the back of the bag the whole time, in very small letters. Small letters are our favourite kind.
