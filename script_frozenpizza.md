# SCRIPT — Frozen Pizza

TITLE: Canadians Must AVOID These 7 "Canadian" Pizza Brands (Only 3 Are ACTUALLY Canadian)

ALTERNATES:
- Canadians Must AVOID These 7 "Mozzarella" Pizza Brands (Canada Has TWO Legal Mozzarellas)
- "It's Not Delivery, It's Discontinued" — What Really Happened to Canada's Frozen Pizza

---

There's a video going around right now telling millions of North Americans that the cheese on their frozen pizza isn't cheese at all — that it's vegetable oil and potato starch whitened with a paint compound. Scary stuff. So I did what this channel does: I went to a Canadian grocery store and flipped every frozen pizza box in the freezer aisle — the famous ones, the store brands, the three-dollar bargain at the bottom. And here's the first surprise: that American scandal mostly doesn't exist here. Nearly every full-size frozen pizza on a Canadian shelf — including the cheapest one in the store — lists real mozzarella cheese: milk, bacterial culture, salt, enzymes. The imitation-cheese pizza is an American problem. But don't relax yet, because what I found on Canadian boxes is stranger, and it comes in two parts. Part one: Canada's food law contains a secret almost nobody knows — this country legally defines two different mozzarellas. One is the real thing. The other is allowed to be more than half water, and the word that unlocks it is printed right on boxes you've bought. Part two — and this is the heartbreaker: the two most Canadian pizza brands in history are gone. One of them was quietly discontinued three years ago and, it turns out, was never made in Canada at all. The other one — the one with the proud Canadian family name from New Brunswick — hasn't actually been that company's pizza for over a decade. It's been German. Seven famous "Canadian" freezer brands whose boxes don't tell you the real story — in order, starting with the most famous slogan in Canadian grocery history — and then the three pizzas that are still actually Canadian, ending with a family story from Quebec that deserves to be the future of this aisle. Let's get into it.

Number one — Delissio. Say it with me: "It's not delivery, it's Delissio." For twenty-five years that line meant pizza night in Canada. So let's give this brand a proper autopsy, because almost nothing about it was what you thought. Start with the name: Delissio was a Kraft trademark sitting in a drawer since the 1980s. When Kraft's American hit DiGiorno — "It's not delivery, it's DiGiorno" — came north in 1999, Kraft simply pulled out the Canadian name and ran the identical playbook: same pizza, same rising crust, twin slogans on either side of the border. Canada's pizza was America's pizza with a different accent. In 2010, Nestlé bought Kraft's entire frozen pizza empire for three point seven billion US dollars, and Delissio became a Nestlé brand. And to be fair to the pizza itself: the cheese was real — "part skim mozzarella cheese: partly skimmed milk, bacterial culture, salt, microbial enzyme." The pepperoni was real pork. The quiet asterisk was on the Deluxe, where the "Italian sausage" was extended with textured soy protein — pork, water, soy filler. Then, on February 1st, 2023, Nestlé Canada announced it was winding down and exiting its entire Canadian frozen pizza and frozen meals business — Delissio, Stouffer's, Lean Cuisine, all of it — over about six months. The company's own explanation, from its Canadian CEO, was simply that, quote, "this decision enables us to further invest in priority categories." And here's the detail that reframes the whole twenty-five years: Nestlé had no Canadian factory making these products. Canada's beloved pizza was made in the United States and trucked north — and when that stopped making financial sense, Canada was simply dropped, while DiGiorno carries on in America to this day. We found no evidence Delissio was ever sold, licensed, or relaunched since. If you still see a Delissio page on a grocery website, it's a ghost — check the actual freezer. There's even a price epitaph in the coverage: reporters noted a pizza that once sold for around three dollars had crept toward seven by the end — the brand priced itself into a corner, and when the trucks from the American plants got expensive, the corner won. Twenty-five years of Canadian pizza nights, and the whole time it was an American pizza wearing a Canadian name Kraft found in a filing cabinet. It's not delivery. It's discontinued. And the second-most-Canadian pizza brand? That story might be even harder to hear.

Number two — McCain. If Delissio was Canada's slogan, McCain was Canada's family: Harrison and Wallace McCain, Florenceville, New Brunswick, 1957 — the company that conquered the planet with frozen french fries. One in every four fries eaten on Earth is a McCain fry. And for decades, McCain Rising Crust and Thin Crust pizzas sat in Canadian freezers wearing that trusted name. Here's what almost nobody noticed. In August 2014, McCain sold its entire North American frozen pizza business — the recipes, the plants in Grand Falls, New Brunswick and Lodi, New Jersey — to Dr. Oetker, the family food giant from Bielefeld, Germany. McCain's own framing was honest: the sale supported its focus on the core frozen potato business — the fries empire. Pizza was a side quest for Florenceville; it was the main event for the Germans. The deal came with a licence to keep printing the McCain name on pizza boxes for just two more years. And in September 2015, the switch happened in plain sight: Dr. Oetker introduced a new brand called "Giuseppe Pizzeria," and its own press release spelled it out — Giuseppe, quote, "comprises of the pizzas formerly served up as McCain Rising Crust… Thin Crust… Multigrain Thin Crust." Read that again. The Giuseppe pizza in your freezer — the one with the Italian name — is the old McCain pizza. Same lines, new label, German owner. Nobody stole anything; McCain chose to sell and refocus on potatoes, and that's fair business. But two footnotes matter for this audience. First: in May 2018, Dr. Oetker closed the old McCain plant in Grand Falls, New Brunswick — about 180 jobs — and moved production to Ontario; locals told the CBC it felt like, quote, "a slap in the face." Second, the fairness clause: today's Giuseppe still uses real cheese — "mozzarella cheese: milk and/or ultrafiltered milk, salt, bacterial culture, calcium chloride, microbial enzyme" — and it's made in Canada. The pizza is fine. The "McCain" memory attached to it is a costume. And where exactly is it made? That's the next entry, because one building now feeds this entire aisle.

Number three — Dr. Oetker Ristorante. The thin, crispy, "restaurant-style" one — and the entry where we meet the aisle's new landlord. Dr. Oetker is a fifth-generation German family company, and in May 2014 it opened its first North American pizza plant in London, Ontario — about a hundred million dollars, including roughly nineteen million in federal and provincial government grants. Ten years later — after a further expansion in 2018 — that one plant employs about four hundred and thirty people and turns out roughly four hundred thousand pizzas every single day: Ristorante, Giuseppe, Casa di Mama, the whole Dr. Oetker North American lineup, from one address in southwestern Ontario. Between Delissio dying and McCain selling, one German family company now makes most of the name-brand frozen pizza in your Canadian freezer aisle — in Ontario. Now flip the Ristorante box, because it earns a fair verdict: "Edam and mozzarella cheese: milk, bacterial culture, salt, calcium chloride, microbial enzyme." Real cheese. Real all-pork pepperoni. And at 430 milligrams of sodium per quarter pizza, it's actually the lightest sodium touch we found. Two decodes, though. First, the label on the back says "made in Canada from domestic and imported ingredients" — under CFIA rules that means the pizza was made here, but it is not the stricter claim "Product of Canada," which requires about 98 per cent Canadian ingredients. Made-in-Canada is a factory claim, not a farm claim — worth knowing on every box in this aisle. Second, the price math: at recent prices Ristorante runs about two dollars per hundred grams — nearly four times the cost of the budget rising crust per gram of actual food. Thin crust is elegant; it's also less pizza in the box. If you're enjoying this so far, a quick like genuinely helps more Canadians learn what's behind their freezer aisle. Because the next entry is where the second mozzarella comes out of hiding.

Number four — Compliments, Selection, and the pizza-snack shelf: the "pizza mozzarella" entry. Here is the law almost nobody in this country knows, and I want to put the actual numbers on screen. Canada's federal cheese standards define Mozzarella — the real thing — as at most 52 per cent moisture and at least 20 per cent milk fat. But the same law defines a second, separate legal cheese called "Pizza Mozzarella": up to 58 per cent moisture, as little as 15 per cent fat. And a third, "Part Skim Pizza Mozzarella," may be up to 61 per cent water with just 11 per cent milk fat. Same aisle. Same word "mozzarella." Different legal recipes. It goes deeper: for real mozzarella, at least 83 per cent of the protein must come from actual milk or cream. For Pizza Mozzarella, only 63 per cent must — the rest can come from "other milk products," which is exactly where the label phrase "modified milk ingredients" walks in. And what is that phrase? The regulation's own list: casein, caseinates, whey, milk serum proteins, ultrafiltered milk — quote — "any other component of milk the chemical state of which has been altered." It's all dairy — this is not a chemical scare, and Pizza Mozzarella is a real, federally standardized cheese engineered to melt beautifully on an industrial pizza line. But it is a different cheese, rebuilt from dairy parts, and the tell is printed in plain sight. Where did we actually find it? Sobeys' Compliments pizzas — some list "pizza mozzarella cheese: milk ingredients, modified milk ingredients, salt…" — and the pizza-snack shelf: McCain Pizza Pockets and Metro's Selection pockets both list mozzarella made with "modified milk ingredients." Even mighty Ristorante sneaks "modified milk ingredients" into its parmesan garnish. So here's the sentence to remember, and it's the twist of this whole video: on Canadian shelves, the full-size pizzas — even the cheap ones — use real mozzarella, while the "pizza mozzarella" and rebuilt-dairy wording hides in the snack formats and garnishes. Flip the box; read the bracket after the word cheese. Milk first? Real deal. "Modified milk ingredients" first? Now you know what you're holding. And for the full picture, here's the entire legal cheese ladder in this country, from most cheese to least: real Mozzarella at the top — 52 per cent moisture cap, 83 per cent milk protein. Then Pizza Mozzarella — wetter, leaner, 63 per cent. Then "Processed Cheese," real cheese melted with emulsifiers. And at the bottom, a legal category called "Processed Cheese Food" — which, by regulation, only has to be 51 per cent cheese. Barely half. Every one of those can end up somewhere in a freezer aisle, and every one has a different legal recipe hiding behind a similar-sounding name. One label ladder, four different products — and the bracket after the word "cheese" tells you which rung you're on.

Number five — Pillsbury Pizza Pops. Speaking of the snack shelf — this one is pure Canadiana, and most Canadians have no idea. Pizza Pops were invented in Winnipeg in 1964 by a local restaurateur named Paul Faraci; they've been made in Winnipeg, and only Winnipeg, ever since. You cannot buy them in the United States. And yet the brand hasn't been Canadian-owned in nearly forty years — Faraci's creation was sold to Pillsbury in 1987, and Pillsbury has belonged to American giant General Mills since 2001. A Winnipeg invention, made in Manitoba, owned in Minneapolis. Now, the record requires one serious chapter, told plainly with the government's own numbers. In December 2025, Pizza Pops were recalled across all major flavours, with the recall expanded in January 2026, over E. coli contamination — the Public Health Agency of Canada confirmed 30 cases across seven provinces, with eight people hospitalized. The recall was completed, and on March 19th, 2026, the Public Health Agency formally closed its investigation, stating the outbreak appears to be over. Product on shelves today is post-recall stock. No fear-mongering here — the system did what it's designed to do, publicly and traceably — but a sixty-year-old Canadian icon just had the roughest year in its history, and if a box has been sitting in your deep-freeze since before Christmas 2025, check the recall list before dinner. Add the sodium reality of pocket-format snacks — smaller servings, lower thresholds under the new labelling law — and this beloved Manitoban deserves the label-flip more than anything else in the aisle.

Number six — Daiya. The plot twist entry. Remember the American video's scandal — cheese made of oil and starch? Canada does have exactly one product like that in the pizza freezer… and it's the honest one. Daiya's dairy-free pizzas top their crusts with "mozzarella STYLE shreds": filtered water, tapioca starch, coconut oil, chickpea protein. No milk, no cheese — and no pretending: that word "style" is doing exactly the legal and moral work it should, for shoppers who can't or won't eat dairy. The Canadian angle is the ownership arc: Daiya was founded in Burnaby, British Columbia in 2008 and basically invented the modern vegan-cheese category — a genuine Canadian food-tech success story that ended up in dairy-free pizzas, shreds and slices across the continent. In 2017, it was sold to Otsuka, a Japanese pharmaceutical company, for four hundred and five million Canadian dollars — one of the biggest paydays in Canadian food-startup history, and one more famous "local" label whose profits now cross an ocean. If you want the still-independent version of this idea, Toronto's Wholly Veggie makes cauliflower-crust vegan pizzas and remains Canadian-owned — the small print rewards the curious. Add it to the pile: Delissio to Switzerland's Nestlé, then extinct. McCain's pizzas to Germany's Dr. Oetker. Pizza Pops to America's General Mills. Vegan pioneer Daiya to Japan's Otsuka. One by one, every famous "Canadian" name in this freezer ended up owned somewhere else. Which raises the question the rest of this video answers: what's left? Before the winners, sixty seconds on the money and the new label. Canadian cheese is expensive by design — supply management protects dairy farmers with quotas and a 245 per cent tariff wall on imported cheese; the trade-off is the sticker price. The pressure that creates is real: a decade ago, restaurant chains legally dodged the tariff by importing American mozzarella pre-mixed with pepperoni as duty-free "pizza kits" — until Ottawa slammed that door in November 2013 with a customs memo literally titled "Tariff Classification of Pizza Kits." Restaurants then got their own discounted milk class for fresh-pizza mozzarella. Frozen-pizza factories got neither — which is exactly why the second legal mozzarella and "modified milk ingredients" exist. And one more thing arrived this January: the new front-of-package "High in sodium" symbol is now mandatory, and frozen pizza is judged as a main dish — cross 690 milligrams per reference serving and the magnifying glass goes on the front of the box. Classic pepperoni pizzas run close to a thousand, in a country where the average person already eats about 2,760 milligrams of sodium a day against a 2,300 target. Watch the freezer doors this year: the labels are about to start confessing on their own. One last piece of context, because it explains the whole aisle: Canadians eat over fifteen kilograms of cheese per person per year, mozzarella is our second-biggest cheese behind cheddar — some months it even takes the lead — and cheese prices rose about eighteen per cent in four years. Every trick in this video, from the second mozzarella to the modified milk ingredients, is somebody engineering around that number. Now — the three that are still actually Canadian.

Best option number three — no name. The twist that mirrors everything this channel keeps finding: the cheapest box in the aisle has the cleanest cheese line in the entire category. no name thin crust pepperoni: "mozzarella cheese — milk, salt, bacterial culture, microbial enzyme." Four dairy words, textbook, real. Real pork pepperoni. And it's a Loblaw brand — a Canadian company — at the lowest price point in the freezer. If you want the same trick from the other big-box, Walmart's Great Value rising crust runs about three and a half dollars with plain "mozzarella cheese" on the label too — about fifty-four cents per hundred grams, the cheapest real-cheese pizza dinner in the country — though that one's American-owned, so it doesn't get counted in our Canadian three. Either way, the lesson stands: in Canada, price does not predict cheese purity. The bargain shelf passed the test the premium American brands failed. And run the old slogan's math while you're standing there, because the economics of "it's not delivery" have never been more lopsided: a delivered large pizza in most Canadian cities now lands at twenty-five to thirty dollars once fees and tip are in. The no name box is a fifth of that — with a cheese bracket cleaner than half the delivery chains would dare print. Pizza night for a family at the price of a delivery tip: that part of the old promise still works; it just works from the yellow box now.

Best option number two — President's Choice Wood Fired. Here's a claim with no legal definition — "wood-fired" — attached to a pizza that actually means it. Loblaw's listing says it plainly: baked in a wood-fired oven on volcanic stone. Real mozzarella cheese, a crust of five recognizable ingredients — flour, water, sunflower oil, salt, yeast — and about six dollars for a proper deluxe. Remember: words like "wood-fired," "stone-baked" and "artisan" have zero legal meaning in Canada; the only rule is that a label can't lie. So a brand that backs the romance words with a literal wood-fired oven — and prints real cheese behind them — is what using marketing honestly looks like. One homework item: the box's fine print will tell you where it's baked; read the country-of-origin line while you're there. At a dollar fifty-eight per hundred grams it sits comfortably between the bargain shelf and the boutique shelf — the value sweet spot of the category.

Best option number one — O Sole Mio. And here is the pizza this whole video was looking for. In 1961, the Napolitano family opened a pizzeria in Montreal. Sixty-plus years later, that family business has grown into a forty-eight-thousand-square-foot plant in Boisbriand, Quebec, with more than a hundred employees, shipping pizzas across the country — and it is still family-owned, still Canadian, still actually making its pizza here. Stop and appreciate how rare that just became: we walked an entire freezer aisle where the Swiss killed one Canadian icon, the Germans absorbed another, the Americans hold the Winnipeg invention and the Japanese own the Vancouver innovator — and the last family standing is a Quebec pizzeria that simply never sold. That's the whole recommendation: if the "Canadian" on the box matters to you, this is the box where it's true. Check the cheese bracket on your local O Sole Mio like you now check every box — and give the last Canadian pizza family the pizza-night business the extinct brands can no longer earn. Honourable mention to McCain itself, by the way: the pizzas are gone, but Pizza Pockets stayed in the family, and Florenceville, New Brunswick still feeds the world a quarter of its french fries. The empire is fine. It just doesn't make your pizza anymore.

So here's your freezer-aisle test, thirty seconds, three brackets. One: find the word cheese and read what's inside the bracket. "Milk, bacterial culture, salt, enzymes" — real mozzarella, and in Canada that's most full-size pizzas, even the three-dollar ones. Two: if you see "pizza mozzarella" or "modified milk ingredients," you're holding the second legal mozzarella — still dairy, still safe, but a rebuilt cheese with more water and less milk in it, and now you know the law that allows it: 52 per cent moisture for the real thing, up to 61 for the part-skim pizza version. Three: check the front for the new "High in sodium" magnifying glass, and check the serving line — a "serving" is a quarter or a third of the pizza, and almost nobody eats a quarter of a pizza; double the number you read before you decide, because that's the honest arithmetic the serving line is quietly hoping you skip. That's it. No paint compounds, no panic — the Canadian freezer aisle's cheese is mostly honest. It's the flags on the boxes that aren't. "It's not delivery" turned out to mean "it's not even Canadian" — and it took a Quebec family from 1961 and a yellow no-name box to pass the test the famous names couldn't. Flip the box tonight, and send this to whoever in your family still calls every frozen pizza a Delissio — because now you both know why that word is going to sound different.
