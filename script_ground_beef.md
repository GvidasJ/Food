TITLE: 10 Canadian Ground Beef Brands That Don't Use 100% BEEF (8 That Do)

There is a law in this country that almost nobody shopping the meat aisle has ever heard of, and it is a good one. Under Canada's federal food standards, anything sold with the words "ground beef" on the label must be beef and nothing else. No added water. No fillers. No binders. No preservatives. The Canadian Food Inspection Agency's compositional standard is one sentence long and airtight — ground beef is beef, full stop. So how is it possible that we found toasted wheat crumbs, textured soy flour, hydrolyzed soy protein, corn syrup solids, and sodium phosphate sitting in the beef section of virtually every major Canadian grocery chain? Because that beautiful law has a boundary, and the entire industry knows exactly where it is. The moment a package says "beef burgers" instead of "ground beef," the strict standard stops applying and fillers become legal. And composition is only the first way a package of beef can quietly stop being one hundred percent. There is the beef that is not one hundred percent Canadian. The tray that was not one hundred percent the weight you paid for. The tube where nearly a third of the price can legally be fat. Tonight we hold the ten biggest names at the Canadian meat counter to the one hundred percent test — and then show you the eight that actually pass it.

The stakes are not small. Ground beef is the protein Canadian families lean on when money is tight — spaghetti night, taco night, the long weekend barbecue — and Statistics Canada pegs regular ground beef at roughly fourteen dollars and sixty-seven cents per kilogram as of May 2025, up about twenty-five percent in a single year and more than sixty percent over five years. At those prices, every percentage point that is not beef is your money. Everything you are about to hear comes from documents anyone can check: the companies' own published Canadian ingredient lists, Canadian Food Inspection Agency standards and recall records, Statistics Canada data, and reporting by the Canadian Broadcasting Corporation. Where a claim is a company's own marketing, we will say so, and nothing here calls any product unsafe. Ten that fail the one hundred percent test, starting with the most popular brand in the country. Eight that pass. Let's open the cooler.

The yellow box goes first, because no label moves more beef through Canadian checkouts. No Name belongs to Loblaw, the country's biggest grocer at roughly twenty-nine percent of the market, and the No Name Beef Burgers club pack is a national freezer institution. Read the list from Loblaw's own store listing: beef, water, toasted wheat crumbs, textured soy flour, salt, sugars listed as corn syrup solids and dextrose, hydrolyzed soy protein, spice, and onion extract. Two kinds of soy. Wheat crumbs. Two sugars. Nobody at a barbecue ever wished their hamburger were sweeter — the sweeteners are there because sugar browns cheap food faster and makes it taste rounder. All of it is legal, because the box says burgers. But at this particular company the fine print deserves double attention: this is the retailer that admitted its role in the industry-wide bread price-fixing arrangement running from 2001 to 2015, offered twenty-five dollar gift cards, and settled the class actions with its parent company for five hundred million dollars in 2024. And the Canadian Broadcasting Corporation's recent weight investigations found some Loblaw-owned stores selling meat that weighed less than the label claimed — the packaging itself had been weighed and priced as meat, with overcharges running as high as sixteen percent. None of this makes a No Name burger dangerous. It makes the yellow box a perfect opening exhibit: the biggest beef seller in the country, selling a beef product that is not one hundred percent beef, at a chain where the fine print has repeatedly been worth reading twice. The flip test takes five seconds, and on this box it reads like a bakery invoice.

Sobeys wears the green banner across Safeway, Foodland, FreshCo, and IGA — roughly a fifth of the Canadian market — and its Compliments Traditional Beef Burgers fail the same test the same way: beef, water, toasted wheat crumbs, salt, spice and spice extracts, garlic powder, natural flavour, onion powder, straight from Sobeys' own website. Pay attention to that word "Traditional." Traditional, homestyle, classic — the whole industry uses words that point backwards to a grandmother pressing patties from beef she ground herself, a kitchen that contained no wheat crumbs and no spice extracts. The word sells the memory of the pure product; the recipe belongs to the other rulebook. In fairness, Sobeys states its proteins come primarily from Canadian farms and there is no recent scandal attached to Compliments ground beef — and its premium Sterling Silver line carries the Canada Triple-A grade, which is a real federal grade and not a marketing invention. But the same Canadian Broadcasting Corporation weight investigation that caught Loblaw stores also caught Sobeys-owned stores selling underweight meat — the packaging weighed and priced as product, with overcharges between two and sixteen percent on the packages they tested. Think about what that means in the language of this video: the beef inside was one hundred percent beef, exactly as the law demands. The weight on the sticker was not one hundred percent meat. There is more than one way for a package to fail the test, and this one fails it at the scale, not the grinder. When the tray itself counts toward the meat, you are paying beef prices for plastic.

Costco is Canada's third-largest grocer, and its entry on this list is about a different kind of one hundred percent — the kind Costco will not say out loud. Kirkland Signature ground beef has a clean recent record: no recalls in years, no ingredient games, and under Canadian law that fresh grind is pure beef like any other. What Costco does not publish is a Canada-specific answer to the origin question. Kirkland packaging in the organic line has listed multiple possible origin countries, and Canada imported roughly four hundred and twenty-nine million pounds of beef in 2024 — nearly half from the United States, with fast-growing volumes from Australia, New Zealand, and Uruguay. Under our labelling rules, beef processed and packaged in Canada does not have to carry any foreign-origin statement at all. So "bought at a Canadian Costco" and "raised on a Canadian ranch" are two different sentences, and the membership card that tracks every rotisserie chicken you have ever purchased cannot tell you which one applies to your beef. To be clear about what this entry is: it is not an accusation, and Costco's beef may well be substantially Canadian — plenty of Canada's beef supply is. It is about the asymmetry. A warehouse that documents everything, prints its quality standards on giant signs, and inspires more customer loyalty than possibly any retailer in the country has simply decided this one fact does not need to be in writing. Every other number at Costco is precise. This one is missing. Ask at the counter where the beef was raised — it is a fair question from a paying member. How fast the answer comes tells you plenty.

Walmart's Great Value beef burgers carry the lowest sticker in many freezers, and the verified list explains how: beef, water, textured soy protein, and a seasoning blend including salt, mustard, sodium phosphate, spices, and garlic powder. Soy inside the meat, phosphates in the seasoning — the loophole at maximum efficiency. But hold on to your hat for the twist, because it is the strangest verified fact in this entire video: Walmart Canada — the American chain — is the only one of the five big grocers with a public guarantee that one hundred percent of its FRESH beef is sourced in Canada, and it was the first Canadian retailer to put certified sustainable Canadian beef labels on its own patties, after buying two and a half million pounds of it. Not Loblaw. Not Sobeys. Not Metro. Not Costco. The most Canadian promise at the meat counter currently comes from Arkansas, and every Canadian-owned chain on this list should be embarrassed by that sentence. At Walmart, the choice is literal: the soy patty in the freezer, or the Canadian-guaranteed fresh grind twenty steps away — often for pennies of difference once you do the per-kilogram math. And do that math out loud, because patties are where per-kilogram pricing goes to hide: a box of twenty small burgers advertises the box price, never the kilogram price, and unit-converted, the budget patty frequently costs as much as the pure fresh grind in the same store. You are not always paying less for the fillers. Sometimes you are paying extra for the circle shape.

Schneiders has been on Canadian tables since 1890, Kitchener-born and now owned by Maple Leaf Foods of Mississauga — proudly Canadian ownership, which we respect. The recipe is another matter. Schneiders beef burgers, per Maple Leaf's own published product information: beef, water, toasted wheat crumbs, wheat flour, salt, wheat gluten, vinegar, onion powder, spice. Count the wheat: crumbs, flour, and gluten — three separate wheat ingredients in one beef patty, which is less a recipe and more a bakery with a cattle problem. Nothing about this product changed recently; what changed is that beef costs what it costs now, and a patty that is partly wheat at today's beef prices is an arithmetic problem worth solving before checkout. A century of trust is real. So is the flip test, and the box fails it in three languages of wheat. If you grew up on these — most of us did — understand that the heritage is doing the same job here that "Traditional" does on the green box: pointing your memory at a butcher counter that this recipe left a long time ago. The brand is Canadian, the ownership is Canadian, and the credit for that is genuine. The patty is still not one hundred percent beef, and no amount of history changes what the ingredient panel says in plain English and French.

Metro, the country's fourth grocer, makes the list for what it does not say. Metro states that the vast majority of its beef is North American — a big continent, only part of which is Canada — and its heavily advertised local-sourcing commitment applies to its chicken, not its beef. Here is the rule that makes silence profitable: imported beef sold in its original packaging must declare "Product of" its country, but beef processed, ground, or repackaged at a Canadian facility owes you no origin statement whatsoever. The unlabelled tray is not lying. It is just not talking, while imports climbed more than a fifth in early 2025 alone. In a year when Canadians are scanning packages for maple leaves like never before, remember the difference: a maple leaf printed on the banner over the cooler is decoration; the words "Product of Canada" printed on the tray are a regulated claim requiring virtually all-Canadian content. One of them means something, and it is never the wallpaper. Metro can do better, and later in this video we will show you the exact shelf where Metro proves it — because the same company that will not finish the sentence about its everyday trays runs one of the best all-Canadian beef programs in the country. Silence, it turns out, is a choice made per shelf.

The seventh entry is the most expensive tray in the cooler, and it fails the test in the opposite direction — it is the "grass-fed" premium grind, and what is not one hundred percent here is the definition. Canada has no prescribed federal standard for the term grass-fed. None. The claim is policed only under the general rule that labels must be truthful and not misleading, and any certification behind it comes from private programs, not from the Canadian Food Inspection Agency. So when you pay a premium of several dollars per kilogram for those two words, you are trusting whichever definition the seller chose — fed grass exclusively, finished on grass, or merely acquainted with a pasture at some point in its life. Some producers are rigorous and transparent, and the private certifications behind the best of them are real. But the label alone cannot tell you which kind you are holding, because in Canada nobody official ever wrote down what the words must mean. The check: look for a named third-party certification or a named farm on the package, and if the premium tray offers neither, you are paying extra for vocabulary.

The eighth failure is not a brand — it is the cheapest tube in every store in the country, and the word doing the damage is "regular." Canadian fat ceilings run in four tiers: extra lean at most ten percent fat, lean seventeen, medium twenty-three, and regular up to thirty percent. Thirty. Nearly a third of the priciest staple in your cart can legally be fat that renders out in the pan and leaves as smoke and drippings. And that shrinkage is the hidden second price: the sticker quotes the raw weight, but your tacos are made from the cooked weight, and the higher the fat, the wider that gap. At fourteen dollars and change per kilogram, saving a dollar on regular over medium and then pouring part of it down the drain is not a bargain — it is a donation. These are ceilings, not guarantees, but the label will never tell you where in the range your tube landed, and the price gap between regular and medium is usually a dollar or two per kilogram — far less than the value of the extra beef you keep after cooking. Do the shelf math with the seven-point fat gap in mind: medium is usually the honest sweet spot, enough fat to carry flavour on a grill without paying beef prices for what ends up in the drain. The industry named the highest-fat tier "regular" for a reason. Regular is what you buy when you do not look.

The ninth entry is the tray with no flag at all — the unlabelled ground beef sold everywhere in this country. It is one hundred percent beef by law. What no rule requires it to be is one hundred percent Canadian, and no big grocer other than Walmart will guarantee that in public. Loblaw promotes Canadian-first buying and certified sustainable beef purchases; Sobeys says primarily Canadian farms; Metro says North American; Costco says little. Every one of those phrasings was lawyered, and none of them is the two words that would end the conversation. Until a chain prints "Product of Canada" on the everyday tray, the honest reading of the meat counter is this: the beef is pure, the paperwork is quiet, and the quiet is a choice being made about you, the person paying for it. Canada has roughly forty thousand beef farms and one of the most respected cattle industries on Earth. The idea that a shopper in Saskatoon cannot tell from the label whether their supper came from Alberta or Uruguay is not a technical limitation. It is a policy, and policies change when enough customers ask the question at the counter, out loud, politely, every single week.

And the tenth spot goes to the package that contains zero percent beef and, to its credit, admits it — the plant-based ground sitting one shelf over, in a tray designed to look exactly like the real thing. Canada polices look-alike meat labelling: simulated products must be clearly named for what they are, and the plant-based grounds sell honestly as plant-based protein. This entry is not an attack on anyone's diet. It is here because the tray is beef-coloured, beef-shaped, and beef-adjacent on purpose, and more than one hurried shopper has grilled the wrong package for a crowd of confused guests. It is the only product in the meat cooler legally required to confess it is not beef at all — which, after nine entries of careful corporate wording, makes it strangely the most honest thing in the aisle. There is a lesson in that contrast worth saying plainly: the further a product is from beef, the more clearly Canadian rules make it speak, while the products closest to beef get to whisper. If the plant-based tray can wear its zero percent on the front of the package, the burger boxes can wear their wheat crumbs there too. They just do not have to. Yet.

Before the eight that pass, sixty seconds on what stands behind every single package tonight. Roughly eighty-five percent of Canada's beef is processed by two companies in three buildings: Cargill at High River, Alberta and Guelph, Ontario, and JBS — the Brazilian giant, largest meatpacker on Earth — at Brooks, Alberta. That bottleneck has a history. In 2012, E. coli at the Brooks plant, then called XL Foods, triggered the largest beef recall in Canadian history — more than eighteen hundred products, eighteen confirmed illnesses, a suspended licence, a four million dollar settlement, and the plant ending up in JBS hands. In spring 2020, High River became the site of North America's largest single-site outbreak of the coronavirus pandemic, with roughly nine hundred and fifty workers infected and a two-week shutdown that wobbled the national beef supply. None of that appears on any label, but every tray in this video passed through that system. Two companies, three buildings, one of them foreign-owned, standing between roughly forty thousand Canadian beef farms and your grocery cart — when people ask why beef prices jumped twenty-five percent in a year, or why one plant's bad week can empty coolers across four provinces, this bottleneck is a large part of the answer. Remember it when we get to the final recommendation, because one item on the passing list walks around that bottleneck entirely.

Now the eight that pass the one hundred percent test.

First, the answer hiding in plain sight: any tray in Canada labelled "ground beef," at any store, from the fanciest banner to the deepest discounter. The federal standard is the brand — beef and nothing else, enforced by law, no premium required. There is a satisfying irony in this being the top recommendation: after ten entries about products engineered around a strict standard, the winning move is simply to buy the thing the standard protects, which also happens to be one of the cheapest forms of pure beef per kilogram in the entire store. The industry built a wall of boxes, shapes, and adjectives around it hoping you would never notice. Buy the tier that fits the meal, and cook it to Health Canada's number: seventy-one degrees Celsius internal, because colour is not a reliable indicator and a five dollar thermometer beats guessing every time.

Second, Walmart's fresh counter — because a public one hundred percent Canadian sourcing guarantee, backed by certified sustainable purchases, is exactly the promise this video spent ten entries asking for, whoever's flag flies over head office.

Third, President's Choice Thick and Juicy burgers, the patty that passes the flip test at the country's biggest chain: beef, water, salt, spices, and herbs, with "no fillers added" printed on Loblaw's own listing across the sirloin and prime rib versions too. The same company selling the paragraph-long club pack proves it knows how to make the one-line version — the difference is printed on the boxes, and now you know what the price gap buys.

Fourth, M and M Food Market sirloin beef burgers: beef, water, salt, onion powder, spices, garlic powder — no fillers, and fifty years of Canadian freezer trust behind it. One twist, disclosed in full: Canadian-founded M and M went to Parkland in 2022, and Parkland was bought by Sunoco of Texas in a deal that closed in October 2025. The burger still passes on its ingredients. The maple leaf in the logo now reports to Dallas.

Fifth, Metro's Platinum Grill Angus — the exclusive line that is one hundred percent Canadian Triple-A Angus. The same Metro that stays quiet about its everyday trays proves it can say the whole sentence when it wants to; reward the shelf where it says it.

Sixth, the Certified Angus Beef program at Loblaw banners — a named, audited program with standards you can look up, sitting in an ordinary grocery cooler at ordinary-adjacent prices.

Seventh, President's Choice Free From ground beef, for anyone whose priority is how the animal was raised: a verified "raised without the use of antibiotics" claim, printed in exactly those words, available at every Loblaw banner — a defined claim, unlike the vocabulary we flagged on the premium trays.

And eighth, the oldest answer and, after everything you have heard about two companies and three buildings, the most quietly radical one: your local butcher, or a Canadian farm that ships direct through services like truLOCAL. The butcher grinds in front of you, names the cut and the farm, and answers the origin question before you finish asking it — no loopholes, no lawyered phrasing, no bottleneck plant in the middle. It sometimes costs a little more per kilogram, and after tonight you can say precisely what the extra dollars buy: composition you watched happen, origin you heard out loud, and weight measured on a scale you are standing in front of. Nothing in this video is more one hundred percent than that.

So here is the whole night in three questions you can carry into any store in Canada. Does the label say ground beef or burger — because that single word decides whether fillers are even legal. Which lean tier are you paying for — because "regular" can be thirty percent fat at fourteen dollars a kilogram. And where was the animal raised — because the package is allowed to stay silent, and you are allowed to ask out loud. The law protecting Canadian ground beef is one of the best food laws we have; every trick tonight lived in the products wearing its reputation. Nothing on this list is unsafe — the recent recalls in this country were small, store-level events, and the system publishes its problems, which is more than many countries can say. This was never about safety. It was about honesty, arithmetic, and a maple leaf that is sometimes printed and sometimes real. And notice what the eight winners had in common, because it was not a price point: every single one either said the whole sentence — no fillers, one hundred percent Canadian, raised without antibiotics, a grade you can look up — or let you watch the grinding happen with your own eyes. Honesty at the meat counter turns out to be perfectly affordable. It always was. The expensive part was never the beef; it was not knowing which words to check.
