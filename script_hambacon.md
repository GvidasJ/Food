We bought the bacon. We bought the ham. We pulled ownership records, federal food standards, Statistics Canada price data, and a couple of corporate stories that honestly belong in a movie. We read every label. That's all we do.

Before we get to brand number one, you need to see the shape of this aisle. Because it's not what it looks like. It looks like a dozen competing brands. It's actually three companies.

Company one is Maple Leaf Foods, which owns Maple Leaf and Schneiders. Company two is Olymel, out of Quebec, which owns Olymel, Lafleur, and more. Company three is one most shoppers have never heard of, a seven and a half billion dollar giant from British Columbia, and we're going to keep you waiting on that one, because the way we found out is the best story in this video.

Here's the twist for this episode, and it's a good one. In our butter video, our cheese video, our frozen food video, the question was always the same. Which of these brands is actually Canadian? In the ham and bacon aisle, the answer, almost across the board, is that they are. The real questions are stranger. Who owns whom? What exactly is in the package? And what does the law actually say ham and bacon are?

Let's start with the law, because it surprised us. Under Canada's federal standards of identity, ham has a legal definition, and it's about the cut of the pig. Ham means meat from the hind leg of a swine carcass, above the tarsal joint. The hind leg. That's it. If it's not from the hind leg, it's legally not ham.

Bacon has three separate legal standards. Bacon, the regular kind, must be boneless pork belly. Back bacon must be boneless pork loin, and here's a detail we love, the standard requires it to be cured and smoked to an internal temperature of at least fifty-eight degrees Celsius. That number is in the regulation. And Wiltshire bacon, an old English name frozen into Canadian law, is loin with a portion of the belly attached.

And the strangest part. For standardized bacon, the mandatory ingredients are salt and a preservative. Mandatory. Bacon that isn't cured isn't bacon under the standard. Oh, and buried in the compositional standards is our favourite piece of trivia this year: federal law specifically authorizes artificial maple flavour in cured pork, and an orange flavour in cured pork bellies. Someone, at some point, needed that written down.

Now. The countdown. Most popular first, and in Canada, there's no debate about where that starts.

Number one: Maple Leaf.

The biggest name in the aisle, and if you watched our frozen food video yesterday, you already know how this company got its modern shape. When Wallace McCain was pushed out of McCain Foods by his own brother, he didn't retire. In 1995, he and the Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan bought control of Maple Leaf Foods. Thirty years later, the McCain family connection is still there. According to the company's own 2025 shareholder circular, McCain Capital controls 39.49 percent of Maple Leaf's shares. The brand on your bacon goes back much further. The company's own history page says the Maple Leaf brand first appeared in 1898.

Where's the bacon made? Winnipeg. In 2021, Maple Leaf finished a 182 million dollar expansion of its Winnipeg plant, which the company calls its Bacon Centre of Excellence. That is a real corporate title. By the company's own fact sheet, that plant turns out about 1.8 million packages of bacon a week, and the company's line is, quote, Winnipeg is the Bacon Capital of Canada.

Now the label. We bought Maple Leaf Original Natural Bacon, 375 grams, nine ninety-nine at Metro when we checked. The ingredient list is five items long. Pork, water, sea salt, cultured celery extract, spice. Per two slices, fifty grams, the printed sodium is 310 milligrams, thirteen percent of your daily value.

Hold on to that fourth ingredient. Cultured celery extract. Because there's a story on this label. Back in 2012, CBC Marketplace tested the company's Natural Selections line, which advertised no added preservatives, and reported that the natural-sounding celery extract is actually a nitrite, the same curing agent as the conventional stuff, and Maple Leaf said it would change the packaging. Today, the federal labelling guidance is blunt about this category of ingredient: when cultured ingredients act as preservatives, claims about the absence of preservatives are not appropriate. So here's the label-reading rule this episode runs on. Natural bacon lists cultured celery extract. Conventional bacon lists sodium nitrite. Both are declared ingredients. Both cure the meat. The federal maximum for nitrite going into side bacon is 120 parts per million, and for other cured meats 200. We're not telling you either style is better or worse. We're telling you the two ingredient lists are closer cousins than the front of the package suggests.

And we can't talk about Maple Leaf honestly without talking about 2008. That summer, a listeriosis outbreak was traced to ready-to-eat meats from the company's Bartor Road plant in Toronto. The federal government's independent investigator put the toll at 22 deaths. Maple Leaf's own history page today says 23 tragic deaths. Either number is a catastrophe, and the company has never hidden it. CEO Michael McCain went in front of the cameras and said, quote, Tragically, our products have been linked to illness and loss of life. To those people who are ill, and to the families who have lost loved ones, I offer my deepest and sincerest sympathies. And then, quote, The buck stops right here. He said the two advisers he hadn't listened to were the lawyers and the accountants. The company recalled the products, closed the plant, and settled class actions for as much as 27 million dollars. No charges were ever involved. This was a recall, an apology, and a civil settlement. And in business schools, that response is now taught, literally, as the gold standard of corporate crisis management. We think Canadians over fifty remember exactly where they were that August. We did too. It changed how this country regulates ready-to-eat meat, and it's part of the story of every deli wall in Canada.

One more Maple Leaf fact, and it's a teaser. Last October, this company cut itself in half. Hold that thought until the end of the countdown, because the name involved is a ghost.

Number two: Schneiders.

The Dutch Girl. If you grew up in Ontario, this logo is practically family. The company's own history page tells the story: John Metz Schneider, a Kitchener button factory worker, hurt his hand, started making sausage in the family kitchen, and sold it door to door. The brand was officially founded in 1890, when Kitchener was still called Berlin. The Dutch Girl herself first appeared alongside a Dutch Boy, the boy got dropped, and she was trademarked in 1955.

Now the part most shoppers never heard. In 1998, the Schneider family sold the company. And they didn't sell it to Maple Leaf, who wanted it badly. Maple Leaf launched a hostile takeover bid worth about 130 million dollars. The family instead sold to Smithfield Foods of Virginia, the American pork giant, for about 144 million. So for six years, from 1998 to 2004, the most Ontario brand in the deli aisle was American. Then in 2004, Maple Leaf came back and bought Schneiders from Smithfield for, all in, roughly half a billion dollars including debt. That's in Smithfield's own filing with the American securities regulator.

The bittersweet footnote: the historic Kitchener plant on Courtland Avenue, the one Schneider opened in 1925, closed in 2015. Workers gathered for a last call. Production moved, primarily to a new plant in Hamilton. The Dutch Girl is Canadian-owned again, but she doesn't live in Kitchener anymore.

The label. Schneiders Hickory Smoked Classic Cut Bacon, 375 grams, nine ninety-nine when we checked. Eight ingredients: pork, water, salt, sugar, sodium phosphate, sodium erythorbate, sodium nitrite, smoke. Now put it next to the Maple Leaf natural bacon. Same parent company. Same price. Same size. Five ingredients on one, eight on the other, celery extract on one, sodium nitrite on the other. One company, running both playbooks, side by side in the same cooler.

Number three: the store brands. President's Choice, No Name, Great Value, Compliments.

Here's where the price question gets serious. When we checked, No Name mild sugar cured bacon, 375 grams, was four fifty-five at No Frills. PC naturally smoked bacon was seven dollars. Great Value sits with Walmart. Compliments around seven thirty-four through Sobeys. And the national brands we just covered, nine ninety-nine. Same size package. More than double the price, top to bottom.

So who makes the cheap stuff? Here's the honest answer: the label won't tell you. Store brand packages say prepared for the retailer. The actual packer isn't printed. And we are not going to guess, because we don't report what we can't verify. But history gives you one clean, documented peek behind the curtain. In 2008, when Maple Leaf recalled products from that one Toronto plant, the recall list didn't just say Maple Leaf and Schneiders. It included No Name. Compliments. Kirkland Signature. Burns. Shopsy's. Product supplied to McDonald's and Tim Hortons. One plant. Dozens of labels. And just this July, an American import notice revealed Maple Leaf packing bacon under two brand names we'd never heard of for the US market. The practice is alive and well. We just can't tell you, today, who packs your No Name bacon. Nobody outside the industry can.

One store brand deserves a special magnifying glass. The Great Value bacon listing carries the phrase, Made in Canada from domestic and imported ingredients. That wording is doing legal work. Under the federal origin rules, Product of Canada means all or virtually all, ninety-eight percent, of the ingredients, processing and labour are Canadian. Made in Canada with that qualifier only means the last substantial transformation happened here. The pig, in other words, may have travelled. Read that fine print in the store, because we'll be re-reading it on camera.

And the store brands run the same two-tier playbook as the nationals. PC's regular bacon lists sodium nitrite. PC's Free From line lists cultured celery extract. The premium version of the story costs more everywhere.

Number four: Olymel.

The biggest name out of Quebec, on packages under Olymel, Lafleur, Flamingo and more. And the ownership structure here is, arguably, the most Canadian thing in this entire video. Olymel's majority owner is Sollio Cooperative Group, founded in 1922, Canada's largest agri-food cooperative, democratically governed by thousands of farmer members. When you buy Olymel bacon, the majority owner is a Quebec farmers' co-op.

It hasn't been an easy decade. In 2023, Olymel announced the permanent closure of its Vallée-Jonction slaughter plant in the Beauce, 994 jobs, with the company's president saying the fresh pork business had lost more than 400 million dollars in two years. That's the company's own explanation, and it tells you something real: even the giants of this aisle have been bleeding.

The label. Olymel smoked bacon, 375 grams, eight ninety-nine when we checked, and the same eight-ingredient conventional list as Schneiders, right down the line to sodium nitrite and smoke. And once again, the two-playbook trick: Olymel's natural-style shaved ham lists cultured celery juice powder, while its regular cooked ham lists sodium nitrite. Every company in this aisle is running both stories at once.

Number five: Piller's.

A Waterloo, Ontario story. April 1957. A master butcher named Wilhelm Huber and a partner named George Piller each put five hundred dollars into a small Waterloo butcher shop. That's the whole founding capital of what's now on every deli wall in Ontario. In 2011, Piller's was bought for about 75 million dollars by a company from Richmond, British Columbia. Remember that, because it's the thread we pull on next.

The label is where Piller's earns its segment. Piller's Black Forest Ham lists, among other things, pork broth and carrageenan. Those are water-binding ingredients, declared right on the package, and they point at one of the best consumer protection laws you've never heard of. Under the Food and Drug Regulations, if water or phosphates are added to solid cut meat, cooked ham must still contain at least twelve percent meat protein, and here's the beautiful part, the label has to confess. The words describing added water become part of the product's legal name, and the minimum meat protein percentage must be printed in the common name, in letters at least half the size of the product name, no smaller than one point six millimetres. The law literally sets a font size so the watery ham can't whisper.

And then the punchline. Side bacon is exempt. From the protein minimum, from the declaration, all of it. The regulation names it specifically. Your ham has to tell you how much of it is water. Your bacon doesn't.

By the way, Black Forest ham. We searched the standards of identity, the compositional standards, and the federal labelling rules. The term appears nowhere. In Canada, Black Forest is pure marketing. Wiltshire bacon is in the law. The Black Forest isn't.

Number six: Grimm's. And the reveal.

Grimm's, the western deli brand, Since 1951 on the package, carries the best claim stack in this episode: Proudly Canadian, naturally smoked, and the Verified Canadian Pork logo, which is the pork industry's own traceability mark for federally inspected Canadian pork.

But here's what the front of the package doesn't say. Grimm's and Piller's have the same owner. So does Freybe, the Langley BC charcuterie name. So does Harvest, out of Yorkton, Saskatchewan. They all belong to Premium Brands Holdings of Richmond, British Columbia, a publicly traded company with about seven and a half billion dollars in revenue. It might be the biggest Canadian food company you've never heard of, and that's on purpose. Its strategy is to keep the founders' names on the packages. Four different family stories on the shelf. One shareholder behind all of them. All Canadian, to be clear. But when Grimm's competes with Piller's for space in your fridge, the same company wins either way.

Number seven: Fletcher's. The corporate pretzel.

Stay with us, because this one is almost a riddle. The company that became Premium Brands, the giant we just told you about, was founded in 1917 as Fletcher's. It renamed itself Premium Brands in 2000. Then in 2004 it sold its own original Fletcher's business in Canada to Sofina Foods of Markham, Ontario. And then, in 2016, Premium Brands bought back Fletcher's American operations. So today, Fletcher's bacon in a Canadian store comes from Sofina, and Fletcher's in an American store comes from the company that used to be Fletcher's. A brand that got sold away from its own company.

Sofina, for the record, is one of Canada's quiet powerhouses too. Privately owned, it has Janes, Lilydale, Mastro, San Daniele and Vienna in its stable, and in 2021 it went global, buying the British group that owns Young's Seafood. Canadian-owned, top to bottom.

Number eight: the ghosts.

If you're over fifty, you remember names in this aisle that are gone. Burns, from Calgary, Pat Burns opened his slaughterhouse in 1890 and built one of the great meat empires of the West. Sold to Maple Leaf in 1996. Mitchell's, from Saskatoon, became part of Schneiders in 2002, which Maple Leaf then swallowed. Swift. Gainers. The names outlived the companies, some of them still turning up on packages years later. That 2008 recall list we mentioned actually included Burns branded product, twelve years after Burns the company ceased to exist. In this aisle, brands are ghosts that keep working.

Number nine: peameal bacon. The one that's actually ours.

Here's your Toronto history. In 1854, an English pork packer named William Davies arrived in Toronto. His company grew into what was said to be the largest pork packing operation in the British Empire, half a million hogs a year, and that scale is why Toronto is nicknamed Hogtown. Toronto's food lore credits Davies himself with inventing peameal bacon: wet-cured pork loin, trimmed lean, rolled in ground yellow peas to preserve it, which is where the name comes from. The peas gave way to cornmeal, and the yellow-crusted roll at the deli counter today is that same idea, a century and a half on.

And now the punchline you can test at the border. What Americans call Canadian bacon, the smoked round stuff on their eggs benedict, is not Canadian at all. And here at home, we checked every federal food standard: peameal bacon has no legal definition anywhere. The most Canadian item in the case is pure tradition, zero regulation. The federal nutrient database files it, wonderfully, as back bacon, cornmeal, peameal. In 2016 the peameal bacon sandwich was named Toronto's signature dish, and take off, eh, if you disagree.

Before the finale, three label rules to take to the store, and one piece of arithmetic.

Rule one, the sodium magnifying glass. As of January 1, 2026, packages high in sodium must carry the new front-of-package warning symbol. The threshold for these products is fifteen percent of the daily value, 345 milligrams, measured per serving or per the official reference amount, whichever is greater. Now watch what happens in this aisle. Deli ham's reference amount is 55 grams, and federal nutrient data puts typical deli ham well over 600 milligrams for that amount, roughly double the trigger. Deli ham wears the magnifying glass. Bacon dances right on the line: the Maple Leaf pack we bought works out to about 335 milligrams against a 345 trigger, just under, while generic bacon data lands just over. Brand by brand, slice by slice. Check each package, because we did, and the results genuinely differ.

Rule two, smoke has a legal grammar. Naturally smoked means real smoke from burning hardwood, sawdust, or corn cobs. Smoked can include liquid smoke applied with heat. Smoke flavour means liquid smoke stirred into the meat. Three different phrases, three different processes, all defined by the CFIA.

Rule three, find the round stamp. Federally inspected meat carries the round inspection legend with the crown, and it can legally appear anywhere on the package except the bottom. While you're at it, enjoy this: the regulations even define the see-through window on a bacon package. The rules carve out a strip the width of a slice of bacon so that, quote, the customer is able to see the bacon. Parliament thought of everything.

And the arithmetic. Statistics Canada's average price for 500 grams of bacon was five dollars and three cents in January 2017. This July, six ninety-seven. Up almost thirty-nine percent, after peaking at seven sixty-eight last October. The ham and bacon consumer price index is up about forty-six percent over the same stretch. Part of the backdrop: China put a twenty-five percent tariff on Canadian pork in March 2025, and when Ottawa struck a partial deal with Beijing this January covering canola meal, lobster, peas and crab, pork wasn't in it. Canada is the world's third-largest pork exporter. That tariff is still the live wound under this entire aisle.

And yet we keep buying. A Maple Leaf commissioned poll once found forty-three percent of Canadians, asked to choose between bacon and, well, romance, chose bacon. It was the company's own poll, so season to taste. But per Statistics Canada, we still go through about fourteen kilograms of pork per person per year.

So. The finale, and the ghost we promised.

On October 1, 2025, Maple Leaf Foods completed the spin-off of its entire pork business, the Brandon super plant, the hogs, all of it, into a separate public company. And the name they chose was Canada Packers. The exact company, name for name, that was created in 1927 when William Davies' Hogtown empire was folded together with its rivals. Canada Packers became Maple Leaf Foods in 1991, disappeared for thirty-four years, and on October 2, 2025 it started trading on the Toronto Stock Exchange under the ticker CPKR. The company that made Toronto Hogtown became Canada Packers, became Maple Leaf, and became Canada Packers again. Ninety-eight years, and the wheel came all the way around.

Which brings us to the scoreboard, and the strangest one we've ever read. Maple Leaf, Canadian. Schneiders, Canadian again, with an American chapter. Olymel, owned by a farmers' co-op. Piller's, Grimm's, Freybe, Harvest, all Canadian through Premium Brands. Fletcher's, Canadian through Sofina. The store brands belong to Loblaw, Sobeys, and in Great Value's case, American-owned Walmart. In the ham and bacon aisle, the question was never really who's Canadian. Almost everyone is. The questions were who owns whom, what's actually in the package, and whether the label has to tell you. Now you know all three.
