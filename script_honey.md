On the twentieth of July this year, the Canadian Food Inspection Agency published its Food Fraud Annual Report for 2024 to 2025. It tested eighty-one samples of honey for adulteration with foreign sugars — cane sugar, corn syrup, rice syrup. Fifteen came back unsatisfactory. Nineteen per cent.

Now, before anybody screenshots that number, here is the sentence that almost nobody who quotes it reads you. It is CFIA's own, printed in the same report. "Results of targeted sampling are not representative of overall compliance in the Canadian marketplace."

Targeted means CFIA went looking where it already expected to find something. Importers with a history of non-compliance. Unusual trading patterns. It is a search, not a survey. It is not a random sample of your grocery store.

Now, the thing I said I would come back to, and it is the one that changes how you read that nineteen per cent: what happens when CFIA measures the shelf instead of the importer.

Twice in seven years it ran both programmes on honey side by side. In 2019 to 2020, targeted sampling failed at thirteen point three per cent, and random retail monitoring failed at two per cent. In 2020 to 2021, directed sampling failed at twenty-five point nine, and random retail at four point eight. Four to five times the failure rate when the regulator goes looking on purpose, versus when it simply takes jars off shelves. And across all seven years the targeted rate has gone twenty-one point seven, thirteen point three, twenty-five point nine, twenty-two point five, sixteen, twelve, nineteen — so it is not getting worse and it is not being solved either.

That is the honest answer to "is the honey in my grocery store fake." The last two times the Canadian government actually measured the shelf, the answer was in the low single digits. The nineteen per cent is a hit rate on a stacked deck, by CFIA's own design and its own published admission.

Now the part that replaces the glass of water.

Five home tests circulate constantly. Drop honey in water and see if it dissolves. Light it with a match. Rub it between thumb and finger. See if it crystallises. Put it on bread. Here is the National Honey Board — an American industry body, so we will mark that on screen — on those, quoting their answers. On the water test: "Pure honey is naturally water-soluble and will always dissolve in water." On the flame test: "Whether the match or candle will light depends entirely on the amount of moisture in the honey variety you are using. All honey contains some moisture." On the thumb test: "Pure honey comes in a variety of thicknesses, all of which are pure." And they close the document like this: "Remember, the only way to test honey is with a certified lab."

Two more get pushed that no regulator has bothered to address, so here is the argument from first principles. The iodine test detects starch. Every adulterant CFIA reports finding is a sugar syrup — cane, corn, rice, beet, barley, wheat — and a syrup is what you get after starch has already been broken down. You would be testing for the thing that was converted before the syrup was made. The vinegar test looks for carbonates, which is chalk-type bulking, and across seven years of CFIA honey reports there is not one carbonate finding. Both tests look for adulterants that are not the adulterants.

I am also not going to tell you to hold a flame to a jar of hot sugar syrup, and it would not tell you anything if you did.

And the reason none of it works is not that the tests are unlucky. Canada's own regulator needs two different laboratory instruments running in parallel. Stable isotope ratio analysis catches added cane and corn sugar above a detection threshold of about seven per cent, and is essentially blind to rice syrup. Nuclear magnetic resonance catches both by comparing the sample's full chemical fingerprint against a reference library built, in CFIA's words, "from the analysis of over eighteen thousand authentic honey samples."

Here is what happened when CFIA ran both on the same two hundred and forty jars. Isotope testing flagged fifteen. NMR flagged forty-four. Thirty-four of the NMR failures were invisible to isotope testing, and seven of the isotope failures were invisible to NMR. Two machines, and each one missed most of what the other caught.

A glass of water and your thumbnail are not a third method.

So here is what does work, and it works because Canadian honey labelling is prescriptive. The regulations dictate the exact words.

Check one, and nobody teaches this. Word order in a blend declaration is quantity order, and it is legally required to be. Section 279 of the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations: the countries must be shown "in descending order of the proportion of honey from each state." So "A Blend of Canadian and Argentinian Honey" means Canadian is the larger share. "A Blend of Argentinian Honey and Canadian Honey" means the imported honey is. Those two sentences look nearly identical on a shelf, same type size, same font, and they mean opposite things. What the label will not tell you is by how much — no percentage is required. A blend could be ninety-five to five and read the same as fifty-fifty.

Check two. The word "Canada" in front of the grade number is itself an origin signal. Honey produced in Canada is graded Canada Number One, Two or Three. Imported honey, and blends of imported and Canadian honey, are graded Number One, Two or Three — with no "Canada." Different words, by regulation.

Check three, and this is where it flips on you. Do not read the grade as a quality ranking. Bee Maid's own FAQ, quoting the company: "Canadian honey is graded based on colour, moisture, and filtration, and this grading system is not a quality measurement." Then this: "BeeMaid Raw Honey meets Canada No. 1 standards for both colour and moisture, and is classified as Canada No. 3 for filtration. This is because Raw Honey is strained using a wider filtration process than our Creamed Honey."

Read that twice. A Canada Number Three can be the less processed honey. If you have been trained by other videos to reach for Number One, you have been trained to pick the more filtered jar.

Check four. "Raw" has a definition and "pure" does not. CFIA's guidance says honey "can be considered as raw when it has not undergone any treatment or process such as heat treatment, other than minimal heating used for the purpose of extraction, or filtering." Note the wording — "can be considered as." There is no maximum temperature, no time limit, no threshold and no verification requirement attached to it. And I searched the full consolidated text of both the Food and Drug Regulations and the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations: the words "unpasteurized" and "unfiltered" do not appear anywhere in either one. "Pure" has no honey-specific definition at all. Four of the words most likely to be on the front of the jar are the four the rulebook says least about.

Check five. Crystallisation is not a defect and it is not a test. Canadian law settles this outright. The legal definition of honey — section 15.1.16 of the Canadian Food Compositional Standards — includes the phrase "a fluid, viscous or partly or wholly crystallized consistency," and the requirements for Canada Number One include those same words. Fully crystallised honey can be Canada Number One. It is written into the definition. The only time crystals become a regulatory issue is when the jar says "liquid," because that word is a claim, and Canada Number One liquid honey has to be "free from visible crystals." The counter-myth is wrong too — some genuine varieties barely crystallise at all. Crystals tell you about the flower and the temperature of your cupboard. They tell you nothing about syrup.

Check six. Price per hundred grams, with the floor in your head. National farm gate is about sixty-three cents per hundred grams; on the Prairies, closer to fifty. So a five-hundred-gram jar holds roughly two dollars fifty to three dollars twenty of Canadian honey before packing, freight, retail margin and tax. That tells you when a claim is implausible. It does not tell you what is in the jar, and I am not going to pretend it does.

So, to land this.

The most repeated claim about Canadian honey is that the supermarket shelf is full of fakes. The failures are real, they are concentrated before the jar, and CFIA is pulling six-figure tonnages off the market — a hundred and thirty-three thousand four hundred and twenty kilograms in the last reporting year alone. But every Canadian-produced sample it tested that year passed, and the last two times it measured the retail shelf at random, the failure rate was two per cent and four point eight per cent.

What the label hides is usually not the honey. It is "Canada Number One" being a statement about moisture and colour that most people read as a statement about country. It is a Number Three sometimes being less processed than a Number One. It is the order of two country names being a legally binding statement about quantity that nobody ever explained to you. And it is five of the nine brands in this video not telling you who put the honey in the jar.

A Canadian beekeeper gets about two dollars twenty-seven a pound. Go and look at what you paid per hundred grams. Post both numbers in the comments. Then tell me where you think the rest of it went.
