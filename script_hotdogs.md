# SCRIPT — HOT DOGS

TITLE: Canadians Must AVOID These 8 "Meat" Hot Dog Brands (Only 3 Are ACTUALLY Worth Buying)

ALTERNATES:
- Canadians Must AVOID These 8 "Meat" Hot Dog Brands (The Best One Costs $1.50)
- We Investigated Every Famous Hot Dog In Canada (Only 3 Are Worth Buying)
- Canada's Favourite Hot Dogs Have A Secret Printed Right On The Label (We Read All 8)

---

Before the last long weekend of the summer, we need to talk about what's actually inside Canada's hot dogs. According to Maple Leaf Foods' own fact sheet, Canadians bought forty million kilograms of hot dogs in a single year — the average household buys eight packs a year, and seventy-seven percent of Canadian households buy them. Costco Canada alone sold over thirty-six point six million food-court hot dogs last year. This is one of the most-eaten foods in the country, and almost nobody has ever read the package. So we read them all — that's all we did, we read the labels and the regulations behind them — and we found three things hiding in plain sight. First, an ingredient at the top of several of these lists that, under Canadian law, is not legally "meat" at all — and the law makes them print it right there. Second, a word game about preservatives that goes all the way up to the most expensive "natural" hot dogs in the aisle — because here's something almost nobody knows: under Canada's legal definition, every single wiener in this video must be cured with nitrite. Every one. Even the ones that say they aren't. We'll show you the actual law. And third, the black-and-white magnifying glass that started appearing on packages this year — hot dogs don't just trigger the new warning symbol for sodium. Most of them trigger it twice. Eight brands, biggest sellers first, three winners at the end — and the best one costs a dollar fifty. Let's start with the king.

Number one. Schneiders. If any hot dog is Canada's hot dog, it's the one with the little Bavarian girl on the pack. Maple Leaf's own executives have called Red Hots — introduced in 1928 — Canada's number one hot dog brand. And the story behind it is genuinely great: in 1886, a Kitchener button-factory worker named J.M. Schneider, earning a dollar a day, hurt his hand and started making sausage at home from a family recipe. By 1890 he had a company. The giant Schneiders sign has greeted drivers coming into Kitchener since 1961. Now here's the part of the story the package doesn't tell. In 1998, Maple Leaf Foods tried to buy Schneiders in a hostile takeover — and lost. The family sold to Smithfield Foods of Virginia instead. Then in 2004, Smithfield turned around and sold the whole company to Maple Leaf anyway, for about three hundred and seventy-eight million dollars. Canada's number one hot dog spent six years American before coming home to the company it originally said no to. Now flip the pack. Red Hots Original: pork, water, modified corn starch, salt — then a run of preservation ingredients — and sodium nitrite. That corn starch matters, because under Canada's meat standards, starch in a wiener is a legally defined "filler," and wieners are allowed to contain it as long as the whole product keeps at least nine and a half percent meat protein. The label we filmed says three hundred and fifty milligrams of sodium per thirty-eight gram wiener. Hold that number, because here's the new law it runs into. As of this year, Health Canada judges every package against a reference amount of fifty-five grams of cooked sausage — bigger than one small wiener — and if the sodium hits fifteen percent of your daily value, three hundred and forty-five milligrams, the package must wear the high-in-sodium magnifying glass on the front. Do the math on almost any wiener in this aisle and it clears the line — and the saturated fat usually clears its own line at the same time. Two symbols, one hot dog. And Schneiders' Juicy Jumbos? Seven hundred milligrams in a single seventy-five gram jumbo. That's not a scary mystery — it's printed right there. You were just never told which number to read. One more thing before we move on, about the price tag. The week we filmed this, Red Hots were three ninety-seven at Walmart — on rollback from a regular price of seven twenty-seven. Juicy Jumbos, four ninety-seven, rolled back from nine ninety-six. Nearly half off, both of them, like clockwork before a long weekend. When the discount is that deep that reliably, the "regular" price is doing a job, and the job isn't charging you seven dollars — it's making four dollars feel like a gift. Grocery stores run hot dogs as bait all summer. Enjoy the bait; just know you're the fish.

Number two. Maple Leaf Natural Top Dogs. The premium shelf. Sea salt, no artificial preservatives, "if you can't spell it, you won't find it in our food" — that was the actual campaign line when Maple Leaf reformulated about a hundred products in 2018. At around seven ninety-nine for three hundred and seventy-five grams at time of filming, this is the most expensive mainstream hot dog in the aisle — roughly triple the cheapest. Read the ingredients: chicken, pork, water, tapioca starch, sea salt, modified vinegar, spice, cultured celery extract, cherry powder, smoke. No sodium nitrite anywhere. Which brings us to the single most important piece of law in this video. Canada's meat standards define a wiener as a cured product — and "cured" has a precise legal meaning: salt plus at least one hundred parts per million of nitrite or nitrate must be added. Not "may be." Must. A wiener that isn't cured isn't legally a wiener. So how does a hot dog with no sodium nitrite on the label exist? The federal food agency's own guidance answers that, in writing: cultured celery powder "can be used as an alternative source of nitrites" — because it "contains preformed nitrites," and processors are told to dose it to reach that same one hundred parts per million minimum without passing the two hundred maximum. In other words: the celery is how the nitrite gets in. This isn't our theory — CBC Marketplace lab-tested Maple Leaf's celery-cured products back in 2012, and the expert they quoted put it plainly: "For all intents and purposes it is bio-chemically identical." Maple Leaf updated its labels after that story. And the same agency's claims guidance says that when a "cultured" ingredient has been deliberately built to act as a preservative, no-preservative claims "are not appropriate" — their words, about cultured ingredients generally. Put those two documents side by side and decide for yourself what the phrase "natural ingredients" is doing on this package. One more label fact while you're holding it: three hundred and seventy milligrams of sodium per thirty-eight gram wiener — higher than several of the cheap hot dogs coming up next. And this celery play isn't one brand's trick — it's a whole family tree. Maple Leaf also owns Greenfield Natural Meat Co., whose wieners list cultured celery extract. President's Choice runs the same play with its Free From wieners — pork, water, sea salt, buffered vinegar, cultured celery extract — at six to eight dollars a pack. Everywhere you see a hot dog priced at double the shelf average with the word "natural" doing the heavy lifting, flip it over and look for the celery. It's become the most profitable vegetable in the meat aisle.

If you've got a pack of either of those in your fridge right now for the long weekend — and statistically, most of you do — hit the like button before you grab the tongs. It genuinely helps these investigations reach more Canadians. Now let's go to the bottom shelf, because that's where the law gets interesting.

Numbers three, four and five. The store brands: no name, Great Value, Compliments. Around two fifty for a no name pack at time of filming, about three dollars for Compliments, four ninety-seven for a nine-hundred-gram Great Value family pack — the cheapest hot dogs in Canada. And on two of these three labels you'll meet the ingredient this video's title is about. Great Value Original Wieners, second ingredient: mechanically separated chicken. Compliments Wieners, first ingredient: mechanically separated chicken. And no name? The chicken hot dogs we checked listed plain chicken — not the mechanically separated kind — alongside enriched wheat flour and corn starch, at roughly two and a half dollars for twelve. The cheapest brand in the country isn't the one using the paste. Check your own pack, because formulations shift, but that's what our label said. Now, here's exactly what mechanically separated means, from the legal definition: meat recovered by forcing carcass parts through machinery that removes most of the bone and cartilage, with limits written in the law itself — no more than zero point zero two seven percent calcium for every one percent protein, no bone fragments larger than two millimetres. And here's the sentence that matters most: under Canada's meat standards, the legal definition of "meat" does not include mechanically separated meat. It's its own category. That's why it must be spelled out in the ingredient list by name, in order of proportion — the law forces the honesty, and the label is where it lives. We're not going to tell you it's dangerous — it's inspected, permitted, and everywhere. We're telling you the first ingredient in some of Canada's most popular hot dogs is something the law itself declines to call meat, and you were never told to look. The wheat flour and corn starch beside it are those legal fillers again. And now the twist you don't expect: the Compliments wiener — mechanically separated chicken, first ingredient, cheapest tier — carries three hundred milligrams of sodium per wiener on Sobeys' own numbers. That's the lowest figure of any brand in this video. Lower than the seven-ninety-nine natural one. The cheapest, least glamorous label in the aisle is also the least salty per wiener. That's why you read labels instead of prices.

Number six. Hygrade — Quebec's back-of-the-fridge classic. First ingredient: mechanically separated chicken. Then water, pork, salt, corn starch, and sodium nitrite, spelled out. Nothing unusual — except who owns it. Hygrade belongs to Maple Leaf Foods. The same company whose flagship brand promises — on its own ingredient-promise page — that sodium nitrite is on the list of ingredients it will "never" use in Maple Leaf-brand food. That promise is real, and it's also precisely worded: it covers the Maple Leaf brand. The same company's Hygrade label lists sodium nitrite, its Schneiders labels list sodium nitrite, and its "natural" labels list the celery version of the same cure. One corporation, three vocabularies, one legally identical curing process. And take a second to appreciate how wide this one corporation runs: Maple Leaf Foods of Mississauga — controlled by the McCain family since Wallace McCain bought in back in 1995 — owns Schneiders, Maple Leaf, Hygrade, Shopsy's, Mina, and Greenfield. By its own fact sheet it makes thirty-four types of hot dogs across ten different brands. When you stand in the wiener aisle comparing "competing" brands, most of the competition is one company talking to itself in different fonts. Keep that in mind every time a front panel makes you a promise: check whose name the promise is actually written in.

Number seven. Lafleur — the other half of the Quebec fridge, the steamie standard. Owned by Olymel, whose majority shareholder is Sollio, the big Quebec agricultural co-operative — so this one genuinely isn't Maple Leaf, one of the few. But it holds this video's record: twenty ingredients, the busiest label in the entire aisle. It opens with mechanically separated meats — chicken and/or turkey — then pork and/or beef, water, wheat flour, salt, corn starch, hydrolyzed soy protein, and on and on through milk ingredients and canola oil to sodium nitrite and smoke. Wheat, milk, soy and mustard all appear on the allergen line of a hot dog. For the record, Lafleur wieners were also recalled once, in May 2022 — a Class 3 recall, the lowest-risk category, for what the notice called "texture and appearance issues." Nobody was hurt; the paperwork is public. We mention it because it's the truth and because it tells you the inspection system actually looks at hot dogs. Twenty ingredients, though. A steamie deserves better.

Number eight. The chicken wieners — Maple Lodge Farms, and the halal brands, Zabiha Halal and Mina. These are in millions of Canadian fridges, and the pattern across every one we checked is identical: first ingredient, mechanically separated chicken. Maple Lodge's pack advertises forty percent less fat than other popular wieners — and that claim can be perfectly true while the first ingredient is still the one the law won't call meat. Mina — which, by the way, is also Maple Leaf Foods — lists mechanically separated chicken, corn starch, corn syrup solids, and sodium nitrite. The lesson of the whole avoid list in one sentence: the front of a hot dog package sells you a word — natural, less fat, all beef, halal — and the back of the package is where the law makes everyone tell you the rest.

And speaking of "all beef" — one quick myth to kill before the winners. An "all beef" wiener does not mean a wiener that contains only beef. Under the federal labelling rules, "all," "pure" and "100%" apply to the meat portion — the meat must be exclusively beef. The legal fillers can still come along. Lesters All Beef wieners, the Montreal classic, list beef first and then corn starch, wheat flour, soy protein and wheat gluten. All beef, plus four fillers, perfectly legal. The rules even say a "100% beef" sausage can't be stuffed in a pork casing — the lawyers thought of everything except telling you where the claim stops. If you want a wiener that's just meat, salt, spice and smoke, the claim to look for isn't "all beef." It's a short ingredient list. Which is exactly where we're going. Though first, a fond word for the prairies, because we know Saskatchewan is watching: Harvest Meats has been making wieners in Yorkton since 1928, and it's one of the last big wiener brands that's neither Maple Leaf nor Olymel. The label is honest, mid-pack, nothing hidden: pork and beef up front — no mechanically separated anything — but then modified milk ingredients and corn starch, the legal fillers again, and five hundred and fifty milligrams of sodium in a fifty-six gram wiener. A genuine Canadian institution with a genuinely ordinary label. Read it with clear eyes and love it anyway; that's allowed.

But first, the piece of history that explains why Canadian meat labels are the way they are. In the summer of 2008, ready-to-eat deli meats — not hot dogs, and that distinction matters, so we're saying it clearly: sliced deli meats — from a Maple Leaf plant in Toronto were linked to a national listeriosis outbreak. Fifty-seven Canadians were confirmed sick, and at least twenty-two died. Maple Leaf's CEO Michael McCain went on television, took responsibility, and said the two advisers he'd ignored through the crisis were the lawyers and the accountants — "this is about our being accountable for providing consumers with safe food." The class actions settled for about twenty-seven million dollars; the independent Weatherill investigation delivered fifty-seven recommendations; and the food-safety machinery Canada has today — the inspection rules, the public recall database we quoted two minutes ago — was reshaped by that summer. It's the worst chapter in this industry's modern history, and it's also the reason a hot dog label in Canada is one of the most tightly policed pieces of paper in your kitchen. Read it. That's what it's for.

So what do you actually buy? Three answers, all Canadian, all short labels.

Winner number three. Grimm's European Wieners, from British Columbia. Founded in 1951 by the Grimm brothers in East Vancouver, still making wieners at their Richmond plant. The label: pork, water, beef, salt, dextrose, mustard, spices — and a real sheep casing, which is where that snap comes from. No mechanically separated anything, no flour, no starch, no milk, no soy — the pack says gluten-free, lactose-free and soy-free, and the ingredient list backs it up. It does list sodium nitrite, plainly, by name — and after everything you've learned today you know why: it's legally required to be there in some form. The honest label spells it. That's the whole difference.

Winner number two. Freybe European Wieners — also British Columbia, and the shortest ingredient list we found in the entire category. Ten ingredients: pork, water, salt, glucose, sodium phosphate, spices and spice extracts, dextrose, sodium erythorbate, sodium nitrite, smoke. That's the complete document. One meat, no fillers, no mechanical separation, nothing renamed to sound like a salad. Compare that to Lafleur's twenty and you understand what you're actually paying for at the deli end of the aisle: subtraction.

And winner number one. The Kirkland Signature beef wiener — yes, the Costco one, and yes, for the reason you're hoping. This is the same all-beef quarter-pound wiener served at the food court, and the retail pack's own listing says it plainly: all-beef, no meat by-products, no corn syrup, gluten-free. The ingredient list we checked runs about a dozen items — beef, water, dextrose, salt, spices, garlic, the curing set, sodium nitrite named honestly — no mechanically separated meat, no fillers. One number needs context before the comments find it: the label says about twelve hundred and thirty milligrams of sodium per wiener — but that's a one-hundred-and-twenty-three gram quarter-pounder, three times the size of a regular wiener. Gram for gram it sits right beside everything else in this video; read per hundred grams, never per wiener, when the wieners are different sizes. And then there's the price. A dollar fifty at the food court — hot dog and a pop with refills — a price Costco Canada has held, by every account, since the nineteen-eighties, through every wave of food inflation this country has seen. Thirty-six point six million sold in Canada last year. When Costco's CFO was asked about it, his answer was one sentence: "I also want to confirm the $1.50 hot dog price is safe." The best hot dog deal in Canada is also one of the cleanest mainstream labels in the category. Sometimes the game has a happy ending.

Now zoom out for the last two minutes, because the aisle looks different once you've seen the whole board. Maple Leaf's fact sheet says it outright: "Wieners are synonymous with summer in Canada, and we see a huge spike in sales on the summer long weekends." This weekend — the last long weekend of the season — is the single biggest hot dog moment on the Canadian calendar, which is exactly why we made this video now, and exactly why every wiener in the flyer is on sale. Statistics Canada, meanwhile, tracks the national average price of wieners — four dollars and one cent for a four-hundred-gram pack as of June, up from about three-twelve in 2017. And here's a detail we can't resist: StatCan's own tracking basket used to price a four-hundred-and-fifty-gram pack — the standard pack shrank underneath the statisticians, and the official measuring stick had to shrink with it. Meanwhile the great bun injustice, Canadian edition: our wiener packs are mostly twelve, and Dempster's sells buns in eights — and also, quietly, in twelves. The mismatch everyone jokes about is optional here; check the bag count before you rant at the barbecue. And that street-meat cart downtown? For decades, Ontario's rules only allowed street vendors to sell precooked sausages and little else — the province didn't loosen that until 2007. The hot dog isn't just on your grill; it's written into provincial regulation, federal standards, and one legally binding definition of the word "cured." Our favourite discovery of this entire investigation, though, is hiding in the canned-goods aisle. Canada's food standards contain separate legal definitions for "Wieners and Beans" and "Beans and Wieners." We are not joking. A can labelled Wieners and Beans must, by law, contain at least twenty-five percent wieners. Flip the words to Beans and Wieners, and the legal minimum drops to ten percent. Same words, opposite order, fifteen percentage points of hot dog. There is a federal laboratory method — it has a number, FO-36 — for measuring the wiener content of a can of beans. Somewhere in this country, that is somebody's job. The word order on the front of a can is legally load-bearing, and if that doesn't convince you Canadian food labels are worth reading, nothing will.

So here's your checklist for the wiener aisle, and it fits on a napkin. Read the first ingredient — if it says "mechanically separated," you now know that's a legal category the law itself doesn't call meat, printed there because the rules force it. Treat "no added nitrites" and "natural" as an invitation to find the words "cultured celery extract," because a wiener without a cure isn't legally a wiener. Remember "all beef" covers the meat, not the recipe. Look for the magnifying glass — from this January it does the sodium math for you, and hot dogs usually earn it twice. And when the wieners are different sizes, compare per hundred grams, never per wiener. Tell us in the comments which brand is on your grill this weekend, and which label genuinely surprised you — ours was the cheapest wiener in the video having the lowest sodium number in it. If someone you know is manning a barbecue this long weekend, send them this before they light it. And we'll see you at the next label.
