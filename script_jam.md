The jam aisle in a Canadian grocery store is one of the most misleading stretches of shelf space in the entire building. Gingham lids that suggest a grandmother. Orchard drawings that suggest a farm. Handwriting fonts that suggest a small batch. And under all of it, a legal standard that almost nobody shopping this aisle has ever read. Canada's food regulations say a jar can only be called jam if it contains at least forty five percent of the named fruit. There is a second standard right beside it that lets a jar be called jam with far less fruit, and you have read past those words a hundred times without noticing. Seven brands on the avoid list today, two that earned genuine worth it placements. Every jar is strawberry. Every price was checked at a named store on the twenty sixth of September twenty twenty six. Every ingredient list is read off the Canadian label, filmed, on screen, while the words are spoken. The list runs from the brand with the least shelf space in this country to the brand with the most. Number one is the jar you grew up with. Don't skip ahead.

Before the first brand, here is the yardstick you will use seven times. Four checks, all printed on the jar, none of them on the front where the marketing lives. Check one is the common name. Canadian law makes the manufacturer print what the food legally is, usually in small type near the volume, and in this aisle four answers matter. Jam means at least forty five percent of the named fruit under section ten point four point one of the Canadian Food Compositional Standards. Jam with pectin, section ten point four point two, means at least twenty seven percent fruit. Preserve is its own standard, forty five parts fruit to fifty five parts sweetener on a dry basis. And spread, or fruit spread, has no standard at all. No fruit minimum. Nothing. Check two is the first three words of the ingredient list. Under the Food and Drug Regulations, ingredients are listed in descending order by weight as they went into the batch. Whatever is printed first, there is more of it than anything else. And when a recipe has more than one sugar, they now have to be grouped in brackets after the single word Sugars, with that word placed in the list by their combined weight. So when you see Sugars, bracket, and then strawberries, the sugars together outweigh the fruit. That is what the regulation makes the label mean. Check three is the origin line. Check four is the price divided by the millilitres. Every price you hear came from ten Loblaw banner stores in five cities, plus Walmart, Costco and Sobeys online, on one day, so re-check it on your own shelf. Fruit first, then everything that comes after it. Let's start with the avoid list.

That is three down. Before the next four, stop on the word that fools more Canadian shoppers than any brand does. Read the small type under the brand on any jar in this aisle and you will find one of four things. Jam, which the Canadian Food Compositional Standards define at not less than forty five percent of the named fruit and sixty six percent soluble solids. Jam with pectin, not less than twenty seven percent fruit, same solids, pectin required. Preserve, or conserve, a separate Canadian standard and not a fancier word for jam. And spread, fruit spread, jam type spread, jam style spread, which appear nowhere in the standards document at all. Smucker's own Canadian website says it plainly. Quote, there are no requirements for brix sweetness or fruit content in fruit spreads. A fruit spread is a product that is not a jam, jelly or marmalade. End quote. Now the word pure. The Canadian Food Inspection Agency's labelling guidance says the term pure should not be used on a food that is a compound, mixture, imitation or substitute. It says the claim is not appropriate on a food that contains any preservatives, antifoaming agents or colour, even though the standards may permit them. And it says a standardized multi ingredient food may be called pure only if none of the optional ingredients permitted by that standard are added. That is guidance, not a ruling on any brand, and we are not saying any jar on this list breaks it. We are saying that when pure is printed above a list that opens with the word Sugars, the label and the regulation are having two different conversations. Sound familiar? You will.
