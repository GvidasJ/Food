# SCRIPT — KETCHUP

TITLE: We Investigated 8 "Canadian" Ketchup Brands (Only 3 Are ACTUALLY Canadian)

ALTERNATES:

- Canadians Must AVOID Picking Sides In The Ketchup War (We Read Every Label)
- Heinz vs French's: We Read Both Labels (They're IDENTICAL — Word For Word)

---

The bottle in your fridge door has been there your whole life — and if you're Canadian, there's a decent chance you once switched which bottle it was, on principle. In 2016, this country fought a war over ketchup. Families picked sides. A Facebook post from a construction worker in Orillia got a quarter of a million shares. A grocery giant surrendered to public fury in twenty-four hours. And ten years later, with a new trade war on and "buy Canadian" back on everyone's lips, the Prime Minister and the world's most famous ketchup company were still publicly fighting about it. So we did what nobody did in the middle of the war: we read every label in the ketchup aisle — that's all we did, the labels and the regulations behind them — and what we found changes the story you think you know. The two armies in the great Canadian ketchup war sell bottles whose ingredient lists are, word for word, identical. Both of their parent companies are American. There's a federal law that says ketchup must contain sugar — sugar-free ketchup is legally impossible in this country, and we'll show you the rule. And the actually Canadian ketchup — Canadian-owned, Canadian-made, Canadian tomatoes — has been sitting on the same shelf this entire time, for a dollar or two less than either combatant. Eight brands, biggest first, three genuinely Canadian winners at the end. Elbows up. Let's flip the bottles.

Number one. Heinz — and settle in, because this brand carries the whole saga. Heinz opened its first Canadian plant in Leamington, Ontario in 1909, in the town that calls itself the tomato capital of Canada. For a century, that plant was the town: its beans fed Canadian troops in the First World War — there's a story of a soldier in 1917 writing home on the back of a Heinz label about how much the boys loved the shipment. Close to forty percent of all field tomatoes grown in Ontario went to Leamington in a given year. Then in 2013, Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway and a Brazilian private equity firm bought Heinz, and on November 14th, 2013, the axe fell: Leamington would close. Seven hundred and forty jobs — closer to a thousand people counting seasonal workers. Buffett's explanation, verbatim: "It's really a question of having an unprofitable plant and concentrating production in a more profitable plant. You see that in the auto industry." A century of tomatoes, filed under auto-industry logic. The plant had been running about 225,000 tons of tomatoes a year at ninety-three to ninety-five dollars a ton — money that went straight into Essex County farm families. The workers got two weeks' pay per year of service, a twenty-five-hundred-dollar productivity bonus, and a year of health benefits; Heinz paid forty-three stranded farmers a combined 1.8 million dollars and walked. The last day was June 27th, 2014. And here's the part of the story that deserves to be more famous: that same day, a rescue consortium called Highbury Canco took over the plant — starting with 250 jobs, no ketchup contract, just canning and co-packing. Today Highbury employs around six hundred and thirty people, works with twenty-six growers, and — this is CBC's line, not ours — "it now produces more tomato paste than the old Heinz plant produced ketchup." Meanwhile Heinz made Canada's ketchup in the United States for five years — reported to be Fremont, Ohio — until November 2020, when it announced ketchup production was coming home, to its Mont-Royal plant in Montreal. The first Canadian bottle rolled off that line on July 7th, 2021. Canadian tomatoes came back with the 2022 crop. And now read the fine print on the deal, because it's the best plot twist in this video: the tomato paste in every bottle of Canadian-made Heinz ketchup today comes exclusively — their word — from Highbury Canco. From the Leamington plant Heinz abandoned. Heinz walked out of that building in 2014, and now it buys its Canadian soul back from the people who saved it, on a contract running through 2027. The man running Highbury, Sam Diab, grew up with family ties to the old Heinz plant; the company even launched its own retail pasta sauce, Canada Red, which — Diab reports — got a healthy sales bump when the Buy Canadian wave hit. The rescued plant now has its own brand on Walmart shelves. Meanwhile the giant itself keeps convulsing: in September 2025, Kraft Heinz announced it's splitting into two companies, with ketchup landing in an entity provisionally named — we promise this is real — "Global Taste Elevation Co." A hundred and sixteen years after Leamington, the ketchup doesn't even know what its company will be called next year. The bottle itself: tomato paste from fresh ripe tomatoes, sugar, vinegar, salt, seasonings — four grams of sugars and 150 milligrams of sodium per tablespoon — six and a half to seven dollars a litre at time of filming, the most expensive mainstream bottle in the aisle.

If you remember picking a side in 2016 — and if you're Canadian, you do — hit the like button right now, because the next chapter is the one you lived through, and we found the receipts.

Number two. French's. December 2015: French's — the mustard people — launches ketchup in Canada and makes one promise: Leamington tomatoes, processed at Highbury Canco. Late February 2016, a construction worker from Orillia named Brian Fernandez posts on Facebook — verbatim, as the CBC quoted it: "Since Heinz decided to pull the plug on its Canadian plant in Leamington, 740 jobs were lost… Then, French's (known for its mustard) stepped in… We bought a bottle. Absolutely love it!! Bye. Bye. Heinz." More than 107,000 shares within days — it eventually passed a quarter of a million, and the CBC later made an entire short documentary about the fight. One construction worker, one paragraph, and the biggest food company on earth lost control of its story in Canada. Sales exploded. French's tripled its Leamington paste order. An Ontario MPP campaigned until the provincial legislature's cafeteria served nothing else, declaring — verbatim — "Leamington is the tomato capital of Canada. Ketchup is the iconic product made from those tomatoes… It is quintessential Canadiana." French's president Elliott Penner had promised in January to use only Leamington-grown tomatoes, and by the end of February the company announced it was tripling its tomato paste order from Highbury Canco — the rescue plant suddenly had a marquee customer. And then, March 14th, 2016, Loblaws — the country's biggest grocer — quietly pulled French's from its shelves, saying "Demand for the product has been consistently low." The country erupted. Twenty-four hours later — twenty-four hours — Loblaws folded, verbatim: "We've heard our Loblaws customers. We will restock French's ketchup." French's own president marvelled at it: "We've done this for a long time and I don't think I've ever seen a public outcry like this before… they've been heard. We'll always be indebted to them." The Globe and Mail later reported the real reason in an internal Loblaw memo: French's had been "cannibalizing" sales of Loblaw's own President's Choice ketchup. Remember that memo — it comes back at the end of this video with a twist nobody saw coming. The numbers tell you what that week did: per Euromonitor, French's Canadian ketchup sales more than doubled from 2016 to 2017, to 11.1 million dollars, while Heinz fell five percent to 126.4 million — and by 2018 French's held over five percent of Canadian retail ketchup while Heinz had dropped six points from its pre-war share. David didn't slay Goliath, but he took a visible bite. And French's kept its promise, in stages: first, from 2017, every Canadian bottle was filled by Select Food Products in Toronto — 250 bottles a minute at capacity — then in 2021 the operation moved in-house to McCormick's plant in London, Ontario, where it runs today; the bottle is on the Ontario government's Ontario Made registry, with "Made with 100% Canadian tomatoes" on the listing. Even the trade war blessed it: on Canada Day 2018, when Canada's retaliatory ten-percent surtax on American-made ketchup took effect, the Prime Minister marked the day with a visit to the former Heinz plant in Leamington. You cannot write better symbolism. Now, two things we have to show you, because we read labels for a living. First: in July 2017, McCormick and Company of Hunt Valley, Maryland bought French's for four point two billion US dollars. To their credit, the new owners said it immediately — "we don't foresee any changes to our commitment to produce ketchup in Canada" — and nine years on, they've kept that word. But the fact stands: the patriot brand of the ketchup war is exactly as American-owned as Heinz. Canadian factory, Canadian tomatoes, Maryland profits. And second — put the two enemy labels side by side. French's: "Tomato paste (made from fresh ripe tomatoes), liquid sugar, white vinegar, salt, onion powder, spices." Heinz's Canadian can: "Tomato paste (made from fresh ripe tomatoes), liquid sugar, white vinegar, salt, onion powder, spices." Word. For. Word. Identical. Four grams of sugars each — French's actually carries a touch more sodium, 170 milligrams to Heinz's 150, printed right on the panels. The great Canadian ketchup war was never about what's in the bottle. It was about where the tomatoes grow, who keeps the jobs — and, as you now know, not about who keeps the profits. That part, both sides lost.

Now sixty seconds of ketchup law, because Canada has real ketchup law and it's wonderful. The federal standard — section 10.2.9 of the food compositional standards — defines ketchup as the product of red-ripe tomatoes that contains vinegar, salt, seasoning, and — this is mandatory — "a sweetening ingredient." Read that again: by law, ketchup must contain sugar. A sugar-free ketchup is legally impossible in Canada, which is why French's no-sugar product is carefully named ketchup — quote — "type sauce." Second gem: tomato paste legally requires at least 20 percent tomato solids, puree needs 12 — ketchup itself has no minimum tomato content at all. And third, our favourite: the standard sets a legal limit on mould filaments, measured by an official federal laboratory method, dated November 30th, 1981, for counting mould in ketchup under a microscope. Somewhere, that is a career. There's more: ketchup can carry an official federal grade — "Canada Fancy," a real legal term meaning, verbatim, "a good red, ripe tomato colour" and "practically free from skin, seed particles, black specks" — but grading ketchup is optional, which is why you've seen "Fancy" on cans of tomato juice your whole life and never on a ketchup bottle. The law also still requires every drop of ketchup sold in Canada to be heat-processed — a rule that survived from the old regulations word for word. And here's a stat that took us by surprise: we searched Canada's entire live food-recall database for ketchup. Zero recalls of any retail bottle. Not one. The angriest condiment aisle in Canadian history is also, quietly, one of the safest categories in the entire database — high-acid, hot-filled, and boring in exactly the way food safety loves. One more piece of math while we're here: the new warning-symbol rules judge ketchup per fifteen-millilitre tablespoon, and at four grams of sugars per tablespoon it sails under every threshold — even though ketchup is roughly a quarter sugar by volume. A food judged in tablespoons almost cannot be "high in" anything. The magnifying glass will never find ketchup. Your own eyes, reading the label, just did.

Numbers three through seven — the store brands, and the aisle's best-kept secret. Here's the thing we noticed reading every bottle: they all contain exactly four grams of sugars per serving. Every single one. Heinz, French's, PC, no name, Great Value, Compliments — four grams. The differences hide in the wording. President's Choice: tomato paste, sugar, vinegar, salt — and Loblaw's own copy says, verbatim, "always made here in Canada with 100% Canadian-grown tomatoes." Five dollars a litre. Compliments, from Sobeys: the same recipe skeleton, "made from 100% Canadian tomatoes" per Sobeys, and the lowest sodium of any big bottle — 130 milligrams. Five twenty-nine. Then the bargain floor: no name at three sixty-nine and Great Value around three sixty-five — but read no name's sweetener line: "Sugars (glucose-fructose)." Glucose-fructose is the name Canadian law gives to high-fructose corn syrup. American bottles say HFCS; Canadian bottles say glucose-fructose; almost nobody knows they're the same thing — now you do. And Metro's Selection, at three ninety-nine, is the one genuinely different formula in the aisle: it opens with tomato puree — which is water plus paste — followed by glucose-fructose. Same red bottle shape, measurably different document. The store brands run from 37 to 53 cents per hundred millilitres while the two war heroes charge 60 to 70. For bottles that — as established — read nearly identically. The premium isn't in the recipe. It's in the flag each brand is waving, and neither of those flags, corporately speaking, is a maple leaf. One small-print catch while you're comparing: some store brands declare a seventeen-millilitre serving where the giants declare fifteen — a two-millilitre difference that makes panels look closer than they are; compare per-hundred-millilitres when it matters. And if you want to leave the mainstream entirely, the craft shelf exists: a Hamilton company called Twisted Tomato sells a bottle literally named "Plain Boring All Canadian Ketchup" — about nineteen dollars a litre, which is what handmade honesty costs. Or go full pioneer: a can of Unico tomato paste is a dollar twenty-nine and its complete ingredient list is one word — "Tomatoes" — add vinegar, a spoon of sugar and salt and you've made legal ketchup in your own kitchen, mandatory sweetening ingredient and all. Check the can's origin fine print first; after this video, you check everything's origin fine print.

Number eight. Primo — and this is the bottle the war actually built. In 2016, while the country was sharing Facebook posts, a Canadian family company called Sun-Brite Foods in Ruthven, Ontario — the Iacobelli family, ten minutes down the road from Leamington — looked at the chaos and made a decision. Founder Henry Iacobelli, verbatim: "as Heinz moved out of Leamington, we thought it was a good opportunity to get into the market." They launched Primo ketchup with a plain statement: "Every bottle of Primo Tomato Ketchup is packed right here in Canada using only 100 per cent Canadian farm grown tomatoes." Canadian-owned company. Canadian factory. Canadian tomatoes. Essex County soil. Five forty-nine a litre. While two American giants fought over who loved Canada more, a family from the actual tomato capital quietly bottled the answer.

So — the three that are actually Canadian, in a video full of "Canadian" ketchup. Winner number three: Compliments. Owned by Empire, the Nova Scotia-founded, Stellarton-headquartered company behind Sobeys — one of the last great Canadian grocery families; 100 percent Canadian tomatoes per Sobeys; the same tomato-paste-first recipe skeleton as the giants; and the gentlest sodium number in the aisle — 130 milligrams — printed right on the panel, at five twenty-nine a litre. Winner number two: President's Choice — and yes, we see the irony, because this is the brand that Loblaw memo was protecting when it pulled French's. But irony isn't an ingredient: Loblaw is a Canadian company, and its own claim — "always made here in Canada with 100% Canadian-grown tomatoes" — makes PC, at five dollars, the best-value Canadian bottle in the country. The memo brand turned out to be the real thing all along. And winner number one: Primo. The only bottle in this entire video where the brand, the owner, the factory, and the tomatoes are all Canadian — born in Essex County, because of the war, minutes from the plant where this whole story started. Sun-Brite is the Iacobelli family's operation in Ruthven, one of the country's serious tomato processors, the same family behind Unico — which means the one-dollar paste can and the actually-Canadian ketchup bottle come from the same Essex County kitchen table. Five forty-nine a litre, "Product of Canada" on the label — the origin claim the giants can't print. If the 2016 boycott was about anything real, this is the bottle it was about, and almost nobody in that war ever mentioned its name. Consider that corrected. As for French's — buy it happily. It kept its promise: London, Ontario bottling, Canadian tomatoes, real jobs. Just buy it knowing what you now know: the war's beloved underdog banks in Maryland, and the underdog's underdog banks in Ruthven.

Now the ending this story earned. First, what it cost you: Statistics Canada has tracked the average price of a litre of ketchup since 2017 — three dollars and twelve cents then, a peak of four ninety-five in October 2025, four sixty-two as of June. Up 48 percent, with nearly all of the climb packed into the inflation years of 2021 to 2024. The war didn't do that; the decade did. But look at what the war may have helped protect: ten years after Heinz left, the Ontario processing-tomato harvest hit 671,000 tonnes in 2025 — nearly a hundred million dollars back to the farms of Kent and Essex counties, the exact soil this whole story is about. And a note of precision your trivia night will thank us for: Leamington is famous for two different tomato industries — the glass city of greenhouses growing fresh tomatoes on the vine, and the field-grown processing crop that becomes paste and ketchup. Different plants, different farms, different economies; the war was about the second one. The plant Heinz abandoned employs more people with every passing year and supplies the paste for every Canadian bottle of Heinz. And the war never really ended — it just changed fronts. In January 2025, as the tariff fight ignited, the Prime Minister reached for this exact story, urging Canadians to remember "Heinz's ketchup being replaced by French's ketchup." Kraft Heinz hit back within days — "HEINZ Ketchup is made in Canada, by Canadians, using Canadian tomatoes" — took out a full-page ad in the Globe and Mail, and aired a Super Bowl ad shot inside its Montreal plant, assembled in under a week, with its Canadian president declaring "We make 70 per cent of what we sell in Canada, in Canada" — and admitting, in as many words, "We wanted to make sure that Heinz was not going to be the example." This March they announced a quarter-billion-dollar modernization of the Montreal plant. They even signed on as the Official Ketchup of the Maple Leafs and the Raptors, and the bottle on Loblaws' shelf right now is a Toronto Blue Jays fiftieth-season edition — an American-owned company spending millions to drape itself in every Canadian flag it can license. That is what 2016 taught the biggest ketchup company on earth: fear Canadian grocery shoppers. And the receipts say the fear is justified — four in five Canadians told Angus Reid they're buying more Canadian; NielsenIQ measured US-made food sales in Canada falling nearly seven percent while Canadian-made products gained share despite costing on average sixteen percent more; and by early 2026, scanner data showed US-sourced volumes at major Canadian grocers down double digits, with more than half of shoppers saying they're still avoiding American products. Your grocery cart turned out to be a foreign policy instrument. But read the fine print even on victory: Heinz's bottles say "Prepared in Canada," not "Product of Canada" — because under federal rules, "Product of Canada" requires about 98 percent Canadian content, and Heinz's sugar is imported. Their words, via the CBC. And about ten percent of the Heinz sold here in 2024 was American-made overflow stock, labelled "Product of USA" — two different flags on the same brand, in the same aisle, and only the fine print tells you which one you grabbed. So here's the checklist, and it's short: flip the bottle and read the origin line — "Product of Canada" means ~98 percent Canadian; "Made in Canada from domestic and imported ingredients" can mean almost any mix; "Prepared in Canada" means processed here; and "Product of USA" means exactly what it says, even on a brand wrapped in a Blue Jays label. Read the sweetener word — sugar, liquid sugar, or glucose-fructose. Remember every mainstream bottle is four grams of sugars a tablespoon, so pay for tomatoes and jobs, not for the front panel. And if you want the one bottle where every layer is Canadian, you now know its name. Tell us in the comments which side your family took in 2016 — and whether tonight's bottle survives this video. If someone you know still tells the French's story at barbecues, send them this; the ending changed while nobody was looking. And we'll see you at the next label.
