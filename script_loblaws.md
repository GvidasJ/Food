# SCRIPT: LOBLAWS JUST GOT CAUGHT

Loblaws just got caught. And every Canadian already knew something was wrong.

Not once. Not in one aisle. Across bread, meat, real estate, loyalty points and the labels on the shelf, in a pattern documented by an admission the company itself published, a criminal conviction in an Ontario courtroom, the largest price-fixing fine in Canadian history, the largest antitrust settlement in Canadian history, two federal regulators, and Statistics Canada. That's what makes this story different from every angry grocery video you've ever seen. Almost nothing in it is an accusation. Most of it is on the record, and a lot of it is in Loblaw's own words.

And the thing that makes it land, the thing that had a million Canadians in a boycott subreddit and cheques quietly arriving in mailboxes this spring, is that most of us already knew. You felt it every time the price of bread moved and every store seemed to move together. You felt it when the package got lighter but the price didn't. You felt it when your points disappeared and nobody could tell you why. The doubt was correct.

One more thing before the list, because it's the strangest part of this whole story. The empire we're about to walk through began in 1882, when a teenaged bread deliveryman in Toronto named George Weston bought his boss's bread route. He became, in the words of The Canadian Encyclopedia, Canada's biggest baker by the turn of the century. Hold that thought. Because 135 years after that bread route, the company carrying his name admitted its role in fixing the price of bread. Here is everything that is on the record.

The first thing Loblaws got caught doing is the one you already know, but almost nobody knows how it actually ended. Bread.

On December 19th, 2017, Loblaw and its parent company George Weston published a press release with a title you should read slowly: George Weston and Loblaw announce actions taken to address role in industry-wide price-fixing arrangement involving certain packaged bread products. The period they admitted to: late 2001 to March 2015. Fourteen years. Galen G. Weston said, quote, this sort of behaviour is wrong and has no place in our business or Canada's grocery industry. This should never have happened. Court filings from the Competition Bureau's investigation, as reported by CBC, alleged the industry moved bread prices in a coordinated pattern on at least fifteen separate occasions, seven cents at wholesale becoming ten cents at retail each round, and reporting at the time put the cumulative cost at roughly a dollar fifty per loaf. Those specific numbers are allegations from investigation filings, not court findings, and I want to be precise about that, because precision is exactly what this story rewards.

Here's what is a court finding. In June 2023, Canada Bread pleaded guilty in the Ontario Superior Court and was fined 50 million dollars for its role in the arrangement, for two price increases, in 2007 and 2011, arranged with its competitor Weston Foods. The Competition Bureau's own release calls it the highest price-fixing fine imposed by a Canadian court to date. The Commissioner of Competition said, quote, fixing the price of bread, a food staple of Canadian households, was a serious criminal offence. And here's the part that trips everybody up: Loblaw and George Weston were never fined at all. They admitted participation, they cooperated, and in exchange they received full immunity from prosecution. The company that told you about the crime is the company that walked away from the criminal side of it. That's not an accusation. That's how Canada's immunity program works, and they used it perfectly.

Then came the civil bill. In July 2024, Loblaw and George Weston agreed to a 500 million dollar class action settlement, the largest antitrust settlement in Canadian history according to class counsel. Read the structure of it, because it's a masterclass. 404 million in new money, plus a 96 million dollar credit for the $25 gift cards the company handed out back in 2018. Remember those cards? About 3.8 million Canadians registered. And Loblaw said out loud, in its 2017 release, that the card program was expected to be an offset against civil liability. It was designed as a down payment on the lawsuit, and six years later it was counted exactly that way. Galen Weston, in the 2024 settlement release: on behalf of the Weston group of companies, we are sorry for the price-fixing behaviour we discovered and self-reported in 2015. And this spring, the money finally moved. Starting in May 2026, Canadians who filed claims began receiving $49.11 each, or $24.11 if they took the gift card back in 2018. Twenty-five years after the conduct started, your compensation for fourteen admitted years of bread pricing was one number: forty-nine dollars and eleven cents.

And the case isn't over. The class actions continue against Canada Bread, Sobeys, Metro, Walmart Canada and Giant Tiger, who all deny everything. Metro says in court filings it has been, quote, falsely painted as involved in a criminal price-fixing conspiracy when it was not, and accuses Loblaw of trying to spread the blame across the industry. Loblaw's official reply called Metro's allegations, quote, simply ridiculous and utterly untrue, as will be made clear in court. Canada's two biggest grocers are calling each other liars in court filings, under oath, right now. The Competition Bureau's criminal investigation into the other companies is still open, nine years in, with no charges laid against any of them. Every one of them is presumed innocent. The only two outcomes so far: one immunity deal, one guilty plea.

The second thing Loblaws got caught doing is the meat.

A CBC News investigation published in early 2025 found that Loblaw had sold underweighted meat, packages where the weight of the packaging was effectively included in the priced weight, contrary to federal net-weight rules, at as many as 80 stores in Western Canada, over a period that ended in December 2023. CBC's own store tests found affected prices inflated by roughly four to eleven percent. Loblaw's response, verbatim: unfortunately, the error happened as we were converting our meat trays to a more environmentally friendly format and was corrected when discovered. We apologize for these errors. The company offered a discount on select meat products in the affected stores. And then, in 2025, CBC went back and found underweighted meat again, this time at both Loblaw and Sobeys-affiliated stores. A proposed class action about underweight meat was filed in Manitoba in July 2025. It is not certified, its allegations are unproven, and Loblaw is entitled to its defence. But you don't need the lawsuit for the part that's already established, because the company said it in its own statement: the scale said one thing, the tray weighed something too, and you paid for both.

The third thing Loblaws got caught doing, you've never seen, because it's written into land you'll never look at.

In June 2023, the Competition Bureau published a market study with a title that is itself a finding: Canada Needs More Grocery Competition. Verbatim, from the federal competition regulator: Canada's grocery industry is concentrated. Most Canadians buy groceries in stores owned by a handful of grocery giants. In 2022, Canada's three largest grocers, Loblaws, Sobeys and Metro, collectively reported more than 100 billion dollars in sales and earned more than 3.6 billion dollars in profits. And this sentence, which deserves to be on a billboard: a one percentage point increase in gross margins at grocery stores could add over one billion dollars to Canadians' food bills each year. One of the study's four recommendations was about something called property controls, clauses written into leases and land titles that restrict competing grocery stores from opening nearby, sometimes long after the original store is gone. The Bureau said these controls can make it difficult, or even impossible, for businesses to open new stores, and recommended limiting them, including, if warranted, banning their use.

In June 2024, the Bureau went to Federal Court and got orders compelling George Weston and Empire, Sobeys' parent, to hand over records for its property-controls investigation. The Bureau's own release says, and this line is mandatory: there is no conclusion of wrongdoing at this time. Loblaw's defence, verbatim: restrictive covenants are very common in many industries, including retail. They help support property development investments. And then, one year later, June 2025, Loblaw announced it would end property controls, removing restrictive covenants from land titles or declining to enforce them, entering no new ones, and waiving exclusivity clauses in Halifax and in communities where it runs the only grocery store. The Commissioner of Competition welcomed it: the steps Loblaw is taking towards eliminating property controls will allow for healthier competition in the grocery sector. Draw your own conclusion about why a company voluntarily dismantles something it defended a year earlier. The record only says both things happened, in that order.

Quick pause, because the second half of this list is where it gets personal: the loyalty program a federal regulator just ruled on, the shrinking packages Statistics Canada counted, and the maple leaf labels that earned real fines this year. If this kind of receipts-only investigation is useful to you, the like and subscribe buttons are how it reaches the next Canadian pushing a cart through a store they already don't trust. Back to the list.

The fourth thing Loblaws got caught doing is fighting the referee, then hugging him.

Canada spent years building a Grocery Code of Conduct, a rulebook for how giant grocers treat suppliers. In December 2023, Loblaw wouldn't sign it. Galen Weston told a House of Commons committee the code as drafted could raise prices, citing Australia's experience, and food industry groups, along with the independent reviewer of Australia's own grocery code, publicly called that characterization inaccurate. Loblaw's spokesperson said, quote, we remain aligned to a code of conduct, but will not sign one that is not in the best interest of our customers. By February 2024, a parliamentary committee told Loblaw and Walmart to sign or face legislation. And on May 16th, 2024, exactly two weeks into the biggest consumer boycott in modern Canadian history, Loblaw announced it was ready to sign. The CEO, Per Bank, said, quote, the code now is fair, and it will not lead to higher prices. He also said the decision had nothing to do with the boycott, and that's on the record too, so you can hold both sentences up to the light yourself. The code took effect in June 2025, and full enforcement began this past January.

The fifth thing Loblaws got caught doing, Statistics Canada caught the whole industry doing, and put a number on it.

Shrinkflation isn't a feeling. The federal statistics agency defines it, tracks it, and published this: from 2021 to 2023, 29.6 percent of eligible grocery items tracked in the Consumer Price Index experienced shrinkflation, a smaller quantity sold at the price of the previously larger size. Nearly half of those instances happened in 2022, the year grocery inflation hit a 41-year high of 9.8 percent. On the Loblaw shelf specifically, CBC documented a dozen President's Choice products with reduced sizes on the company's website in a single month of 2024, instant oatmeal that lost two pouches per box, hot chocolate mix fifty grams lighter. Those examples are journalism and consumer documentation, not a regulator's ruling, and no rule requires a company to announce a smaller package. Which is precisely the point. It's legal, it's measured, and three in ten grocery items did it in two years.

The sixth thing Loblaws got caught doing has a price tag on the public record: ten thousand dollars, twice.

Through the Buy Canadian era, Loblaw covered its shelves with maple leaf tags, and in March 2025 introduced a T symbol marking products hit by tariffs. Shoppers documented complaints that the maple leaves and T symbols didn't always match the products, and that's consumer documentation, not a finding. But this part is a finding: in early 2026, the Canadian Food Inspection Agency fined two Loblaw-owned Ontario stores 10,000 dollars each for displaying imported food under Canadian labels, including a Fortinos in Etobicoke where French-made Président cheese sat under a maple-leaf tag, part of 47,000 dollars in so-called maple washing penalties the CFIA has issued across five businesses since April 2025. Administrative penalties, not criminal charges. But at the exact moment Canadians were paying premiums to buy Canadian, the federal food regulator fined the country's biggest grocer, twice, for labels that said Canada on food that wasn't.

The seventh thing Loblaws got caught doing lives in your wallet, next to your debit card.

PC Optimum. More than 17 million active members, over a billion dollars in points redeemed in a single year, by Loblaw's own disclosure. In March 2026, the Privacy Commissioner of Canada published the findings of a formal investigation into how PC Optimum handles your data when you try to leave. The findings, in the regulator's language: Loblaw failed to respond to all inquiries, contravening Principle 4.10 of the federal privacy law, well-founded and resolved. And on data retention: Loblaw is retaining personal information no longer needed, and did not demonstrate that the information it retains is effectively anonymized, well-founded and conditionally resolved. Loblaw disagreed with the anonymization finding and committed to an independent third-party assessment within twelve months. Alongside the ruling sits the consumer record: CBC's Go Public documented a member locked out of an account holding roughly 43,000 dollars worth of points, in CBC's words, without warning or explanation, and dozens more reporting frozen points. The card that watches everything you buy just got a federal ruling about what happens when you ask it to stop watching.

Now put the list next to the money, because the two halves of this story were happening at the same time.

In March 2023, Galen Weston sat in front of a House of Commons committee and said, quote, on a customer's $25 grocery basket, we earn just one dollar in profit. And, quote, no matter how many times you read it on Twitter, the idea that grocers are causing food inflation is not only false, it's impossible. The NDP's Jagmeet Singh fired back with his own math, telling Weston, quote, your company is making one million dollars a day in excess profits. That was Singh's characterization, and Weston rejected it. The economists split the same way: Dalhousie's food lab said it failed to see any evidence of profiteering, while economist Jim Stanford's analysis of Statistics Canada data found food retail profit margins had more than doubled compared with pre-pandemic norms. The Competition Bureau, for its part, chose careful words: margins increased over five years by a modest yet meaningful amount. Nobody has ruled greed illegal, and this channel isn't going to pretend otherwise. Here's what the audited filings say, and you can weigh them yourself. Loblaw's revenue: 56.5 billion in 2022, 63.9 billion in 2025. Profit available to shareholders: roughly 1.9 billion in 2022, almost 2.7 billion in 2025. Retail gross margin: 31 percent, then 31.3, touching 32 in mid-2024. In 2022, the year food inflation hit its 41-year high, the boards of Loblaw and George Weston hired a compensation consultant who concluded Galen Weston was underpaid, and his combined pay rose about 1.2 million dollars, to 11.79 million.

The pattern shows up in the small gestures too. In October 2022, Loblaw announced it was hitting the brakes on food inflation by freezing prices on 1,500 No Name products until the end of January. Experts quoted by CBC called it a PR strategy, noting No Name items had already seen some of the biggest increases, and Metro pointed out, in its own words, that an industry-wide price freeze from November 1st to February 5th is standard practice every single year. A freeze during the season prices are always frozen. That's attributed analysis, not a ruling, but the receipts are public. And in January 2024, Loblaw quietly ended the 50-percent-off stickers on food nearing its best-before date, met a wall of public anger, and reversed within days: we've listened to the feedback from our customers and colleagues and are reverting.

And in May 2024, Canadians did the only thing left. A subreddit called Loblaws Is Out Of Control, started by an Ontario mental health worker named Emily Johnson, organized a boycott of every Loblaw banner. Fifty-six thousand members on launch day, eighty-four thousand by the end of the month. A national poll found 58 percent of Canadians supported it. Galen Weston called the criticism misguided. And on the next earnings call, executives never said the word boycott, but the chief financial officer allowed, quote, we did notice a bit of an impact in certain stores in specific markets, and food same-store sales growth that quarter was 0.2 percent, versus 6.1 percent a year earlier. The CEO called the impact minor. Two weeks into that boycott is when the company decided the Code of Conduct was fair after all. The company says those two events are unrelated.

So how did one company end up this big? Go back to the bread route.

George Weston, the teenaged deliveryman of 1882, built the Model Bakery, then the biggest baking company in the country. His son Garfield started buying shares in a grocery chain in 1947, a chain founded in 1919 by Theodore Loblaw, an orphan from Elmgrove, Ontario who invented the self-serve grocery store in Canada, the groceteria, where you picked up the food yourself instead of handing a clerk your list. Loblaw died in 1933. There has been no Loblaw in Loblaws for over ninety years. By 1953 the Westons controlled it, and the family has run it for four generations, George to Garfield to Galen to Galen G. In 1978 came No Name, sixteen yellow-and-black products launched into a price war; in 1984, President's Choice; in 1988 the Decadent cookie, 39 percent chocolate chips, Canada's best-selling cookie within three years while sold in only a fifth of the country's food stores. The man behind those brands, Dave Nichol, sold Canadians a beautiful idea: the store's own label as the little guy's revenge on Big Food. Today the empire that idea built runs more than 2,400 stores, Real Canadian Superstore, No Frills, Zehrs, Fortinos, Provigo and Maxi in Quebec, Dominion in Newfoundland, T&T, plus 1,300 Shoppers Drug Mart locations bought for 12.4 billion dollars, plus a bank, plus the points card. Loblaw's own website says 90 percent of Canadians live within 10 kilometres of one of its locations. The Competition Bureau found that of Canadians who shop for groceries every week, 49 percent shop at a Loblaw banner. You may not shop at Loblaws. You almost certainly shop at Loblaw.

And the bread? In 2021, George Weston Limited sold the entire bakery business, Wonder, D'Italiano, ACE, all of it, for about 1.47 billion dollars. The company that began with one man's bread route no longer bakes bread at all. It just sells it to you.

And if you think this is all history, check the date on your receipt. On August 26th of this year, as Canada's counter-tariffs against the United States were about to hit, Loblaw announced it was reinstating the T symbol, with the CEO promising that where tariffs increase costs, any increase on the shelf will reflect that impact, penny for penny. His words. On September 1st, the company announced 1.2 billion dollars in new spending for the rest of 2026, most of it aimed at opening more No Frills and Maxi discount stores, because, in Per Bank's phrasing, Canadians are being thoughtful about every dollar they spend. Two days later, Atlantic Superstore cut prices on four thousand products and put out a press release about it. The biggest grocer in the country is spending a billion dollars to look like the affordable option, at the exact moment its own settlement cheques for the bread years are still landing in Canadian mailboxes. Both of those things are true at once. That's the whole story of this company.

So here is what got caught, all of it at once. Fourteen admitted years of industry-wide bread price-fixing, an immunity deal, a 50 million dollar criminal fine paid by a co-conspirator, a 500 million dollar settlement, and cheques for $49.11 arriving this spring. Underweighted meat at up to 80 stores, apologized for in writing, then found again. A federal study saying Canada needs more grocery competition and a property-controls investigation that ended with Loblaw dismantling the clauses it had defended. A conduct code refused in December, signed two weeks into a boycott, in force today. Shrinkflation on three of every ten tracked grocery items, counted by Statistics Canada. Two 10,000 dollar federal fines for maple leaves on imported food. And a Privacy Commissioner ruling that the loyalty program kept personal information it no longer needed. Sobeys, Metro, Walmart and Giant Tiger deny the bread allegations against them, no charges have ever been laid against them, and the civil case continues. Every allegation in this video is labelled as an allegation. Everything else came from a courtroom, a regulator, a federal agency, or Loblaw itself.

Everyone already knew something was wrong. The doubt about the bread was correct, and it took a $25 gift card, a guilty plea and half a billion dollars to prove it. The doubt about the meat was correct, in the company's own apology. The doubt about the shrinking boxes was correct, to a decimal place, in a federal database.
