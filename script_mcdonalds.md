# SCRIPT — McDONALD'S CANADA EPISODE (format test 2: documented commentary)

TITLE: Something Is Seriously Wrong at McDonald's... Canada Is Starting to Notice

ALTERNATES:
- Something Is Wrong at McDonald's Canada (Their Own CEO Said It First)
- McDonald's Froze Its Prices in Canada (Here's What They Noticed First)

---

That was a real McDonald's commercial, exactly as it aired on Ontario television in 1984. A staffed, bookable birthday party, at the restaurant, with the cake and the games included. If you're over fifty, there's a decent chance you were at one. Hold that picture in your mind, because this whole video is about the distance between that restaurant and the one at the end of your street today — and about the fact that the distance is now showing up in places McDonald's cannot control: consumer surveys, industry reports, and the company's own earnings calls.

Here's where we start, and it's not with an opinion. In May 2025, the chief executive of McDonald's, Chris Kempczinski, told investors that fast-food traffic from lower-income customers was down nearly double digits — and that, unlike a few months earlier, middle-income traffic had fallen nearly as much. A clear indication, he said, that the economic pressure had broadened. On that same call, he told investors the company had seen an uptick in unfavourable sentiment toward American brands, an eight-to-ten point shift, most pronounced in two places on earth: Northern Europe — and Canada. He was careful to add that it hadn't changed how people feel about McDonald's itself, and we'll be just as careful to repeat that. But sit with the sentence. The CEO of McDonald's, on the record, naming Canada.

Everything in this video comes from a document: an earnings call, a press release, a Statistics Canada table, a court of public record — and one retired railway caboose on Prince Edward Island. And stay to the end, because this is not a takedown. The last section is what McDonald's is actually doing about all of it — including something it hadn't done in over a decade. We read every document. That's all we do.

Start with the price, because the price is where every Canadian noticed first.

Canada's first McDonald's opened on June 1, 1967, in Richmond, British Columbia — the first McDonald's anywhere outside the United States. The Richmond Museum records that customers lined up at two take-out windows for eighteen-cent hamburgers. For the fiftieth birthday in 2017, McDonald's Canada sold hamburgers for sixty-seven cents for one day, three per customer, and the line-ups came back. And in 2006, some of you will remember, the Globe and Mail covered the launch of the Value Picks menu: bacon cheeseburgers and Sausage McMuffins for a dollar thirty-nine.

Now the modern series, and we're using the most neutral referee that exists: The Economist has surveyed the price of a Big Mac in Canada twice a year since 2000, and publishes the raw data. April 2000: two dollars and eighty-five cents. January 2017: five ninety-eight. And July 2026, the latest edition: eight dollars and seventeen cents. Up thirty-six and a half percent since 2017, and almost triple the year-2000 price. Before anyone says this is a Canadian problem — the American Big Mac rose thirty-eight percent over the same window in their own currency; the two curves are nearly identical. The detail in the data that stings is the shape: from 2019 through early 2022, the surveyed Canadian price barely moved — six seventy-seven, edition after edition after edition. Almost the entire modern climb happened after 2022, more than twenty percent in four and a half years. And remember these are national survey figures for the sandwich alone: McDonald's restaurants are priced by their franchisees, which is why your local number may run higher, and why the viral extreme examples are usually one location, not the chain. Statistics Canada tells the wider version of the same story: restaurant food is up about forty-four percent since January 2017, while overall inflation ran thirty-one. Eating out has outrun everything else you buy by a dozen points — a ten-dollar restaurant order from January 2017 costs about fourteen dollars and forty cents at the average pace of that index today.

And Canadians said so, loudly. In 2023, Narcity reported on a Canadian discussion thread with more than eight hundred comments about McDonald's prices. One customer reported their Chicken McMuffin going from two forty-nine in January to four ninety-nine by summer. Another wrote, about a two-dollar hash brown: it's literally eaten in ten seconds and two bites. A third: just a couple years ago I was getting a Big Mac meal with the app for six dollars — now they seem to think ten dollars for the same thing is a great deal. And one line that could caption this whole era: fast food isn't cheap anymore — it's just for convenience at this point. Those are customers' reported words, not ours — and Narcity noted it asked McDonald's Canada about the increases and didn't receive a response before publication. Even McDonald's own leadership conceded ground here: in 2024 the president of McDonald's USA published an open letter acknowledging the average American menu item had risen about forty percent since 2019 — his number, about his market, but you'll recognize the shape.

Now, because this channel deals in documents and not outrage, here are two facts that cut the other way, and we're going to say them plainly. First: if you earn minimum wage in Ontario, Quebec or B.C., a Big Mac actually costs you slightly fewer minutes of work than it did in 2017, because minimum wages rose faster than the burger. The exception is Alberta, where the minimum wage has been frozen at fifteen dollars since 2018 — an Albertan on minimum wage now works about three minutes longer per Big Mac than they did then. Second: cooking at home didn't escape either. Statistics Canada's average grocery price for ground beef — the raw material of a burger — is up nearly eighty-one percent since January 2017, more than double the Big Mac's own climb. The squeeze is real everywhere. What changed at McDonald's is that "cheap" quietly stopped being the guaranteed answer.

Which brings us to what Canada noticed before the corporation said anything.

The surveys came first. An Angus Reid study for Restaurants Canada found three-quarters of Canadians dining out less because of the cost of living — eighty-one percent among the youngest adults. Leger found eighty-eight percent of us hunting the value column of the menu, and half of us doing it more than the year before. Circana and Restaurants Canada found thirty-six percent of Canadians cutting back specifically on fast food. TouchBistro's diner survey caught the same shift from the other side: takeout up, dine-in visits down thirteen percent in a year, and the share of Canadians who rarely or never eat out growing. And by this spring, Restaurants Canada reported that eighty-one percent of quick-service operators — the restaurant owners themselves — said their profitability was falling, with more than half reporting fewer customers walking in, sixty-nine percent saying customers were dining out less because of affordability, and thirty-six percent operating at a loss or break-even — triple the 2019 level. Their forecast for 2026: inflation-adjusted restaurant sales actually shrinking. The squeeze is hitting both sides of the counter, and the operators' own association says so in its own reports.

The business press had a name for what came next: the value war. The Canadian Press reported in late 2024 that the deal-stacking would run deep into 2025, quoting a Bernstein analyst predicting elevated promotions for at least six months, the CEO of Tim Hortons' parent company describing a more value-sensitive environment, and the CEO of Montreal's MTY Group — the food-court giant from our chains video — reaching for the industry's favourite ghost: remember the five-dollar footlong at Subway? It was very successful while it lasted. When every chief executive in the sector is quoting discount folklore to analysts, the customer has already won the argument about what the problem is.

Then, quarter by quarter, the corporation's language caught up. February 2024: the battleground is with the low-income consumer — the CEO's words. April 2024: the chief financial officer told analysts everybody was fighting for fewer consumers, and that McDonald's needed a street-fighting mentality — his word, street-fighting, about selling hamburgers. Summer 2024: the first negative global quarter since 2020, ending a run the company had been advertising as thirteen straight quarters of growth. Spring 2025: the worst American quarter since the pandemic, minus three point six percent, with the company's own release attributing it primarily to negative guest counts. Then a 2025 recovery the CEO summed up in February 2026 with a declaration: McDonald's value leadership is working. McDonald's is not going to get beat on value and affordability — it's in our DNA. And then, this August, the newest stumble: growth slowed hard again, and Kempczinski told investors, in one of the franker sentences you'll hear on an earnings call: we don't have a strategy problem. We simply didn't execute at the level we needed to. He called one of the company's own promotional changes — pulling digital offers customers liked — a bad trade, and the company installed a new president for its American business the same week.

And here is the phrase to learn, because it keeps appearing in the releases even in good quarters: positive check growth, partly offset by negative comparable guest counts. Translation: fewer people, paying more each. In 2025 McDonald's Corporation earned eight and a half billion dollars of net income on twenty-seven billion in revenue — nearly thirty-two cents of every revenue dollar — while its own filings kept using that phrase. Nothing about that is illegal or even unusual; ninety-five percent of its restaurants are franchised and the corporation's income is mostly rent and royalties. It is simply the arithmetic of the complaint your neighbour has been making in the drive-thru line, printed in a securities filing.

Then there's the app — the thing that was supposed to fix the relationship. Globally, loyalty is now enormous for McDonald's: the company reports around two hundred and ten million active users and tens of billions in loyalty-driven sales. McDonald's Canada launched its rewards program in November 2021, and Canadians built routines around it. Then, effective September 16, 2025, as reported by iPhone in Canada from the company's own customer email: large fries jumped from four thousand points to six thousand. Large hot drinks moved up a tier. And the original 2021 redemption list — McDoubles, McFlurries, Big Macs, nuggets, Happy Meals — was removed. The deals got smaller in the same years the prices got bigger, and the most loyal customers, the ones with the app, were precisely the ones positioned to notice — a loyalty program's most engaged member is also its most precise measuring instrument. Even inside the company family, the value push has caused documented friction: CNBC reported this February that a long-running survey of American franchisees rated their relationship with corporate at 1.37 out of 5, and that an independent operator group had adopted what it called a Franchisee Bill of Rights, including the ability to set prices without fear of recourse. Those are the franchisees' reported positions in a business dispute — nobody's villain, everybody's margin. The physical experience moved the same direction. Dollar Drink Days, the beloved any-size summer promotion, now covers small drinks. The self-serve fountain is disappearing behind the counter — CTV covered the shift in 2023, with the company's American timeline running to 2032. The kiosks and table service arrived in 2016 and 2017 under a corporate program literally named Experience of the Future. And all-day breakfast — launched across Canada with fanfare on February 21, 2017 — was pulled in the spring of 2020 to simplify operations, and never came back.

While we're being precise about what left, let's correct the internet's version of two Canadian memories, because your memories deserve accurate obituaries. The McPizza: launched across Canada around 1992, with Howie Mandel in the commercials — the ad survives, and we'll show it. It reportedly took about eleven minutes to prepare, and by January 2000 it was pulled across Canada because, per the Canadian Press, it simply took too long to make. The famous "last two McDonald's still serving pizza" that made headlines in 2017? Not Canadian — Pomeroy, Ohio and Spencer, West Virginia. A Canadian who wanted one final slice had to drive to Appalachia. And the orange drink — the one from the cooler jug at every Brownies meeting and sports day, the one Canadians remember leaving orange mustaches — faded off Canadian menus quietly around 2009. The loud "orange drink cancelled" story you may remember from 2017 was American; that was their Hi-C. Ours didn't even get headlines. This year, McDonald's Canada added a drink called Orange Dream to its permanent lineup — an orange-cream soda the reviews call creamsicle-inspired. They are, quite literally, selling the memory back. We note it without judgment. Mostly.

The rest of the ledger, quickly, because your comments will fill in whatever we miss. The fried apple pie gave way to the baked one — Daily Hive's dated list of discontinued Canadian items leads with it. The Arctic Orange Shake. Cinnamon Melts, gone around 2015. The McHotDog, which Canadians could genuinely buy at the SkyDome and the Toronto zoo until 1999. And the callbacks keep coming: Pizza Bites appeared at participating Canadian locations in June 2025 — a snack-sized wink at the McPizza — and in August 2024 the collector cups came back, styled after the Beanie Baby and Shrek collectibles of your kids' childhoods, promptly reselling online for multiples of their price. The nostalgia economy is real, and McDonald's is both its museum and its gift shop.

The McLobster deserves its own line in the ledger, because it's the rare discontinuation with an official cause of death. In 2013 the company proudly expanded the Atlantic lobster roll. In June 2017, a McDonald's spokesperson told CBC, on the record: the increased price of lobster per pound means we are currently unable to offer this menu item at a reasonable price for our guests. Input costs killed it, and the company said so. And the PlayPlaces — no memo ever killed those. They disappeared one renovation at a time, and to be fair, some new Canadian locations still build them; Sault Ste. Marie got one in 2024.

But if you want the whole story in a single object, it's parked at a railway museum in Elmira, Prince Edward Island. A caboose, built in 1912, that ran for Grand Trunk and then Canadian National. In the 1980s, McDonald's in Charlottetown bought it, painted it up, and made it the birthday party room — a real train, in the parking lot, for the kids. In 1997 the restaurant expanded and the caboose was donated away. CBC covered its restoration. Every removed party room, every vanished cooler jug, every play structure that didn't survive the renovation — it's all standing in that one caboose, lovingly preserved, by somebody other than McDonald's.

Now the balance, and take it as seriously as everything above, because the company's response is genuinely substantial. In December 2024, McDonald's Canada rebuilt its budget menu as McValue. Then, on January 13, 2026, it did something the coverage described as unprecedented in recent memory: a one-year price promise. McValue meals down to five dollars — breakfast included — and the small coffee locked at one dollar for the entire year, no minimum purchase. The new president and CEO of McDonald's Canada, Annemarie Swijtink — who started at the company as a fifteen-year-old crew member in the Netherlands — said it directly: Canadians are facing challenges and are insecure financially. What we are doing is listening and giving them what they want. Read that quote twice. It's the company's own confirmation of everything in the first half of this video. Food economist Sylvain Charlebois called the freeze a defensive economic move, and noted that when McDonald's moves, the entire sector adjusts — A&W had launched its own under-four-dollar value menu eight days earlier. And here's the counter-fact that makes the one-dollar coffee genuinely remarkable: Statistics Canada's average price for a bag of grocery coffee has more than doubled since 2017. Whatever else is wrong, a dollar coffee in 2026 is a real concession.

There's more on the credit side of the ledger. The burger upgrades Americans got as "Best Burger" in 2024? Canada quietly implemented its version back in 2019. The executive McDonald's hired in 2025 to rescue its American marketing, Alyssa Buetikofer, came from the Canadian operation — the one that launched the loyalty program and the Chicken Big Mac, reportedly Canada's most successful limited-time offer ever. CosMc's, the futuristic beverage spinoff the company launched in late 2023, was gone by June 2025 — about eighteen months, never reaching Canada — but its drinks are being folded into regular menus, which is how Orange Dream reached you. Poutine went national on Canadian menus in 2013 at three ninety-nine, and it's still ours alone. And McHappy Day — this deserves saying clearly — was invented in Canada, in 1977, by George Cohon, and went worldwide from here. The first Canadian Ronald McDonald House opened on Dundas Street in Toronto in April 1981 with nineteen bedrooms, after McDonald's of Canada flew Toronto hospital parents down to see the American original; the 2011 rebuild made Toronto's the largest such House in the world at the time. The 2025 McHappy Day raised over eleven million dollars, bringing the Canadian all-time total past a hundred and eleven million for children's charities. And a leadership note your daughters and granddaughters might appreciate: the executive who ran McDonald's Canada until this September, Michèle Boudria, started on crew in Quebec in 1984 and became the first woman ever to lead the company here. The company also says its beef has been one hundred percent Canadian since 2003, primarily Alberta and Saskatchewan — its claim, with one pandemic-era interruption in 2020 that it announced and reversed publicly.

A word on scale before the ending, because the institution being discussed is still enormous here: by its own releases, McDonald's Canada runs roughly fourteen and a half to fifteen hundred restaurants, over ninety percent of them locally owned by franchisees, employing close to a hundred thousand Canadians, buying most of its food from Canadian suppliers, serving on the order of a million or two guests a day depending on which company release you read — the two most recent ones give different numbers, which tells you something about how fast the story is moving. This is not a chain in collapse. It is a chain whose own documents describe a strained relationship with the people in the drive-thru line — which is a different, quieter kind of serious.

So let's end where McDonald's Canada actually began — with two men. George Tidball, the Western entrepreneur who put down fifty thousand dollars for the rights, opened Richmond in 1967, built thirty-two stores, and later founded The Keg — if you watched our restaurant chains video, that's the same Tidball, and Canada's fast-food history keeps being written by about a dozen people. And George Cohon, the Chicago lawyer who borrowed seventy thousand dollars for the East, opened London, Ontario in 1968, became a Canadian citizen in 1977, and then spent fourteen years dragging McDonald's through the Iron Curtain. When the Moscow location opened in January 1990 and thirty thousand people lined up in Pushkin Square — a company record for an opening day — that operation ran through McDonald's Canada. The man wrote a book called To Russia with Fries. He died in November 2023, at eighty-six, with the Order of Canada. And in 2017, when the original Richmond restaurant was rebuilt for the fiftieth anniversary, workers found the original 1967 red-and-white floor tiles in the demolition — and preserved them in the entrance, with the old golden arches sign designated heritage by the city. Even McDonald's is nostalgic for McDonald's.

Here's the thesis, one last time, in documents. Canada was the first country outside America that McDonald's ever trusted. Fifty-nine years later, the company's own CEO named Canada as one of the two places on earth where sentiment had shifted most — and eleven months after that, his Canadian company froze its prices for an entire year because, in its own president's words, Canadians are insecure financially and the company is listening. Nobody freezes prices for a country that isn't noticing.

So tell us in the comments — and be honest, because this is the real measurement the surveys can't reach: what's the McDonald's memory you'd pay full price to have back? The birthday caboose? The orange drink mustache? The McPizza delivered to your table on a rack? And if you were at one of those 1984 birthday parties, tell us where. We read every document. That's all we do. See you next time.
