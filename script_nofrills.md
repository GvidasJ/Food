On October 2nd, Loblaw posted an update to a notice about customer accounts. It said information accessed in an incident earlier this year included names, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, dates of birth, loyalty account numbers, certain purchase order details and incomplete payment card information. A month earlier, Loblaw announced it now expects to open about seventy-five new locations in 2026, focused predominantly on No Frills and Maxi. And in North York, a Whole Foods that closed last year has just reopened as a No Frills. So here's the strange part. Loblaw says more Canadians are choosing No Frills. Scroll the comments under any No Frills video and you'll read people saying they're done with it. Both can't be the whole story. So we went through Loblaw's own filings, its own website data, the federal rules and the news record, to find out what's actually going on. Because the truth is not always on the menu.

Here's what this video is. Nine things you should know before you shop at No Frills again, counted down from nine to one. Then three things No Frills genuinely gets right, because there are some. And along the way, the one sign at the front of most grocery stores that most shoppers walk straight past.

First, the honest bit, because the title needs it. Loblaw's own reports do not show Canadians leaving No Frills. In its second quarter report this July, Loblaw wrote that its discount banners once again outperformed, reflecting continued consumer demand for value and greater access to Maxi and No Frills stores. The same report said Food Retail sales were supported by higher customer traffic and basket size. Loblaw doesn't publish No Frills sales on their own, so nobody outside the company can measure the banner by itself. What the record does show is real frustration. In May 2024, a Reddit group called Loblaws is out of control organised a month-long boycott of Loblaw-owned stores, including No Frills. CBC reported the group had about sixty-two thousand members when it started. In that quarter, Loblaw's food same-store sales growth was zero point two percent, against six point one percent a year earlier. Loblaw's report didn't use the word boycott, and CEO Per Bank told analysts the overall financial impact was minor. We're not linking those numbers to the boycott. We're just giving you both. So some Canadians are refusing, and others are walking in for the first time. This video is about what's pushing people away, checked against the record. Every claim gets a label: a finding, an allegation, a settlement, an admission, or a company statement. Canadian Counter has no commercial relationship with No Frills, Loblaw or any retailer in this video, and nobody reviewed it before release. Let's get into it.

Number nine. The name on the door. Walk up to a No Frills and the sign often has a first name on it. Bo's No Frills. Nick's No Frills. That's not decoration. Loblaw's latest annual information form lists No Frills with zero corporate stores and three hundred and thirty-six franchised stores, as of January 3rd, 2026. In Loblaw's own words, franchisees are independent operators. Its careers page calls them Owner-Operators, and says the job requires a fifty-thousand-dollar personal investment, plus a business-supported loan for store setup. So what does that change for you? On shelf prices, less than you might think. On October 3rd, we checked the same fifteen products on Loblaw's own website at three No Frills stores, in Toronto, Calgary and Vancouver. Twelve of the fifteen had the identical price at all three, sale prices included. The only gaps were eggs, bananas and one beef item. Where stores do differ is in the services around the shelf. And in where they are at all. Of the three hundred and fifty-one No Frills locations in Loblaw's pickup data, two hundred and twelve are in Ontario, and none are in Quebec, where Loblaw's discount banner is Maxi. In Loblaw's own pickup data, one hundred and forty-three of three hundred and fifty-two No Frills locations say PC Express pickup is unavailable at that location. Loblaw says the model gives owners local flexibility, and some shoppers like knowing whose name is on the building. The fix is simple. Treat each No Frills as its own store. Check that store's page for what it offers before you plan your trip around it.

Number eight. The multi-buy is gone, but the limit isn't. On July 3rd, 2024, No Frills announced that stores across the country had removed the multi-buy promotion. No more buy two for five dollars or one for two ninety-nine. The banner's president, Melanie Singh, said customers told them loud and clear they don't want to have to buy more to save. That change was real, and it still shows. In a sample of about six hundred products per store in Loblaw's own online data on October 3rd, three of four No Frills stores showed no multi-buy badges at all. The Loblaws in Markham showed two hundred and eight, and the Real Canadian Superstore in Mississauga showed ninety-one. But here's what replaced it on some deals. At No Frills stores in Toronto, Calgary and Vancouver that day, Kraft White Cheddar Macaroni and Cheese, one hundred and seventy-five grams, was a dollar on special until October 7th. The badge said: Limit 6, after limit $2.00. So the first six cost a dollar each, about fifty-seven cents per hundred grams. The seventh rings at two dollars. That's a normal, disclosed retail tactic, and No Frills prints it. It just isn't the same thing as every item at one simple price. The fix: when you see a big red sale price, read the line under it before you load the cart.

Number seven. Online No Frills plays by different rules. A lot of shoppers assume the app is just the store on a screen. Loblaw's own wording is more careful. In a July 2026 statement, Loblaw said prices may vary by store, region, banner, promotion, flyer, online channel, clearance item, or supplier cost changes. Then there are the store notes. At sixty-one No Frills locations, Loblaw's pickup data carries the line: No coupons or price matching for PC Express. So whatever you'd get matched at the till, you won't get matched on a pickup order there. The help pages add two more details. PC Express Pass gives unlimited free pickup and delivery on same-day or next-day orders of thirty dollars or more. And when you place an order, No Frills places a temporary hold on your card for one hundred and twenty-five percent of the estimated total. Its own page says a bank can take up to ten business days to release that hold. All of that is on No Frills' own pages. But a hold of a hundred and twenty-five dollars on a hundred-dollar order matters if you're watching your balance to the dollar. If something goes wrong with an online order, No Frills says Report an Issue is available within two hours after you receive it, and that refund requests are typically processed within seventy-two hours. The fix: if you price-match, shop in person. If you order online, leave room on your card for the hold.

Number six. The points that don't wait for you. PC Optimum is free to join, and its own site says ten thousand points equals ten dollars of redeemable value, and that points can also be donated to charity. But at the grocery store, the site lists no base points per dollar for a plain card. You earn mainly through personalised offers and on-shelf promotions. No Frills' help page says offers have an expiry date, typically around seven days from the day they become available. And it says, if you forget to load your offers one week, you will not be able to earn the points associated with those offers once they expire. So a busy week can cost you points, not because of anything at the till, but because you didn't open an app. Loblaw's 2025 annual report says the program has over eighteen million active members, so that's a lot of weekly reminders. There's been other churn too. Loblaw sold PC Financial to EQ Bank, and EQ Bank says the deal was completed on July 1st, 2026. It also says there will be no changes to the PC Optimum program as a direct result. The Insiders subscription page now says new subscriptions are closed. And Loblaw says offers can differ between members, but the shelf price does not. Its words: the price on the shelf is the price everyone pays for that item at that store. The fix: set a weekly reminder and load your offers before you leave the house.

Number five. The email you might get this month. Back to that account notice. On March 10th, Loblaw said an unauthorized third party accessed some basic customer details, including names, phone numbers, and email addresses. At the time it said passwords, health information and credit card data were not compromised. The update on October 2nd added more detail. It listed name, address, phone number, email address, dates of birth, loyalty account number, certain purchase order details and incomplete payment card information. And it said, in the coming weeks we will begin notifying impacted customers directly. That's a company statement about its own incident. We're not adding to it, and we don't know how many accounts are involved, because the update we read doesn't give a number. What it means for you is practical. When a real notice is on its way, copycat messages often follow. The fix: don't click links in an unexpected Loblaw or PC Optimum email. Open the app or type the website yourself, and change your password if you reused it anywhere else.

Number four. The maple leaf on the sign versus the line on the box. In July 2025, CBC News reported that at a Loblaw-owned No Frills in Toronto, strawberries were displayed with signage that included a red maple leaf and the phrase Prepared in Canada. The berries' packaging said product of USA. CBC said Loblaw didn't directly answer its questions, but that shortly after it asked, the signage was gone. That's a media observation, not a regulator finding. The CFIA told CBC it would be inappropriate to use a Made in Canada or Prepared in Canada claim if a product was only packaged here. Here's why the wording matters. Loblaw itself said in March 2025 that it was marking products Prepared in Canada with a distinctive red maple leaf. But Prepared in Canada isn't one of the CFIA's defined claims. Under CFIA guidance, Product of Canada means all or virtually all major ingredients, processing and labour are Canadian. Made in Canada needs a qualifier, like Made in Canada from imported ingredients. And the CFIA has issued penalties. In January 2026 it fined two Loblaw-owned stores ten thousand dollars each over displays that made imported food look Canadian. To be fair to No Frills, those two stores were a Real Canadian Superstore and a Fortinos, not a No Frills. The CFIA told CBC that between November 2024 and February 2026, it found seventy-eight country-of-origin violations at retail stores, across all retailers. CBC also reported that current CFIA fines max out at fifteen thousand dollars. Then on August 26th, 2026, Loblaw said it is reintroducing the country-of-origin in our fresh produce aisles, and bringing back a T symbol for products sourced directly from the U.S. that are affected by tariffs. Credit for that. The fix: the shelf sign is the store talking. The line on the package is the label the CFIA's origin rules apply to. Read the package.

Number three. What the package promises, and what Canada actually checks. Pick up a four-hundred-gram bag of something. Does it have to contain four hundred grams? Not exactly. Under the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations, each package gets a small tolerance, and a batch has to meet the declared amount on average. For a four-hundred-gram package, the tolerance is three percent, which is twelve grams. One pack a little under is allowed. Too many short packs, or a batch that averages under the label, is not. The Competition Bureau calls this the Average System. Then there's what changes when the package itself changes. Statistics Canada published a study in February 2025 on shrinking products. It said that from 2021 to 2023, twenty-nine point six percent of eligible grocery items tracked in the CPI experienced shrinkflation. And it said a much higher proportion of name-brand products than house-brand products experienced it, seventy-seven point six percent against twenty-two point four. Loose food and the deli counter are a different system. Those go by Measurement Canada's rules, and its complaints page says sellers must not include the weight of the container in the weight of the food. Supermarket scales face a mandatory examination every five years. The tool that catches all of this is the unit price. But outside Quebec, unit pricing isn't required by law, and No Frills has no Quebec stores. Manitoba announced in June it will adopt unit pricing, and a federal private member's bill is in committee. On meat, one sentence on the record. In January 2025, CBC reported finding underweight meat packages at a Loblaw-owned No Frills in Calgary, and Loblaw said that in a small number of Western stores in 2023, we made a mistake. That's a media finding and a company admission, not a regulator ruling. The fix: compare price per hundred grams, and if the units on two tags don't match, do the division yourself.

One thing before the last two. If you want the next Canadian grocery name checked against the record like this, take a second to subscribe. Canadian Counter reads the fine print so you don't have to.

Number two. The price tag that can change itself. Loblaw started introducing electronic shelf labels in 2018. This year, a debate over surveillance pricing reached Ontario. That's the idea of using personal data to charge different people different prices. Manitoba passed a law that names electronic shelf labels and makes demanding a higher price because of personalized algorithmic pricing an unfair practice. In April, Premier Doug Ford rejected a similar ban for Ontario. In August, Global News reported, based on freedom of information records, that Ford met Loblaw's CEO at his home the day after. Global said the calendar doesn't reveal the subject of the conversation. Loblaw said it is already on the record as not using surveillance pricing. Loblaw also told The Logic: we do not use, nor do we plan to use, algorithmic pricing to set or increase prices in our stores, including on our electronic shelf labels. And in another statement: in any particular store, pricing will not vary by customer, time of day, or demand, and everyone in a store pays the same price for the same product. Here's where we stand. There's no verified evidence of personalised shelf pricing at No Frills, and we're not going to claim it. The Competition Bureau has opened an examination of pricing algorithms, loyalty programs and shrinkflation, with a report expected in spring 2027. No findings yet. The fix: snap a photo of the shelf tag on anything big, and compare it to your receipt.

Number one. The discount store wasn't the cheapest store in our basket. On October 3rd, we priced fifteen identical products, same product codes, on Loblaw's own website at seven Loblaw-owned stores. At Bo's No Frills on Richmond Street West in Toronto, the basket came to seventy-three dollars and twenty-four cents. At the Loblaws on Bullock Drive in Markham, seventy-eight fifty-one. So No Frills beat its sister Loblaws, as you'd expect. But at the Real Canadian Superstore on Argentia Road in Mississauga, the same fifteen items came to seventy dollars and ninety-seven cents. That's two dollars and twenty-seven cents less than the No Frills. Most of that gap is one item. Tide Original liquid, one point seven seven litres, was fifteen ninety-nine at that No Frills, about thirty-three cents a load. At the Superstore it was on sale for nine ninety-nine until October 7th, about twenty-one cents a load. Take Tide out, and No Frills wins by about three dollars seventy. The other way round, Cheerios Family Size, five hundred and seventy grams, was four eighty-seven on sale at No Frills, against seven forty-nine at that Superstore. Here's why it matters. The Competition Bureau's 2023 grocery study put it plainly: Loblaws owns No Frills and Maxi, Sobeys owns FreshCo, and Metro owns Food Basics and Super C. It said this is different from other countries, where large grocers compete against lower-priced options like ALDI or Lidl. The study also found grocers' food gross margins had generally increased by one or two percentage points since 2017, which it said is roughly one to two dollars on each hundred dollars Canadians spend on groceries. That finding covered grocers collectively, not Loblaw alone. In the same study, a government survey found about half of Canadians said they shop at Loblaws or a store it operates. And the bread record sits in the background. Loblaw and George Weston admitted taking part in an industry-wide bread price-fixing arrangement from 2001 to 2015, and received immunity for reporting it. They settled the consumer class actions for five hundred million dollars, and payments of forty-nine dollars and eleven cents started going out this May. For people who took the twenty-five dollar Loblaw card back in 2018, it was twenty-four dollars and eleven cents. That's an admission and a court-approved settlement. Allegations against other grocers remain allegations, and those companies deny them. And the price can follow your postal code. The same No Name Large eggs, twelve-pack, were three ninety-three at that Toronto No Frills on October 3rd, about thirty-three cents an egg. They were four eighteen at Kevin's No Frills in Calgary, four twenty-one at the No Frills on Fraser Street in Vancouver, and four ninety-eight at the Atlantic Superstore on Young Street in Halifax. The fix: the banner on the sign is not a price guarantee. Compare the flyer, not the logo.

How to protect yourself. First, compare unit prices, not shelf prices. Divide the price by the grams or millilitres yourself when the tags use different units, because outside Quebec nobody is required to do it for you. Second, watch the till. Under section fifty-four of the Competition Act, a store can't charge more than the lowest of two or more prices it clearly displays, and that includes a shelf label. And look for that sign at the front of the store: the Scanner Price Accuracy Code. Under the code, as the Competition Bureau describes it, if an item without a price tag scans higher than the shelf price, it's free when it's worth less than ten dollars. Over ten, you get ten dollars off. The Canadian Press reported that Loblaw is a signatory, and that it applies to the first unit, not to items priced by weight or to member prices. Third, load your PC Optimum offers before you go, read any limit line under a sale price, and bring a competitor's current flyer if you want an in-store price match. Fourth, read the origin line on the package, not the sign above it. If packaged food looks short, report it to the CFIA. If a scale at the deli looks off, that goes to Measurement Canada. And Measurement Canada's own advice is to try to resolve it with the store first.

None of this is about boycotting No Frills. It's about walking in with your eyes open. And it's only fair to say No Frills gets some things right.

First, the store brand price gap is real. At that Toronto No Frills on October 3rd, No Name ketchup, one litre, was three sixty-nine, against five seventy-nine for Heinz, both regular prices. That's about thirty-six percent less. No Name smooth peanut butter, one kilogram, was four fifty on special, against six fifty for Kraft. No Name frozen peas, seven hundred and fifty grams, were two seventy-seven on special, against four dollars for Green Giant. Not every time, though. On the same capture, Unico diced tomatoes, seven hundred and ninety-six millilitres, were one seventy-nine on sale, twenty-one cents under the No Name can. Check the tag, not the brand.

Second, the written guarantees. No Frills' own return policy says you can return merchandise for exchange or refund within fourteen days with the original receipt. It also offers what it calls the Try It You'll Love It Guarantee on President's Choice and No Name food. Its words: if you are not completely satisfied with the quality, return it with the receipt or the original package for an exchange or refund. That's a policy, not a quality claim from us, but it's a useful one.

Third, the near-date discounts. Loblaw and Flashfood said in March 2026 that the app saved customers more than fifty-eight million dollars on groceries in 2025, at over nine hundred Loblaw stores, including select No Frills. And in January 2024, after public backlash, Loblaw told CBC it was going back to fifty percent off stickers on items nearing their best-before date, where that had existed before. Loblaw's release says Flashfood orders are paid in the app and picked up from a Flashfood Zone inside participating stores. Check whether your store has one, and look for the yellow stickers.

That's the full picture: nine things worth knowing, three things worth using, and a store that Loblaw says is growing, even as some shoppers walk away. APPENDIX B — DO NOT SAY (on air, lower-thirds, description, pinned comment or thumbnail)
- "Canadians are leaving / abandoning No Frills", "sales are falling", "retailers are taking notice". Loblaw's filings point the other way.
- Personalised, surveillance or algorithmic pricing at No Frills as fact. NDP leader Stiles's "isn't a coincidence". Any motive for the Ford–Bank meeting.
- The CFIA penalties as No Frills penalties. They were at RCSS #1033 and Fortinos #7920.
- The PC Health drone ad as a No Frills ad.
- Franchise owners' full names (API `ownerName`). Anything about any individual franchisee.
- Price-match terms (limit 4 per item, exclusions). The page could not be read. Verify it before any mention.
- PC Express fee amounts, Pass price, Insiders price, the 2-year account closure: unverified.
- The "$1.69 MIN 3" pop at No Frills #3907, unless captured on shelf. Never as a contradiction.
- Lines our Dec 2025 video got wrong:
  - Loblaw owns FreshCo;
  - Loblaw bought Neilson;
  - PC Optimum since 2000;
  - a "15–25% higher" study;
  - the Kirkwood chicken;
  - "all No Name stores closed".

- Any health, nutrition, food-safety, additive or recall content. "Secret", "hidden", "caught", "exposed", "scam", "trick", "quietly".
- Bread: never "convicted" for Loblaw/Weston (admission + immunity). Never present the other grocers as anything but allegations, which they deny.
