On October 3rd, we looked up the same fifteen products, same product codes, at seven stores under Loblaw banners, on Loblaw's own website. The No Frills was not the cheapest. At number one, you'll see which store beat it, and the one item that made the difference. Meanwhile, Loblaw says it expects to open about seventy-five new grocery stores and pharmacies this year, with its grocery expansion focused predominantly on No Frills and Maxi. Yet under No Frills videos, you'll find people saying they're done with it. Both can't be the whole story. So we went through Loblaw's own filings, its own website data, the federal rules and the news record. Because the truth is not always on the menu.

Here's the plan. Nine things to know before you shop at No Frills again, counted down from nine to one. Then three things No Frills genuinely gets right. And one sign at the front of the store that matters if something scans higher than the shelf. One honest note first. Loblaw's own reports don't show Canadians leaving No Frills. They say its discount banners once again outperformed. So this is about why some shoppers say they're frustrated, checked against the record.

Number nine. The name on the door. Look up a No Frills on Loblaw's own website, and the store name often starts with a first name. Bo's No Frills. Nick's No Frills. That's not decoration. Loblaw's latest annual information form lists No Frills with zero corporate stores and three hundred and thirty-six franchised stores, as of January 3rd, 2026. In Loblaw's own words, franchisees are independent operators. Its careers page calls them Owner-Operators, and says the job requires a fifty-thousand-dollar personal investment, plus a business-supported loan for store setup. So what does that change for you? On the prices Loblaw's website shows, less than you might think. On October 3rd, we checked the same fifteen products on Loblaw's own website at three No Frills stores, in Toronto, Calgary and Vancouver. Twelve of the fifteen had the identical price at all three, sale prices included. The only gaps were eggs, bananas and one beef item. Take the No Name Large eggs, twelve-pack. You'd have paid three ninety-three at the Richmond Street West store in Toronto, about thirty-three cents an egg. In Calgary and Vancouver it was four eighteen and four twenty-one, both about thirty-five cents. So if you've ever felt your store charges more than your cousin's in another province, sometimes it does, and Loblaw's own site shows it. Where stores do differ is in the services around the shelf. Loblaw's pickup data lists three hundred and fifty-two No Frills locations, a live list rather than a filed store count. Two hundred and twelve are in Ontario, and none are in Quebec, where Loblaw's discount banner is Maxi. And one hundred and forty-three of them say PC Express pickup is unavailable at that location. Loblaw's careers page says owners lead their own business with local flexibility. The fix is simple. Treat each No Frills as its own store. Check that store's page for what it offers before you plan your trip around it.

Before number eight, how this works. Every key claim gets a label: a finding, an allegation, a settlement, an admission, or a company statement. Canadian Counter has no commercial relationship with No Frills, Loblaw or any retailer in this video, and none of them reviewed it. And one correction from us. Our December video said Loblaw owns FreshCo. It doesn't. Sobeys does.

Number eight. The multi-buy is gone, but the limit isn't. On July 3rd, 2024, No Frills announced that stores across the country had removed the multi-buy promotion. No more buy two for five dollars or one for two ninety-nine. The banner's president, Melanie Singh, said customers told them loud and clear they don't want to have to buy more to save. That change was real, and it still shows. In a sample of about six hundred products per store in Loblaw's own online data on October 3rd, four No Frills stores showed seven multi-buy badges between them. The Loblaws in Markham showed two hundred and eight, and the Real Canadian Superstore in Mississauga showed ninety-one. But a handful of deals, no more than three per No Frills store in that sample, carry a different condition. Take the No Frills on Richmond Street West in Toronto, on Elbow Drive in Calgary, and on Fraser Street in Vancouver. At all three that day, Kraft White Cheddar Macaroni and Cheese, one hundred and seventy-five grams, was a dollar on special until October 7th. The badge said: Limit 6, after limit $2.00. So the first six cost a dollar each, about fifty-seven cents per hundred grams. By the badge's own terms, the seventh is two dollars. The limit is spelled out right in the deal badge online. It just means the sale price covers the first six, not however many you buy. The fix: when you see a big red sale price, read the line under it before you load the cart.

Number seven. Online No Frills plays by different rules. A lot of shoppers assume the app is just the store on a screen. Loblaw's own wording is more careful. In a July 2026 statement, Loblaw said prices may vary by store, region, banner, promotion, flyer, online channel, clearance item, or supplier cost changes. Then there are the store notes. At sixty-three No Frills locations, Loblaw's pickup data carries the line: No coupons or price matching for PC Express. So if your store price-matches at the till, that won't carry over to a pickup order there. The help pages add two more details. PC Express Pass, a monthly or annual subscription plan, gives unlimited free pickup and delivery on same-day or next-day orders of thirty dollars or more. And when you place an order, No Frills places a temporary hold on your card for one hundred and twenty-five percent of the estimated total. Its own page says a bank can take up to ten business days to release that hold. All of that is on No Frills' own pages. But a hold of a hundred and twenty-five dollars on a hundred-dollar order matters if you're watching your balance to the dollar. The fix: if you price-match, shop in person. If you order online, leave room on your card for the hold.

Number six. The points that don't wait for you. PC Optimum is free to join, and its own site says ten thousand points equals ten dollars of redeemable value. But at the grocery store, the site lists no base points per dollar for a plain card. You earn mainly through personalised offers and on-shelf promotions. No Frills' help page says offers have an expiry date, typically around seven days from the day they become available. And it says, if you forget to load your offers one week, you will not be able to earn the points associated with those offers once they expire. So a busy week can cost you points, not because of anything at the till, but because you didn't open an app. Loblaw's 2025 annual report says the program has over eighteen million active members. To be fair, No Frills' page says it can email you reminders to load offers, and lets you save an offer for next week. There's been other churn too. Loblaw sold PC Financial to EQB, the parent company of EQ Bank, and EQ Bank says the deal was completed on July 1st, 2026. It also says there will be no changes to the PC Optimum program as a direct result. And Loblaw says offers can differ between members, but the shelf price does not. Its words: the price on the shelf is the price everyone pays for that item at that store. The fix: set a weekly reminder and load your offers before you leave the house.

Number five. The notice you might get in the coming weeks. On October 2nd, Loblaw updated a notice about customer accounts. If you have a PC Optimum or Loblaw account, it's worth knowing. Here's the timeline. On March 10th, Loblaw said an unauthorized third party accessed some basic customer details, including names, phone numbers, and email addresses. At that point, it said its investigation indicated that passwords and credit card data were not compromised, and that stores and payment systems were unaffected. The update on October 2nd added more detail. It said the information varied by customer, and not every type was involved for every customer. But the list included names, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, dates of birth, loyalty account numbers, certain purchase order details and incomplete payment card information. And it said that, in the coming weeks, it will begin notifying impacted customers directly. That's a company statement about its own incident. We're not adding to it, and we don't know how many accounts are involved, because the update we read doesn't give a number. What it means for you is practical. Loblaw's own update asks customers to keep using caution with unexpected emails, calls or texts that ask for personal information or ask them to click a link. The fix: don't click links in an unexpected Loblaw or PC Optimum email. Open the app or type the website yourself, and change your password if you reused it anywhere else.

Number four. The maple leaf on the sign versus the line on the box. In July 2025, CBC News reported that at a No Frills in Toronto, strawberries were displayed with signage that included a red maple leaf and the phrase Prepared in Canada. The berries' packaging said product of USA. CBC said Loblaw didn't directly answer its questions, but that shortly after it asked, the signage was gone. That's a media observation, not a regulator finding. The CFIA told CBC it would be inappropriate to use a Made in Canada or Prepared in Canada claim if a product was only packaged here. Here's why the wording matters. Loblaw itself said in March 2025 that it was marking products Prepared in Canada with a distinctive red maple leaf. The CFIA's guidance does allow Prepared in Canada, but only for a food that has been entirely prepared here. Under CFIA guidance, Product of Canada means all or virtually all major ingredients, processing and labour are Canadian. Made in Canada needs a qualifier, like Made in Canada from imported ingredients. And the CFIA has issued penalties. In January 2026 it fined two stores under Loblaw banners ten thousand dollars each, over displays it said were misleading about a product's origin. Loblaw told CBC it was sorry for any confusion this may have caused. To be fair to No Frills, those two stores were a Real Canadian Superstore and a Fortinos, not a No Frills. The CFIA told CBC that between November 2024 and February 2026, it identified seventy-eight country-of-origin violations at retail stores. CBC didn't break that number down by retailer. Then on August 26th, 2026, Loblaw told Global News it is reintroducing country-of-origin signs in its fresh produce aisles. It also said it is reinstating the T symbol for products sourced directly from the U.S. that are affected by tariffs. Credit for that. The fix: the CFIA's origin rules cover shelf signs as well as packages, but the two can disagree. When they do, go by the origin line on the package, and you can report the mismatch to the CFIA.

Number three. What the package promises, and what Canada actually checks. Pick up a four-hundred-gram bag of something. Does it have to contain four hundred grams? Not exactly. Under the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations, each package gets a small tolerance, and a batch has to meet the declared amount on average. For a four-hundred-gram package, the tolerance is three percent, which is twelve grams. One pack a little under is allowed. Too many short packs, or a batch that averages under the label, is not. Competition Bureau guidance says this method is commonly known as the Average System. Then there's what changes when the package itself changes. Statistics Canada published a study in February 2025 on shrinking products. It said that from 2021 to 2023, twenty-nine point six percent of eligible grocery items tracked in the CPI experienced shrinkflation. And it said a much higher proportion of name-brand products than house-brand products experienced it. Loose food and the deli counter are a different system. Those go by Measurement Canada's rules, and its complaints page says sellers must not include the weight of the container in the weight of the food. The tool that helps with all of this is the unit price. But outside Quebec, unit pricing isn't required by law, and No Frills has no Quebec stores. Manitoba announced in June it will adopt unit pricing, and a federal private member's bill is in committee. On meat, briefly. In January 2025, CBC reported finding underweight meat packages at a No Frills in Calgary in late 2024. That's a media finding, not a regulator ruling. Separately, Loblaw said some weights in a small number of Western Canadian stores in 2023 weren't updated correctly in its system. In its words, in this situation, we made a mistake. That's a company admission, and it doesn't name No Frills. The fix: compare price per hundred grams, and if the units on two tags don't match, do the division yourself.

Before the last two, including the store that beat No Frills in our basket, one quick thing. If you want the next Canadian grocery name checked against the record like this, take a second to subscribe. Canadian Counter reads the fine print so you don't have to.

Number two. The electronic price tag. Loblaw started introducing electronic shelf labels in 2018. This year, a debate over surveillance pricing reached Ontario. That's the idea of using personal data to charge different people different prices. Manitoba passed a law that names electronic shelf labels and makes demanding a higher price because of personalized algorithmic pricing an unfair practice. In April, Premier Doug Ford rejected a similar ban for Ontario. In August, Global News reported, from freedom of information records, that Ford met Loblaw's CEO at the premier's home the next day. Global said the calendar doesn't reveal the subject of the conversation. Ford's office said the premier frequently meets with businesses and stakeholders. Loblaw said it regularly meets elected officials across the political spectrum, and is already on the record as not using surveillance pricing. Loblaw also told The Logic: we do not use, nor do we plan to use, algorithmic pricing to set or increase prices in our stores, including on our electronic shelf labels. And in a statement to Global News: in any particular store, pricing will not vary by customer, time of day, or demand. Everyone in a store pays the same price for the same product. There's no verified evidence of personalised shelf pricing at No Frills, and we're not going to claim it. The Competition Bureau has opened an examination of Canada's food supply chain, including pricing algorithms, loyalty programs and shrinkflation, with a final report expected in spring 2027. No findings yet. The fix: snap a photo of the shelf tag on anything big, and compare it to your receipt.

Number one. The discount store wasn't the cheapest store in our basket. On October 3rd, we looked up fifteen identical products, same product codes, on Loblaw's own website at seven stores under Loblaw banners. At the No Frills on Richmond Street West in Toronto, the basket came to seventy-three dollars and twenty-four cents. At the Loblaws on Bullock Drive in Markham, seventy-eight fifty-one. So No Frills beat its sister Loblaws, as you'd expect. But at the Real Canadian Superstore on Argentia Road in Mississauga, the same fifteen items came to seventy dollars and ninety-seven cents. That's two dollars and twenty-seven cents less than the No Frills. One item more than covers that gap. Tide Original liquid, one point seven seven litres, was fifteen ninety-nine at that No Frills, about thirty-three cents a load. At the Superstore it was on sale for nine ninety-nine until October 7th, about twenty-one cents a load. Take Tide out, and No Frills wins by about three dollars seventy. The other way round, Cheerios Family Size, five hundred and seventy grams, was four eighty-seven on sale at that Toronto No Frills, about eighty-five cents per hundred grams. At that Superstore it was seven forty-nine, about a dollar thirty-one. That's one day, fifteen items and website prices only. Loblaw says prices may vary by online channel, and its data lists pickup as unavailable at all three No Frills in the basket. Here's why it matters. The Competition Bureau's 2023 grocery study put it plainly: Loblaws owns No Frills and Maxi, Sobeys owns FreshCo, and Metro owns Food Basics and Super C. It said this is different from other countries, where large grocers compete against lower-priced options like ALDI or Lidl. Separately, Loblaw and George Weston admitted taking part in a bread price-fixing arrangement that ran from late 2001 to March 2015, and received immunity for reporting it. That's an admission, and the consumer class actions ended in a five-hundred-million-dollar court-approved settlement. Allegations against other grocers remain allegations, and those companies deny them. The fix: the banner on the sign is not a price guarantee. Compare the flyer, not the logo.

How to protect yourself. First, compare unit prices, not shelf prices. Do the division yourself when tags use different units. Outside Quebec, nobody is required to do it for you. Second, watch the till. Under section fifty-four of the Competition Act, a store can't charge more than the lowest of two or more prices it clearly displays, and that includes a shelf label. And look for that sign at the front of the store: the Scanner Price Accuracy Code. Under the code, as the Competition Bureau describes it, if an item without a price tag scans higher than the shelf price, it's free when it's worth less than ten dollars. Over ten, you get ten dollars off. The Canadian Press reported that Loblaw is a signatory, and that it applies to the first unit, not to items priced by weight or to member prices. Third, load your PC Optimum offers before you go, and read any limit line under a sale price. If you want an in-store price match, check your store's terms first and bring the competitor's current flyer. Fourth, read the origin line on the package, not the sign above it. If packaged food looks short, report it to the CFIA. If a scale at the deli looks off, that goes to Measurement Canada.

None of this is about boycotting No Frills. It's about walking in with your eyes open. And it's only fair to say No Frills gets some things right.

First, the store brand price gap is real. At that Toronto No Frills on October 3rd, No Name ketchup, one litre, was three sixty-nine, about thirty-seven cents per hundred millilitres, against five seventy-nine for Heinz, about fifty-eight cents, both regular prices. That's about thirty-six percent less. No Name smooth peanut butter, one kilogram, was four fifty on special, forty-five cents per hundred grams, against six fifty for Kraft, sixty-five cents. No Name frozen peas, seven hundred and fifty grams, were two seventy-seven on special, about thirty-seven cents per hundred grams, against four dollars for Green Giant, about fifty-three cents. Not every time, though. On the same capture, Unico diced tomatoes, seven hundred and ninety-six millilitres, were one seventy-nine on sale, about twenty-two cents per hundred millilitres. That's twenty-one cents under the No Name can, at twenty-five cents per hundred. Check the tag, not the brand.

Second, the written guarantees. No Frills' own return policy says that, with the original receipt, merchandise may be returned for exchange or refund within fourteen days, with original tags and packaging, and that some exceptions apply. It also offers what it calls the Try It You'll Love It Guarantee on President's Choice and No Name food. No Frills says that if you are not completely satisfied with the quality, you can simply return it along with the receipt or original package for an exchange or refund. That's a policy, not a quality claim from us, but it's a useful one.

Third, the near-date discounts. Loblaw and Flashfood said in March 2026 that the app saved customers more than fifty-eight million dollars on groceries in 2025, at over nine hundred Loblaw stores, including select No Frills. And in January 2024, Loblaw told CBC it had listened to feedback from customers and colleagues, and was going back to fifty percent off stickers on items nearing their best-before date, where that had existed before. Check whether your store has a Flashfood Zone, and ask whether it uses fifty percent stickers on near-date items.

That's the full picture: nine things worth knowing, three things worth using, and a store that Loblaw says is growing, even as some shoppers walk away.
