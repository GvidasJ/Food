# SCRIPT — INSTANT NOODLES

TITLE: Canadians Must AVOID These 10 "Noodle" Brands (Only 3 Are ACTUALLY Worth Buying)

ALTERNATES:
- Canadians Must AVOID These 10 "Noodle" Brands (The 3 Worth Buying Will Surprise You)
- We Investigated 10 Instant "Noodle" Brands In Canada (Only 3 Are Worth Buying)
- Every Famous Instant Noodle In Canada Is About To Get A Warning Label (Except These 3)

---

Before you boil another packet of instant noodles, flip it over and look at one number on the back. Because starting January the first, 2026, that number stops hiding. A new federal rule means nearly every package in the instant noodle aisle — the one your kids ask for, the one you grew up on, the one trending all over the internet — has to start wearing a black-and-white warning symbol on the front. A magnifying glass, right on the label, that says: high in sodium. We went through the ten most popular instant noodle brands in Canada, and we read every label — that's all we did, we read the labels and the regulations behind them — and what we found goes way past sodium. We found a beloved "Canadian" brand that isn't made in Canada. We found "Japanese" ramen that hasn't seen Japan in decades. We found a preservative named right there in the fine print that most people have never once read out loud. And we found an American law that says most of these packages couldn't even legally be called noodles south of the border — we'll get to that one, because the only brand in this video that would pass the American definition of a noodle is made in Canada, and it's probably not the one you think. Ten brands, biggest first, three winners at the end. Let's start with the one in almost every Canadian pantry.

Number one. Mr. Noodles. The bright packets, the seventy-nine-cent price tag, the name that's been on Canadian shelves since the late nineteen-seventies. If any instant noodle feels Canadian, it's this one — and the brand is Canadian. It belongs to a company called Anderson Watts, established in 1977, headquartered in the Vancouver area. But pick up a packet and read the fine print, because the label doesn't say made by. It says imported by. Imported by Anderson Watts of Vancouver — which means the noodles themselves are made somewhere else, and the somewhere else is overseas. A Canadian brand, a Canadian company, and a noodle that takes a boat to get here. Check the fine print on your own packet, because that's where the country of origin lives, in letters this small. Now the label itself. The noodle block: enriched wheat flour, palm oil, salt, sugar, guar gum, garlic powder. So the second ingredient in the noodle — before you ever open the seasoning — is palm oil, because these noodles, like almost every brand in this video, are flash-fried in oil at the factory. That's the 1958 invention that made instant noodles possible, and it's never left. Then the soup base: salt first, then artificial chicken flavour, then monosodium glutamate, spelled out right there, third ingredient. And here's the number that matters: on the packages we filmed, a full packet of Mr. Noodles lands around the range where one packet can carry most of a day's worth of sodium — read your own label, because the printed number is right there and it varies by flavour. Remember that number, because in January it triggers something. Health Canada's new front-of-package rule draws a line for sodium at fifteen percent of your daily value — three hundred and forty-five milligrams. Meet or beat that, and the package has to wear the high-in-sodium symbol on the front. A classic instant noodle packet doesn't just meet that line. It clears it three, four, five times over.

Number two. Sapporo Ichiban. If you grew up in British Columbia or on the prairies, this is the one. The Japanese distributor that brings it into Western Canada started with a single order of twenty-five cases back in 1969, and by their own account they now move more than eight hundred thousand cases a year in Western Canada alone. That's not a product, that's an institution. So here's the first surprise: the "Japanese" ramen in that bag is made in California. Sanyo Foods, the family company behind the brand, opened its American plant in Garden Grove, California in 1978, and by its own account that plant has been making Sapporo Ichiban for the United States and Canadian market for more than forty years. Japanese recipe, Japanese name, California noodle. Now read the bag with us, because this label does something almost none of the others do — it tells you the truth in an interesting way. The ingredient list on the package we checked reads: palm oil, and then in brackets, T-B-H-Q and citric acid added to protect flavour. TBHQ. Tertiary butylhydroquinone. Most people have eaten it for decades and never once read it out loud. It's a preservative for the frying oil, and here's the thing — it's completely legal. Health Canada's list of permitted preservatives allows TBHQ in fats and oils at up to zero point zero two percent, and since the noodle is fried in that fat, a trace of it rides along into the block. Legal in Canada, listed right there on the bag. We're not telling you it's dangerous — we're telling you it's printed on the package, and now you know what the letters mean. One more thing this label does honestly: the serving size is the whole package. One hundred grams, one serving, and the sodium for that serving on the label we filmed: one thousand eight hundred and twenty milligrams. That's seventy-nine percent of an entire day, in one bag, before the toast, before the soup at dinner. Against the new three-forty-five trigger, that's more than five times over the line.

If you've ever eaten either of those two brands — and if you're Canadian, you have — do us a favour and hit the like button, because it genuinely tells us to keep making these investigations. Now it gets stranger.

Number three. Nongshim Shin Ramyun. The red bag with the big number that Korea made famous. And in Canada this isn't a niche import anymore — Nongshim itself says it holds the second largest market share in the Canadian instant noodle market, it registered Nongshim Canada in Mississauga in 2019, and the four-packs sit in Walmart and Costco next to the bread. Here's what to check before anything else: the product-of line. Because two different Shin Ramyuns circulate in Canada — one made in South Korea, and one made at Nongshim's plants in Rancho Cucamonga, California, which ship across North America. Same red bag, two different factories, and only the fine print tells you which one you're holding. Now the label. On the North American ingredient list there's no monosodium glutamate anywhere. Instead you'll find hydrolyzed corn protein, hydrolyzed soy protein, and yeast extract — ingredients that bring glutamates to the party under different names. Keep that trick in mind, because it's about to matter on the next brand. And the sodium: a full bag of Shin Ramyun, per the company's own nutrition data, carries around seventeen hundred and ninety milligrams — seventy-eight percent of your day. But on American-format labels you might see the serving listed as half a bag, which makes every number on the panel look half as big. Here's what most Canadians don't know: that half-bag serving is not allowed here. Canadian law — the Food and Drug Regulations, section B.01.002A — says that when a package holds less than twice the official reference amount, the Nutrition Facts serving must be the entire package. The reference amount for noodles is eighty-five grams, twice that is one-seventy, and a hundred-and-twenty-gram bag of Shin Ramyun is under it. So in Canada, the label has to show you the whole bag's sodium. In America, the half-block dodge is business as usual. It's one of the quiet ways a Canadian label is more honest than an American one — when you're reading the Canadian bag.

Number four. Samyang Buldak. The black packet with the fire-breathing chicken — the fastest-rising noodle brand in the country, powered by a billion spicy-challenge videos. Samyang is actually the elder statesman here: founded in 1961, it made Korea's very first instant ramen in 1963, half a century before Buldak turned eating noodles into content in 2012. The Canadian packs are made in South Korea, and a five-pack at Walmart runs about nine dollars at time of filming — which makes this one of the most expensive noodles in the aisle, roughly four times the price of Mr. Noodles per packet. Now, you may have heard something about this brand being banned. Here's what actually happened, precisely. In June 2024, Denmark's food authority recalled three Buldak varieties, saying the capsaicin levels — the spice — were high enough to pose a risk of acute poisoning. One month later, after its scientists re-ran the assessment, Denmark reversed itself on two of the three and put them back on shelves; only the Three-X Spicy version stayed recalled. Part of the original scare was based on the company's own marketed spice figures. And Canada? We searched the federal recall database for Buldak before filming this. Zero results. No recall, no advisory, nothing — every variety is perfectly legal on Canadian shelves. Two governments looked at the same noodle and made different calls; that's the whole story, and you deserved to hear it straight. The label, meanwhile, says what it says: noodles that are eleven percent palm oil, and sixteen hundred and forty milligrams of sodium in the packet — seventy-one percent of your day, and since it's a stir-fry noodle, the sauce mostly ends up eaten, not poured off.

Number five. Nissin Cup Noodles. The company that invented the entire category. Momofuku Ando fried the first instant ramen on August 25th, 1958, watched it become the cheapest hot meal on earth, then invented Cup Noodles in 1971 so you wouldn't even need a bowl. The cups on Canadian shelves come from Nissin's American plants, and the cup we checked carries eleven hundred and sixty milligrams of sodium — the whole cup is the serving, fifty percent of your day, and more than three times the new warning-symbol trigger. But that's not why this brand earns its spot. This label performs a magic trick. On the front of many Cup Noodles: no MSG added. On the back of the same cup: hydrolyzed corn protein, hydrolyzed soy protein, yeast extract. The same glutamate family, wearing different name tags. And here's what makes that interesting in Canada: the federal food agency's own guidance says claims like "no MSG added" are considered misleading and deceptive when other sources of free glutamates are present — and it names hydrolyzed vegetable proteins and autolyzed yeast extracts specifically. Their words, not ours. So read the front, then read the back, and decide for yourself what that claim is worth. While you're at it, look for the letters we taught you on brand two — this cup lists TBHQ, printed plainly, legal as ever. One more thing for the record: in March 2025, one imported Nissin line — the Big Cup Noodle brought in from overseas — was recalled in Canada. Not for spice, not for chemicals. For undeclared shrimp in one flavour and undeclared peanut in another — allergens missing from the label. Because in this country, the recalls that actually happen in the noodle aisle are about what the label fails to say. It happened again with a brand called Baixiang in the summer of 2024 — a Class 1 recall across Alberta, B.C. and Ontario for undeclared peanut, triggered not by an inspector but by a single consumer complaint. One person read a label carefully, and the system moved. Keep that in mind next time someone tells you reading labels doesn't do anything.

Numbers six, seven, and eight — and we're grouping them because they're triplets. No name from Loblaws, Great Value from Walmart, Compliments from Sobeys. The store brands. Thirty-nine cents for a packet of no name at time of filming, very likely the cheapest hot meal in Canada. And when you read all three labels side by side, they're the same recipe in different jackets: a fried palm-oil noodle block, then a sachet where salt comes first, monosodium glutamate spelled out plainly — no hiding behind hydrolyzed anything, although no name and Great Value actually list both MSG and hydrolyzed soy protein — artificial chicken flavour, and the flavour-enhancer twins, disodium inosinate and guanylate. Give the store brands this: nothing here pretends. The no name label even declares the serving as the entire eighty-five gram package — thirteen hundred and sixty milligrams of sodium, fifty-nine percent of your day, stated straight. The thirty-nine-cent noodle is more upfront about what it is than cups costing four times as much. One more label detail worth respecting: every one of these blocks is made with enriched wheat flour, and that's not a choice. Canadian law requires that any white flour in food sold in Canada be enriched — thiamine, riboflavin, niacin, folic acid, iron — no matter where on earth the noodle was fried. Even the cheapest packet in the store carries that. And if you're wondering about President's Choice — Loblaws' premium brand doesn't currently make a classic instant noodle packet at all. The gap in the lineup is almost a statement.

Number nine. Indomie Mi Goreng. Made by Indofood in Indonesia — and Indonesia is the second-biggest instant noodle nation on the planet, fourteen and a half billion servings a year. This is the packet with five little sachets — seasoning powder, seasoning oil, sweet soy sauce, chili sauce, fried onions — and the on-shelf name is refreshingly honest: instant fried noodles. It says fried right on the front. Read the fine print, though, and our TBHQ friend appears twice, once in the noodle and once in the fried onions, disguised under its European code name: antioxidant 319. Same preservative, different alias — European-style labels use numbers, Canadian-style labels use words, and both are legal here. The seasoning brings monosodium glutamate as flavour enhancer 621, plus a yellow dye called tartrazine as colouring 102. And now the honest surprise: the sodium on the label we checked is eight hundred and sixty-nine milligrams for the whole packet — the lowest of any classic fried brand in this video. The scariest-looking label in the aisle, five sachets and a wall of E-numbers, is carrying half the sodium of the gentle-looking Japanese bag from number two. That's the entire reason you read labels instead of judging packaging. Remember Indonesia, by the way. It comes back at the end of this video in a way that involves your own backyard.

Number ten. Maruchan. In America, this is the king — Toyo Suisan's plants in California, Virginia and Texas push out over three point six billion packs a year, and in the States those yellow packets go for pocket change. In Canada, it's a bit player, scattered across Walmart and dollar stores, and the famous foam Instant Lunch cups only turn up patchily. But we're including it for one reason: the label. The American Maruchan panel is the half-block trick in its purest form — serving size: half a noodle block with half the seasoning packet, seven hundred and sixty milligrams. Nobody on this earth cooks half a block of ramen and folds the packet closed for tomorrow. Double it, like a human being eating dinner, and you're at roughly fifteen hundred milligrams. That serving-size game is exactly what Canadian law shuts down for packs this size — here, as you now know, the label must declare the whole package. The noodle itself: fried in vegetable oil, preserved by TBHQ — their words — with the seasoning listing monosodium glutamate and all three hydrolyzed proteins, corn, wheat and soy. The full house. And that's the avoid list: ten brands, and one pattern you can now spot from across the aisle — a fried block, a salt-first sachet, and a sodium number that clears the January warning line by multiples.

So what do you actually buy? Three answers, in order.

Winner number three. Nongshim Shin Light. Same company as the red bag at number three — and this one earns its spot for a single line printed on the front: air dried, not fried. The noodle block skips the oil bath entirely; there's no palm oil in the block at all, and the only fat in the bag is a small seasoning-oil sachet. Nongshim claims seventy-five percent less fat than regular Shin — their number, from their packaging. It's in Walmart and T&T right now. But we promised honesty, so here's the honesty: the sodium is not better. Around eighteen hundred milligrams a bag, right up there with the original — this will wear the January symbol just like its red sibling. Buy it for what the label actually delivers, the un-fried noodle, and use half the sachet if sodium is what you're watching.

Winner number two. Lotus Foods millet and brown rice ramen. Find it at Superstore and the natural aisle. The entire ingredient list of the noodle block is two items long: organic brown rice flour, organic millet flour. That's it. No oil, no salt, nothing else — the company describes its rice ramen as never fried, and the block itself contains no added sodium at all. Every milligram lives in the miso soup sachet, so the label's five hundred and ninety milligrams per half pack is a choice you control — use the whole sachet, half of it, or none, and season it yourself. Fair warning so we stay honest: it's the one label in this video still using a half-pack serving, so double the numbers if you eat the whole thing — which still lands it lowest in the video. It costs more per bowl than a thirty-nine-cent packet. Two ingredients usually do.

And winner number one. Farkay steam-fried noodles. Here's the twist this whole video has been building to. Remember the American law we mentioned at the start? In the United States, federal rules say anything sold as "noodles" must contain egg — five and a half percent egg solids. Canada has no such rule; here, "noodles" is a free word, which is why every egg-free block in this video is legally named. Well — Farkay contains actual egg. Liquid whole egg, right in the ingredient list, the old Chinese-Canadian noodle way. It's the only brand in this entire video that would pass the American definition of a noodle, and it's the one that's actually made in Canada — by Northwest Food Products on Powell Street in Vancouver, a company that's been making Chinese-style noodles on the West Coast for generations. Under four hundred grams for around five and a half dollars at time of filming, it's in Metro, Superstore and Well.ca, and here's the best part: there is no sachet. No seasoning packet, no salt bomb, no flavour enhancers — you season it in your own wok, which means the sodium is whatever you decide it is. Full honesty as always: steam-fried still means it meets palm oil in the process, so it's not an oil-free noodle. It's something better — a Canadian-made, egg-containing, sachet-free noodle from the same city where Mr. Noodles' owners run a brand that's made overseas. One Vancouver company imports its noodles. The other one still makes them.

Now zoom out, because the big picture is stranger than any single label. Canadians eat about two hundred million servings of instant noodles a year — that sounds enormous until you learn it ranks us thirty-third in the world, at not even five servings per person per year. That's roughly one packet every two and a half months. South Koreans average somewhere around seventy-five to eighty each — more than one a week, every week, for every man, woman and child. China as a whole eats forty-three billion servings. Indonesia, fourteen and a half billion. And here's a small absurdity we stumbled on while researching prices: Statistics Canada tracks the monthly retail price of a hundred and ten grocery items — dry pasta is in there, canned soup is in there — but instant noodles aren't tracked at all. Quite possibly the cheapest hot meal in the entire country is invisible to the official food-price basket. And here's the part nobody tells you: Canada is quietly inside those numbers. Cereals Canada — the organization that markets our wheat to the world — describes our flagship red spring wheat as ideal for noodles and Asian products, and lists one class, Canada Prairie Spring Red, as ideal specifically for instant noodles. This crop year, some twenty-eight and a half million tonnes of Canadian wheat are headed to more than eighty countries — including, on the five-year average, over two million tonnes a year to Indonesia, home of Indomie, and one point seven million tonnes to Japan, where instant noodles were born. So when a packet of noodles crosses the ocean to reach your grocery shelf, there's a real chance it started as wheat on a prairie farm, sailed away, and came home fried. We don't eat many instant noodles by world standards. We grow a staggering share of the world's.

One last number before you go. Health Canada asked the soup and noodle category to bring sodium down — voluntarily — starting back in 2012, and instant oriental noodle soups are named in that category by name. When the results were measured in 2017, the category had only met the first interim step. Across all foods, Health Canada's own evaluation found just fourteen percent of categories hit their targets, and nearly half made no meaningful progress at all — while seventy-seven percent of the sodium in the Canadian diet comes from processed food, their figure. So in 2020 the same target was politely re-issued, still voluntary, still no penalty. That's why the January rule matters: the symbol isn't a request. So here's your checklist for the noodle aisle. After January first, look for the magnifying glass — it does the math for you. Before then, flip the package: in Canada, on a single packet, the whole package must be the serving, so the number you see is the number you eat. Check the product-of line, because the brand's flag and the factory's flag are rarely the same. Look for the words air dried or steam dried if you want to skip the oil bath. And read seasoning sachets like a skeptic — especially any package that brags about what it doesn't contain. Tell us in the comments which of these ten is in your pantry right now, and which label genuinely surprised you — for us it was the thirty-nine-cent packet being the most honest one on the shelf. If someone in your house lives on instant noodles, send them this video before January, when the aisle fills up with magnifying glasses. And we'll see you at the next label.
