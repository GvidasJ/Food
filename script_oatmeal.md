On December 4th, 2025, Statistics Canada reported that oat production in this country rose 16.7 percent, to 3.9 million tonnes. Agriculture Canada says Saskatchewan alone grew 45 percent of it. Statistics Canada's trade data shows 205,067 tonnes of rolled and flaked oats exported in 2025, 93 percent of those tonnes to the United States, about 14 tonnes out for every tonne that came in. In February 2025, the Angus Reid Institute found four in five Canadians, 78 percent, said they were already buying, or likely to buy, more Canadian products. And in February 2026, Dalhousie University's Agri-Food Analytics Lab found 21.2 percent of the Canadians it surveyed always, and 37.1 percent often, check where their food came from. So go to your cupboard and find the oats. If it's the one-kilogram Quaker Large Flake box, the front reads, made with 100% whole grain Canadian oats. A label panel that Loblaw's website shows for that box puts small print under a red maple leaf. Made in Canada from domestic and imported ingredients. On September 30th we read the oat labels on retailers' own Canadian websites, looked up who owns each brand, and read the federal rules behind those words. Eleven brands. Eight that miss at least one of our two tests, most expensive per hundred grams first. Then three that pass both. Because the truth is not always on the menu.

First, sixty seconds of origin law. In Canada, the Canadian Food Inspection Agency's guidelines set out what these phrases mean. A food may say Product of Canada when all or virtually all major ingredients, processing and labour are Canadian. The agency says minor ingredients generally means less than a total of 2 percent. The word Canadian on its own counts the same as Product of Canada. And when a label says 100% Canadian about an ingredient, the agency says all of that ingredient must be Canadian, not just virtually all. Made in Canada is different. It needs a qualifier, like from domestic and imported ingredients, and the agency says that claim may be used regardless of the level of Canadian content. Its own worked example is a cookie made in Canada with Canadian flour, oatmeal and shortening, and imported sugar. Prepared in Canada means the food was entirely prepared here. It says nothing about where the oats grew. The agency's own explainer video says Product of Canada means all, or almost all, Canadian ingredients, prepared in Canada, and that made in Canada means the food was last significantly changed here. Its March 2025 notice to industry recommends that a maple leaf on a pack sit next to a domestic content statement. Its consumer guide says the Government of Canada does not pre-approve food labels. And it puts it plainly. There is no official logo for Canadian food products, and a maple leaf on a product does not always mean the product is Canadian. It adds that a label saying prepared for a Canadian retailer doesn't mean the food was prepared in Canada. Two more things matter. Oats are not on Canada's list of foods that must show a country of origin. And the name on the back only has to be the company the food was made by or for. It does not have to be the mill. One piece of background. During the 2025 trade fight, Canada put a 25 percent counter-tariff on American oat grain from March 4th to August 31st. Rolled oats and breakfast cereals were not in Canada's surtax orders. So every bag gets two questions. Is the company at the top of the ownership chain Canadian? And does the bag itself say Product of Canada? Pass both, and in this video, that is what actually Canadian means. How to protect yourself. First, read the origin line, not the maple leaf. Product of Canada means all or virtually all of it is Canadian. Made in Canada from domestic and imported ingredients can be used regardless of the level of Canadian content. Prepared in Canada says where it was prepared, not where the oats grew. And a Canada Organic logo next to the word imported means exactly that. Second, find the owner. The name on the bag is the company responsible for it, not always the parent. Robin Hood's bag appears to name Smucker Foods of Canada, and the parent is in Ohio. And if a food was made entirely abroad and only a Canadian company is named, the rules want the words imported by or imported for, unless the country is shown. Third, don't assume you know the mill. The label doesn't have to name it. Some companies say so on their own sites, like Quaker in Peterborough, and Rogers in Armstrong and Chilliwack. If neither the bag nor the site says, you don't know, and neither do we. Fourth, compare the price per hundred grams on the shelf tag. On September 30th, a 1 kilogram box of Quaker large flake ran from 3.75 on special at the Superstore to 6.99 on Save-On's Langley site. The three that passed both tests cost 45 to 73 cents per hundred grams.

Canada grew 3.9 million tonnes of oats last year, and on September 16th, Statistics Canada's model estimated this year's crop would fall 22.7 percent, to 3.0 million. The small ones tell you where they came from and whose name is on them.
