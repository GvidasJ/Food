First, sixty seconds of origin law. In Canada, the Canadian Food Inspection Agency's guidelines set out what these phrases mean. A food may say Product of Canada when all or virtually all major ingredients, processing and labour are Canadian. The agency says minor ingredients generally means less than a total of 2 percent. The word Canadian on its own counts the same as Product of Canada. Made in Canada is different. It needs a qualifier, like from domestic and imported ingredients, and the agency says that claim may be used regardless of the level of Canadian content. Its own worked example is a cookie made in Canada with Canadian flour, oatmeal and shortening, and imported sugar. And when a label says 100% Canadian about an ingredient, the agency says all of that ingredient must be Canadian, not just virtually all. Prepared in Canada means the food was entirely prepared here. It says nothing about where the oats grew. The agency's own explainer video says made in Canada means the food was last significantly changed here. And the agency's March 2025 notice to industry recommends that a maple leaf on a pack sit next to a domestic content statement. Its consumer guide puts it plainly. There is no official logo for Canadian food products, and a maple leaf on a product does not always mean the product is Canadian. It adds that a label saying prepared for a Canadian retailer doesn't mean the food was prepared in Canada. In March 2026, the agency said it had issued 47,000 dollars in financial penalties since April 2025 for inaccurate or misleading country of origin claims. None of the businesses it named sells oats, and none of them is in this video. Two more things matter. Oats are not on Canada's list of foods that must show a country of origin. And the name on the back only has to be the company the food was made by or for. It does not have to be the mill. So every bag gets two questions. Is the company at the top of the ownership chain Canadian? And does the bag itself say Product of Canada? Pass both, and in this video, it's actually Canadian. One last piece of background. During the 2025 trade fight, Canada put a 25 percent counter-tariff on American oat grain from March 4th to August 31st. Rolled oats and breakfast cereals were never on Canada's lists. And Canada mills plenty of its own. Richardson Milling lists oat mills in Portage la Prairie, Manitoba, and Martensville, Saskatchewan. Grain Millers says it has milled oats in Yorkton, Saskatchewan since 2001. None of the companies in this video names its mill on the bag.

The owner is Loblaw, and George Weston's 2025 annual report says all of Loblaw's material operations are carried out in Canada. So the owner passes. Now the box. On the front, beside the Canada Organic logo, with its red maple leaf, the box says imported, and in French, importé. That is not a slip. The agency says that on imported products using the Canada Organic logo, a country of origin statement or the word imported is required near the logo, to avoid misleading consumers. Under the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations, an imported food that carries the Canada Organic logo must show either the country it came from or the word imported, close to the logo. The box shows the word imported. We could not find the country on any image Loblaw's site served, and we are not going to guess it. The ingredient line on Loblaw's site reads organic whole grain rolled oats, sugars, organic evaporated cane syrup, organic maple sugar, sea salt, organic natural flavour. The owner is Canadian, and the label is doing exactly what the regulation asks. A Canadian grocer, a maple leaf on the logo, and the word imported right beside it.

How to protect yourself. First, read the origin line, not the maple leaf. Product of Canada means all or virtually all of it is Canadian. Made in Canada from domestic and imported ingredients can be used regardless of the level of Canadian content. Prepared in Canada says where it was prepared, not where the oats grew. And a Canada Organic logo next to the word imported means exactly that. Second, find the owner. The name on the bag is the company responsible for it, not always the parent. If a food was made entirely abroad and only a Canadian company is named, the rules want the words imported by or imported for, unless the country is shown. Robin Hood's says Smucker Foods of Canada, and the parent is in Ohio. Third, don't assume you know the mill. The label doesn't have to name it, and most don't. Some companies say so on their own sites, like Quaker in Peterborough and Rogers in Armstrong. If neither the bag nor the site says, you don't know, and neither do we. Fourth, compare the price per hundred grams on the shelf tag. On September 30th, the same Quaker box ran from 3.75 to 6.99 depending on the store, and the three bags that passed both tests cost 30 to 47 cents per hundred grams.

Canada grew 3.9 million tonnes of oats last year, and on September 16th, Statistics Canada's model estimated this year's crop would fall 22.7 percent, to 3.0 million. Whose name is on the bag is a separate question, and the answer is in the smallest type on the box. The big letters sell you the oats. The small ones tell you where they came from and who owns them.
