Eighty-five bottles of olive oil went into a federal lab in this country last year. Twenty-eight of them failed. That is one in three. It was the worst result of any food the Canadian Food Inspection Agency tested that year. Worse than honey. Worse than fish. Worse than grated cheese. Seven thousand two hundred and forty-five litres of adulterated olive oil were pulled before they could be sold. And the report does not name a single brand.

So the only inspector standing in front of your bottle on a Saturday morning is you. This video is about how to be that inspector. Seven bottles you have seen your whole life, counted down from seven to one, and the two we would actually pull off the shelf. Number one is the bottle in the fridge door of a million Canadian kitchens, with a seven-million-dollar settlement behind two words on its label. Don't skip ahead. Everything here comes from the CFIA's own reports, court filings, the brands' own pages, and shelf prices pulled this week in Toronto, Calgary, Vancouver, Montréal and Halifax. Every price gets re-checked on camera.

Before the brands, four things you need in your pocket, because the whole video is built on them.

One. The grade word. Look at what is printed immediately in front of the words olive oil. Extra virgin is a grade. Under the international standard the CFIA tests against, it means oil taken from olives by mechanical means only, with free acidity no higher than 0.8 grams per hundred, and no defects on the taste panel. Virgin is the grade below it. Then it gets interesting. If the front says pure, classic, light, extra light, cooking olive oil, or just olive oil with nothing in front of it, that is refined oil. Refined olive oil with some virgin oil blended back in for colour and taste. The back label will usually say so, in five words: refined olive oil and virgin olive oils. Refined is not a scandal. It has a job. It is neutral and it takes heat. The problem, and you will see it seven times, is paying extra virgin money for it.

Two. The origin line. Here is the thing almost nobody in this country knows. The CFIA says it plainly in its own fraud report: it is not mandatory to declare the country of origin on oil labels. The rule for imported oil is only that the label shows it is imported, and that can be satisfied with the words imported by and the name of a company in Ontario. No country at all. So when a label does name a country, read the whole sentence. Product of Italy is one statement. Imported from Italy is another. Packed in Italy with oils from Italy, Spain, Greece and Tunisia is a third. In the European Union the law says the origin of extra virgin oil is where the olives were harvested and milled, not where the bottle was filled. Canada has no such rule for oil.

Three. The date. A best-before date is somebody's guess about when the oil will die. A harvest date is a fact about when it was born. We looked for a printed harvest date on every mainstream bottle we could get a label image for this week. Bertolli, Filippo Berio, Gallo, Colavita, Carapelli, President's Choice, no name. We did not find one. You will check the physical bottles on camera, but go in expecting to find only a best-before.

Four. The number. Take the shelf price, divide by the millilitres in the bottle, multiply by a hundred. That is your cost per hundred millilitres. Write it on your hand. And because every label in this country is printed twice, read the French line under the English one. Produit d'Italie and emballé en Italie are not the same claim, and on some bottles they sit a centimetre apart.

Number seven. No name extra virgin olive oil. The yellow label. Seven hundred and fifty millilitres, ten dollars at Loblaws this week, nine dollars at No Frills and Maxi. That is a dollar thirty-three per hundred millilitres at the ten-dollar price. Here is what is genuinely good about it. It promises you nothing. There is no hillside on the label, no family, no grandfather. When a value brand tells you nothing, it is not hiding anything. It is just not selling you anything either.

So why is it on this list. Two reasons. First, the bottle is clear plastic, and clear plastic under grocery-store lighting for weeks is the fastest way to age an oil there is. Second, and this is the beat that repeats all the way to number one: no name also sells a bottle called pure olive oil. Same size, same yellow label, same shelf. Loblaws price this week, ten dollars. No Frills, nine dollars. Identical to the extra virgin. One is the top grade. One is refined oil with virgin blended back in. Same price. Loblaw's own product pages could not show us the origin line for either bottle, so that gets read on camera. The habit from number seven: read the grade word before you read anything else, and do the arithmetic.

Number six. Great Value extra virgin olive oil. Walmart's own label, and on the number we are carrying, the cheapest national-brand extra virgin on any Canadian shelf this week. One litre, ten ninety-seven, which is a dollar ten per hundred millilitres. For a lot of families that is not a preference, it is the budget, and nobody here is going to lecture anybody about that. The honest concession is real: at that price it beats the Italian names beside it by a wide margin, and a coat of arms on a bottle has never made the oil inside it better.

Here is what to check instead. Walmart's own listing for the five-hundred-millilitre bottle says a product of Spain. One country. Good. The listing for the one-litre bottle says nothing about where it came from, and the one-litre is the one most people buy. Then the same trick as number seven: Great Value pure olive oil, one litre, ten ninety-seven. Identical price to the extra virgin. Both bottles get turned around on camera. The habit from number six: count the countries on the back. If the count is zero, that is information too.

Number five. President's Choice. Now the gap starts to open, because this is positioned as the good one. PC Splendido, the dark glass, the pop-up spout, the words la cucina italiana on the front. Loblaws has spent forty years teaching Canadians that President's Choice means somebody in a test kitchen cared, and often somebody did. Full credit: it is a proper dark bottle, and the organic version prints its origin right on the front. Product of E.U. Produit d'U.E. That is a truthful, checkable statement, even if E.U. is twenty-seven countries wide.

Here is what the brand name is hiding, and it is not the oil. It is the price. Same one-litre Splendido, same week. Eleven dollars on sale at Loblaws, Provigo and Atlantic Superstore. Sixteen dollars regular at Loblaws and No Frills. Fourteen ninety-nine at Shoppers Drug Mart. The three-litre tin: thirty-two dollars at Atlantic Superstore, thirty-five at Real Canadian Superstore, forty-five at Loblaws in Toronto. Thirteen dollars of difference on one tin, from one company, in one week. The retailer's description says the oil is crafted in Italy. Crafted is not a legal term. The origin line on the Splendido label was not readable in any of Loblaw's images, so that is another back-of-bottle read on camera, in both languages. The habit from number five: the brand covers many bottles and many prices. The origin line and the per-hundred number are the information. The logo is not.

Number four. Carapelli. Casa Olearia Carapelli, Firenze, 1893, the tricolour on the front, and in big letters, one hundred percent Italian. This is the dearest of the big Italian names on a Canadian shelf: eighteen dollars for five hundred millilitres at Loblaws, which is three dollars sixty per hundred. Twelve ninety-nine at Superstore, which is two sixty. The concession first: one hundred percent Italian is a specific, falsifiable claim, and a company that makes it is a company that can be held to it. Most of this shelf will not say that much.

Here is what is not on the front. Carapelli is owned by Deoleo, a company headquartered in Madrid, which also owns Bertolli. Deoleo's own press releases describe it as the producer of Bertolli, Carapelli and Carbonell. Two Italian names at two ends of the shelf, one Spanish holding company. That is legal, and brands get bought and sold. But hold the Italian story up against the record. In 2016 Italy's competition authority fined Carapelli Firenze three hundred thousand euros over three oils sold in Italy as extra virgin that its testing graded as virgin, the level below. That was an administrative fine, in Italy, about grade, not about adulteration, and the company said at the time that its defence had been taken into account. We could not confirm the final status of the appeal, so treat it as what it was: a regulator's decision in one country in one year. The habit from number four: one company, three Italian names. When the front says one hundred percent Italian, hold them to it, and check that the French line says the same thing.

Number three. Colavita. The green bottle, the yellow label, family-owned, real roots in Molise, and a brand a lot of restaurant people have used happily for years. Full credit where it is earned: Colavita sells a one-hundred-percent-Italian bottle, a Greek bottle that says on its own ingredient line extra virgin olive oil from Greece, and a California bottle. When a company offers single-country lines at all, that is a company with the traceability to do it. Most do not bother.

Here is the trap, and it is the same trap at every brand at this level. The single-origin bottle and the general Premium Selection bottle look almost identical from two metres away. Same colours, same shape, same logo. And the general bottle is the one on most shelves. Twenty-five dollars a litre at Loblaws this week, twenty-three at No Frills, fifteen ninety-nine at Longo's, twelve ninety-nine at Cataldi in Toronto, whose own flyer prints product of Italy beside it. Loblaw's product images could not show us the side panel, and Sobeys' listing shows no origin field at all, so the wording on the Canadian one-litre bottle gets read on camera. One more thing, said carefully. In 2011 the University of California's olive centre bought the five top-selling imported extra virgin brands in California and ran them past two accredited taste panels. Ten of the eighteen Colavita samples failed both panels for the extra virgin grade. Every sample passed the chemistry for acidity, and no foreign oil was found in any of them. That study is fifteen years old, funded by California producers, run on American shelves, and the university says it should not be used to describe oil on sale today. It is the origin of most of what you have heard about this shelf, and you deserve to know exactly how much it does and does not say. The habit from number three: buy the specific bottle, not the logo. If the front does not name one country, you are holding the blend, and you should be paying blend money for it.

Quick favour before the last two. If this is going to change what you pick up on Saturday, tap the like button. It costs you nothing, and it puts this in front of somebody who is about to spend twenty dollars on the wrong bottle.

Number two. Filippo Berio. Dal 1867, the dark green glass, the gold cap, and a name that sounds like a person who stood behind what was inside. Start with what is true. It is a real company, Salov, founded in Lucca in 1919, and the extra virgin bottle is proper dark glass, which is the right choice. Two things the front does not tell you. Since 2014, ninety percent of Salov has been owned by Bright Food Group of Shanghai; the deal counsel's own announcement says so. And the company's own FAQ says its oil is sourced from Italy, Spain, Greece, Portugal and Tunisia.

Now the American case, because you will hear it described wrongly. In 2014 a California shopper sued over the words imported from Italy on the front of the bottle, arguing they meant Italian olives. The company's defence was that the back label already said packed in Italy with select extra virgin olive oils from Italy, Spain, Greece and Tunisia, and that no reasonable shopper would read the front any other way. A judge let the case proceed. It settled in 2017: up to five dollars a customer, and the company agreed not to use the phrase imported from Italy, which the objectors noted it had already dropped. No court found the label false. Nothing was admitted. That is an American case under American rules, and we will say so plainly. It shows you exactly where to look: the front of the bottle, then the back, and whether they tell the same story.

Then the staircase, and this is the biggest piece of misdirection on any shelf in the country. Filippo Berio extra virgin, seven hundred and fifty millilitres, twenty dollars at Loblaws. Filippo Berio classic taste, all-purpose, same size, twenty dollars. Filippo Berio extra light taste, high heat uses, same size, twenty dollars. On sale at Superstore this week all three are nine ninety-nine. Identical prices, top to bottom. The ingredient line on the second and third bottles reads olive oil composed of refined olive oil and virgin olive oils. Light is not a grade. It does not appear in the international standard the federal lab tests against, and the CFIA's fats-and-oils page contains no definition of it for olive oil. It describes the colour and the taste after the oil has been refined. We are making no health claim here at all. We are telling you the grade is different and the price is not. The habit from number two: on a staircase, the gap in price between the rungs is often zero, and the gap in what is inside is the whole difference between pressed juice and refined fat.

Number one. Bertolli. Dal 1865. The bottle in the fridge door of a million Canadian kitchens. The front says world's number one olive oil brand, and this year it carries a sticker that says number one most trusted by Canadians. Start with what is true and good, because this brand has earned some of it. The extra virgin comes in a dark green bottle that the company says is one hundred percent recycled plastic. It is on every national banner from Superstore to Sobeys to Walmart, which for a lot of people is the difference between buying olive oil and not buying it. And the two-litre jug at Walmart this week is sixteen ninety-seven, eighty-five cents per hundred millilitres, one of the cheapest extra virgins in the country.

Now the part most Canadians do not know. Bertolli is not an Italian family company. It is owned by Deoleo of Madrid, the same company as Carapelli at number four. Deoleo's own FAQ for Canadian shoppers says the oil is blended from multiple origins, and that importing from Southern Hemisphere countries, Argentina, Peru and Chile, lets it source first-harvest oil in March, April, May and June. We looked at nine retailer images of the Canadian bottle, front, back, sides and top, and did not find a country of origin line on any of them. That is legal in Canada. It is also the single most important thing to read on camera.

And the settlement. In the United States, Deoleo's American arm paid seven million dollars in 2018 to settle a class action about two phrases on the Bertolli label: imported from Italy, and extra virgin. The shopper alleged most of the oil was pressed outside Italy from olives grown outside Italy, and that the company's handling did not ensure the oil would still meet the extra virgin grade by the best-before date. Deoleo denied every allegation. The settlement agreement says, in its own words, that it shall not be used as an admission of any fault. The company had already begun dropping imported from Italy from its labels in December 2013 and agreed to keep them off. American case, American court, and we will say that plainly. But seven million dollars is what those two phrases cost in one country over eight years.

Then the staircase again, and Bertolli's is the longest on the shelf. Rich taste extra virgin, one litre, sixteen dollars at Loblaws. Extra light taste, one litre, sixteen dollars. Cooking olive oil, one litre, sixteen dollars. Twelve ninety-nine each at Superstore, twelve ninety-seven each at Walmart. Same bottle shape, same colours, shoulder to shoulder, and the extra light comes in a clear bottle. The ingredient line on the extra light and the cooking oil is refined olive oil and virgin olive oils. The price gap between the top rung and the bottom is zero dollars. That is why this one is number one. Not because the oil is fake, and not because anybody broke a law in this country. Because the shelf is designed so that reaching for the wrong rung feels like the same purchase, and it is not. And one last number: the one-litre extra virgin at sixteen dollars is a dollar sixty per hundred millilitres. The two-litre of the same oil at Walmart is eighty-five cents. Same brand, same grade, nearly double the price per millilitre for buying the smaller bottle.

That is the caution list. Now the two we would actually pull off the shelf, and the reason in each case is something you can check, not something you have to taste.

Worth it, number two. Terra Delyssa. Tunisian, single origin, and the words are printed on the front: traceable to the field, scan and trace, with a QR code. The company's own page says every bottle can be traced back to when and where the olives were harvested, milled, analysed and bottled. Dark glass. One litre, twenty dollars at Loblaws, sixteen at Maxi, sixteen seventy-nine at No Frills, eleven ninety-seven at Walmart this week, which is a dollar twenty per hundred. And a word about Tunisian oil, because there is an old snobbery about it that costs Canadians money. Tunisia's growers' chamber told Reuters last autumn the harvest was heading for a record five hundred thousand tonnes. When you buy Tunisian oil labelled as Tunisian oil, you are buying the crop without paying for somebody's Italian photograph. The one thing to check on camera: there is no printed harvest date on the label, the QR code does that job. Scan it in the aisle and read what comes up.

Worth it, number one. Kirkland Signature. Costco's own label, and we know how that sounds after everything just said about house brands. The reason is structural. Costco stocks one bottle per line, and every line names a country. One hundred percent Italian, two litres, thirty-one ninety-nine on costco.ca, product of Italy. One hundred percent Spanish, three litres, thirty-six ninety-nine, product of Spain. California, one litre, nineteen ninety-nine, product of USA, and on the front, certified by the Olive Oil Commission of California and maximum acidity 0.3 percent, which is a tighter number than the international standard requires. No staircase. Costco's own page says the Italian oil is certified by an independent third party, with an ISO sticker on each bottle. It does not name the certifier, so we will not either, until we film the sticker. Two honest caveats. The organic two-litre, twenty-one ninety-nine, names no country on Costco's page, only the Mediterranean region. And Costco also sells a three-litre refined jug at thirty ninety-nine that declares refined olive oil eighty-five percent, extra virgin fifteen percent, on the label. It is the only refined oil on this list priced below its own extra virgin, and it says what it is. The one thing you have to do to protect a big jug: decant it. A small dark bottle, a funnel, fill what you will use this month, keep the rest sealed away from the stove.

One small bottle deserves a mention. Irini, imported by Pilaros of Laval, twelve dollars for seven fifty at Loblaws. The front prints product of Greece, produit de la Grèce, and acidity zero to 0.8 percent. A label that states the country and the grade limit in both languages is doing the job the big brands leave to you.

Now the test you can do at your own counter in under a minute. Forget the fridge test. Cold makes some oils thicken and it cannot tell you a thing about honesty. Pour a spoonful into a small glass, cup your hand over it for thirty seconds, then put your nose in. The standard's taste panels are trained to catch three defects: rancid, fusty, musty. Real extra virgin should smell green, like cut grass. Then a small sip, and swallow. The bite at the back of the throat and the bitterness are what the grade is scored on. No smell, no bite, no bitterness, and you are holding an oil that lost its grade somewhere between the tree and your kitchen, whatever the front says.

The run-back. No name tells you nothing, which is honest, so read the grade word and notice the pure bottle costs the same. Great Value is priced right, so count the countries. President's Choice covers many bottles at many prices, so read the origin line, not the logo. Carapelli says one hundred percent Italian and is owned in Madrid, so hold it to the claim. Colavita sells a single-origin bottle and a blend that look the same, so buy the specific bottle. Filippo Berio sells a classic and a light beside its extra virgin at the same price. And Bertolli is a Spanish-owned company with an Italian name, a seven-million-dollar American settlement behind two phrases, and a staircase where every step costs the same. Terra Delyssa tells you the field. Kirkland tells you the country on every bottle and prices its refined oil like refined oil.

Here is the honest split, because best quality and best value are two different questions. Best quality goes to a single-origin bottle in dark glass from one named country, whatever the brand. Best value goes to the Kirkland two-litre or three-litre, decanted properly, or the Terra Delyssa litre when Walmart has it under twelve dollars.

And the bigger number to carry out of the store. Statistics Canada tracks the average price of a litre of olive oil across the country. It peaked at seventeen fourteen in June 2024. This July it was eleven sixteen, a third off the peak, because Spain's harvest came back. A litre of olive oil now costs about what three litres of canola costs. If the bottle in front of you is still priced like 2024, that is a decision the store made, not the market.

Three questions at the till. Where did the olives come from, and does the French line say the same? Is there a harvest date, or only a best-before? What is the price per hundred millilitres, and is the pure or light bottle beside it the same price? If the bottle cannot answer the first one, put it back.
