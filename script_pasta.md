Start with what Canada requires, because it's less than you think. The entire pasta section of the Food and Drug Regulations is two rules. One: you can't call it egg pasta unless it has at least four per cent egg-yolk solids. Two: you can't call it enriched unless it contains a specific table of added nutrients. That's it. Nothing says pasta has to be made from durum wheat. Nothing defines bronze die, or slow dried, or artisan. In Canada those words on a bag are marketing, and the regulator's rule for marketing is the general one: nothing false, misleading or likely to create an erroneous impression. So the two things you can verify on any pack sold here are the ingredient line and the origin line. Everything else is the company's word.

The origin line does have rules, and to be fair to the regulator, they're enforced. CFIA guidance says Product of Canada means all or virtually all of the ingredients, processing and labour are Canadian, with anything minor generally under two per cent. Made in Canada must carry a qualifier, from domestic and imported ingredients, or from imported ingredients. And in March this year the CFIA announced forty-seven thousand dollars in penalties since April twenty twenty-five against five businesses for misleading Canadian-origin claims, including two Loblaw-banner stores. None of those cases involved pasta. But it means the origin line is the one piece of pasta marketing that somebody is actually checking.

Now Italy, where the rule is stricter. Under Italy's pasta law, a product sold as pasta di semola di grano duro must be made from durum semolina and water, with soft wheat flour tolerated at no more than three per cent. And since twenty eighteen, pasta sold in Italy must state on the label the country where the wheat was grown and the country where it was milled. That rule was due to lapse and has been extended again, most recently by a decree published in February, through the thirty-first of December twenty twenty-six. Two cautions. It's a domestic Italian rule, with an export derogation in the law and a mutual-recognition clause, so whether the pack on a Canadian shelf still carries the wheat-origin line is a per-pack fact you read on the back. And it doesn't tell you the wheat is Italian.

Because a lot of it isn't. Canada is the world's largest exporter of durum wheat. In the twenty twenty-four to twenty-five crop year Canada exported over five point eight million tonnes, and Agriculture Canada puts Italy at sixteen per cent of that, about nine hundred thousand tonnes. UN trade data for calendar twenty twenty-four has Canada as Italy's number one durum supplier, by value and by volume, at seven hundred and seventy-four thousand tonnes, ahead of Greece, Turkey and the United States. And Italy needs it: the Italian pasta industry's own association says the country exported two point four six million tonnes of pasta last year, more than sixty per cent of everything it makes, and an Italian farm-sector analysis this month put Italy's self-sufficiency in durum at fifty-six and a half per cent. Those two are industry and press figures, not government ones, so treat them as the industry's account. The government numbers are the Canadian ones, and they say the same thing from the other side. The Canadian Grain Commission describes Canada Western Amber Durum as producing high semolina yield, bright yellow colour, and, its words, superior cooking quality of pasta. I'm not going to tell you the five dollar Italian pack in your hand is made from Canadian wheat, because that's a per-pack fact and I haven't read your pack. I'm telling you that the Italian pasta industry buys more durum from Canada than from anywhere else, and Italian law makes them print where it came from. If the line on the back says Canada, you paid five dollars for wheat that left Saskatchewan.

So what does the manufacturing actually change? Three things, and the science is clearer than the marketing.

First, the semolina. A peer-reviewed review in twenty twenty-two, co-authored by a scientist at the Canadian Grain Commission, says bright, speck-free semolina is required for the look of a premium product, and that finer grinding with fast drying can triple output. A separate review explains the trade-off: low ash and low starch damage come from a medium-low extraction rate and coarse particles, and a medium extraction rate means a lower milling yield. De Cecco's own site says it takes about sixty-five kilograms of semolina from a hundred kilograms of wheat where eighty is possible. That's the company talking, but it's consistent with the literature: coarse semolina costs more because you throw more of the wheat away.

Second, the die. Pasta is extruded through a plate. A Teflon-coated plate gives a smooth, bright surface. A bronze plate gives a rough one. The same review records what bronze costs the factory: lower extrusion pressure and speed, faster wear of the die, and a more porous strand that's twenty to thirty per cent weaker before cooking. So bronze pasta really is slower and more fragile to make, which is a real cost. Whether the rough surface holds sauce better is, in every source I read, the manufacturer's claim, not a measured Canadian result.

Third, the drying, and here's where the marketing runs backwards. Old-fashioned drying was around thirty to forty degrees over one to two days. Since the seventies the industry moved to seventy-five, then ninety degrees and above, in a few hours. A nineteen ninety-six North Dakota study dried spaghetti at forty, seventy-three and ninety degrees and measured what came out: cooking loss fell from seven point nine per cent to six point five, and firmness rose from four point seven to five point nine on their scale. Higher heat, firmer pasta, less starch in the water, at the cost of browning at the top end. A twenty twenty-two Italian survey of more than sixty supermarket pastas found a heat marker above the level associated with high-temperature drying in almost all industrial pasta, and, its words, surprisingly, even in some artisan pasta. And the bluntest line comes from De Cecco itself, in its own document on slow drying: there is no legal definition in Italy of high quality. So "slow dried" on a box is a claim you can't check, and the lab work says fast drying isn't the villain the box implies.

Italpasta Artisan won it this year, which is real, and it's an entry-fee award. Protégez-Vous in Quebec has a spaghetti comparison behind a paywall I couldn't open. The only Canadian university sensory study I found, at Guelph in twenty twelve, used a trained twelve-person panel on lab-made macaroni and found the drying method changed only one thing, perceived firmness, by less than half a point on a fifteen-centimetre scale. Italy's consumer magazine tested twenty spaghettis in January with a consumer tasting; that's the Italian market, so it stays in Italy. In Canada, on this question, the honest answer is nobody knows, and any video that tells you the cheap one tastes worse is guessing.

If you want the rough bronze surface, the cheapest place the claim appears on a Canadian label is Italpasta Artisan at around forty cents, and Loblaw's own PC spaghetti describes bronze dies too, so check that tag. You are not paying for a Canadian-verified difference in the bowl, because that verification doesn't exist.

The checklist fits on the back of your hand. Divide the price by the grams. Read the ingredient line. Read the origin line, and if it only says imported by, that's all the law asked for.
