Four days ago, on September 8th, the Government of Canada put a 50 percent tariff on American whey protein. Not on cars. Not on steel. On the powder in the jug sitting in your kitchen cupboard. It's on the official counter-tariff list from the Department of Finance, tariff item 0404.10.10, whey protein concentrate, right there between the dairy codes. And that's just the newest chapter in the strangest year protein powder has ever had in this country. A market intelligence firm called the global whey market a once-in-a-generation supply crisis. The Globe and Mail reported in July that high-protein whey prices have climbed 162 percent since the start of 2025. A Calgary supplement business owner told Global News, quote, literally no one wants to do business with us. If you're not ordering a million-dollar truckload, you can't even get a hold of someone.

And while all of that is happening, the biggest sports nutrition company this country ever produced, the one from Oakville, Ontario whose tubs fill an entire wall at every Walmart in Canada, quietly went through creditor protection, had its Chinese owner wiped out to zero, and got bought by a group of investors hiding behind a numbered company. As of today, nobody outside that deal can tell you who owns MuscleTech.

So today we're investigating nine protein powder brands sold in Canada, in order of who dominates the shelf the most. Who actually owns them. What the label math says when you calculate the real price per gram of protein. Why the tax on that jug is higher than the tax on the bottle of Ensure beside it, and the exact federal wording that makes it legal. By the end, you'll know which three brands are actually Canadian, and you'll hear how the entire modern protein industry traces back to two brothers and a garage in Montreal. Nobody paid for a spot on this list, nobody sponsored this video, and every number here was checked against a printed label, a court record, or a government document, priced at the time of research. Your shelf may vary, and I want to hear about it in the comments.

Number one. Optimum Nutrition.

Gold Standard 100% Whey. The black and gold tub. If protein powder has a default in this country, this is it. Optimum Nutrition's own parent company calls Gold Standard, quote, the world's best-selling whey protein powder. Walk into any Popeye's Supplements, any Walmart, scroll Amazon, there it is. So here's the question this channel always asks first. Who owns it?

Ireland does. Optimum Nutrition belongs to Glanbia, a public company headquartered in Kilkenny, Ireland, and Glanbia's origin story is genuinely the stuff of a farming documentary. In 1964, five Irish farmer co-operatives joined together to form what eventually became Glanbia. An Irish dairy co-op. In 2008, Glanbia's own corporate timeline says it made, quote, a decision to vertically integrate with the acquisition of one of its customers, the US sports supplement firm Optimum Nutrition. Then it kept collecting. BSN in 2011. Isopure in 2014, for 153 million US dollars. So when you compare Optimum, BSN and Isopure at the shelf, you are choosing between three labels owned by the same Irish dairy company. By 2024, Optimum Nutrition alone was 66 percent of Glanbia's entire performance nutrition revenue. The best-selling whey in Canada is, at the end of the chain, Irish milk money.

Now the label math. At the time of research, a five pound tub of Gold Standard was listed at SupplementSource for 114 dollars and 99 cents on sale, regular price 159.95. Seventy-three servings, 24 grams of declared protein each. Run the math on the sale price and you're paying about 2 dollars and 47 cents for every 30 grams of protein. Remember that number, because this video has a price-per-protein scoreboard and the winner is going to surprise you. One more detail before we move on. At one major Canadian retailer, the Gold Standard tub is now listed not as five pounds, but as 4.65 pounds. Same tub shape. Check the weight printed on yours.

Number two. Kirkland Signature.

Costco's house whey, the 2.45 kilogram bag, launched in 2024, and the Costco crowd treated it like an event. At 25 grams of declared protein per 35 gram scoop, roughly 70 servings a bag, it is priced to embarrass everyone else in this video. Depending on your warehouse price, the math lands around a dollar twenty per 30 grams of protein, roughly half the price of the brands it sits near. And in late 2025 Costco Canada added a Kirkland protein shake, 18 bottles for 29.99, pricing one industry publication said, quote, destroys other high-value competitors like Premier Protein.

But here's what we can't tell you about the bag, and we investigated. Who makes it? Costco doesn't say. When we investigated vitamins on this channel, federal recall notices eventually revealed exactly which Winnipeg factory makes Kirkland vitamins, because the manufacturer's name appears on the recall paperwork. Kirkland whey has no such paper trail. No recall, no disclosure, no name. The fine print on Costco's own website says Costco obtains its label information from its suppliers. Plural. So the honest answer to who makes Canada's best-value protein powder is: an American retailer knows, and you don't. The value is real. The transparency isn't there.

Number three. Six Star and MuscleTech.

Two brands, one company, and the wildest ownership story in this entire investigation. Six Star is the blue tub wall at Walmart. MuscleTech is the shelf above it. Both belong to Iovate Health Sciences, headquartered at 381 North Service Road West in Oakville, Ontario. This company started in 1995 as MuscleTech Research and Development, a Canadian mail-order supplement business, and grew into one of the biggest sports nutrition companies on the planet. A genuine Canadian giant.

Then, 2016. A Chinese conglomerate called Xiwang Foodstuffs, based in Shandong Province, bought Iovate from a Toronto family trust for 730 million Canadian dollars. The law firm on the deal published the number. Iovate's own press release promised the headquarters would stay in Oakville, with all 330 employees in place. And for nine years, the biggest sports nutrition company headquartered in Canada was majority Chinese-owned, and almost nobody buying a tub of Six Star at Walmart in Moose Jaw had any idea.

Then it fell apart, and every step of this is public court record. In April 2024, a US court awarded a competitor, Orgain, a judgment against Iovate that grew to twelve and a half million US dollars, and Orgain got legal permission to garnish about 8 million US dollars from Walmart, one of Iovate's biggest customers. Iovate then defaulted on a loan with the Royal Bank of Canada. By September 2025 the company filed for creditor protection, and on October 31st, 2025 it entered the CCAA process at the Ontario Superior Court of Justice. And here is the ending. In the restructuring approved this spring, all existing shares were cancelled for no consideration. That's the actual legal wording. Xiwang paid 730 million dollars in 2016 and walked away in 2026 with nothing. On May 27th of this year, Iovate was purchased out of creditor protection by a consortium of buyers acting through a newly formed company called 1001542267 Ontario Inc. A numbered company. The buyers have not been publicly named.

So who owns MuscleTech and Six Star today? Officially, a number. Through the entire saga, a Toronto family trust, a Chinese conglomerate, an insolvency court, a mystery consortium, the tubs on the Walmart shelf never changed. Same label, same maple-leaf-adjacent muscle branding. The label is the only thing that stayed Canadian.

One more thing on Iovate, and we're going to say this precisely, because precision is the whole point of this channel. Back in 2015, Iovate paid two and a half million US dollars to settle an American class action alleging that protein counts on some US-sold products were boosted by counting nitrogen from added amino acids and creatine, a practice the lawsuits called protein spiking. That was a settlement, and a settlement is not a finding of wrongdoing. As part of it, coverage reported the company agreed to modify its testing and labelling so that nitrogen from non-protein ingredients is not included in the protein calculation. And to be completely fair to the entire industry, an industry legal review published just last month put it plainly: no court in that whole wave of protein lawsuits has ever ruled, on the merits, that a product actually contained less protein than its label said. Allegations, settlements, procedural rulings. Not one final verdict on the powder itself. Keep that sentence in mind any time you see a scary lab-test screenshot online.

The tax rule that makes your jug dearer than a meal in a bottle, the tariff wall with a protein-shaped hole in it, and the two brothers from Montreal who built this entire industry. Number four. Vega.

The plant-based one. The brand every Canadian grocery store puts at eye level, the one whose website says, in capital letters, rooted in Canada, powered by plants. And the roots are real. Vega was born in Burnaby, British Columbia, in the early 2000s, founded by Charles Chang together with Canadian endurance athlete Brendan Brazier, under a company called Sequel Naturals. A genuine BC startup, built on pea protein instead of whey.

Now follow the money. In 2015, the founders sold Vega to the American food company WhiteWave for approximately 550 million US dollars in cash. In 2017, the French dairy giant Danone swallowed WhiteWave whole, and Vega with it. And in 2021, Danone sold Vega again, this time to WM Partners, a private equity firm in Florida. Three sales in six years. Today the brand whose website says, quote, from the beginning of Vega, our Canadian roots run deep, is owned by American private equity. And read the manufacturing line on that same page carefully, because it's written like a lawyer held the pen: quote, a majority of our Canadian products are manufactured in Canada. A majority. Where the rest is made, the page doesn't say.

For the record, and purely as a matter of public record: in July 2025, one lot of Vega Organic Protein and Supergreens vanilla was recalled in Canada due to pieces of plastic, a Class 3 recall notice filed by Sequel Naturals covering online sales in four provinces. Routine, factual, on the federal recalls database for anyone to read. We mention it because that recall notice is also the only public document where you can still see the original Burnaby-era company name doing the paperwork.

Number five and number six. Premier Protein and Dymatize.

We're doing these two together, because they're the same company, and almost nobody knows it. Premier Protein is the Costco pallet of shake cartons, 18 bottles of chocolate, 30 grams of protein each. Dymatize ISO100 is the premium hydrolyzed isolate the gym crowd pays extra for, around 179.99 for a 2.3 kilogram tub on Amazon at the time of research, about 2 dollars 84 per 30 grams of protein. Different aisles, different customers, one owner: BellRing Brands, an American company spun out of the cereal giant Post Holdings. Post bought Premier Nutrition in 2013, bought Dymatize in 2014, and spun them both out as BellRing in a deal completed in 2022. The budget shake and the premium isolate report to the same shareholders in Missouri.

And Premier Protein is the perfect door into the strangest thing we found in this entire investigation. The tax.

Here is the rule, and every word of this comes from the Canada Revenue Agency's own Basic Groceries memorandum. Groceries in Canada are zero-rated. No GST, no HST. But CRA's memorandum, paragraph 148, says products commonly referred to as dietary supplements, quote, are not considered to be basic groceries, end quote, and are taxable. Paragraph 150 names protein, in powder form, explicitly, right in the list. The CRA even wrote out a worked example, and you can tell someone at the agency had fun with it. It describes a flavoured protein isolate powder that you mix with 500 millilitres of milk, and it concludes that even though, quote, the resulting mixture has an appearance, texture and taste similar to that of a milkshake, it is not an ingredient to a beverage. The powder, in CRA's words, is added to milk as a means of ingestion. An average consumer, says the government of Canada, would not purchase this product as a food.

So your jug of whey is taxed like a supplement. Thirteen percent HST in Ontario. But paragraph 160 of the same memorandum says meal replacement products and nutritional supplements that qualify under the Food and Drug Regulations are, quote, considered to be zero-rated basic groceries. That's the category Ensure and Boost live in. Which produces this checkout: a 60 dollar jug of protein powder in Ontario takes 7 dollars and 80 cents of tax. In New Brunswick, Newfoundland or PEI, 9 dollars. In Alberta, 3 dollars. Sixty dollars of Ensure, in every single province: zero. The bottle that says meal replacement rides tax-free while the jug beside it pays full freight. And the rabbit hole keeps going. A single ready-to-drink shake under 600 millilitres is generally taxable, the multi-pack of the same shake can be zero-rated, and a qualifying meal replacement is zero-rated at any size, except, and I promise this is real, from a vending machine, where even Ensure becomes taxable. There is even a CRA ruling from 2003 about a protein bar that was taxed as candy, because it was promoted as an alternative to candy and had, quote, a coating that makes the bar similar in texture and substance to a chocolate bar. Photograph your receipts, Canada. The supplement aisle is a tax map, and nobody handed you the legend.

Number seven. Allmax.

The IsoFlex brand, out of North York, Ontario, and one Canadian retailer bills IsoFlex as Canada's number one selling isolate, which is the retailer's marketing claim, not an audited number, so take it as shelf-talk. What we can quote is Allmax's own About page: proudly Canadian owned and operated, a proud Canadian company established in 1998, committed to developing, formulating, researching and testing all of its products in Canada, with, quote, some ingredients sourced internationally, due to their unavailability within Canada. Read that wording the way this channel reads all wording. Developing, formulating, researching, testing. The page commits to where the thinking happens. It does not say where every tub is manufactured. That's not an accusation. It's just what the sentence chooses not to promise. On the official record, we found no Canadian or American regulatory action against Allmax products, and that deserves saying plainly too, because in this industry a clean recall record is worth something.

Number eight. PVL and MUTANT.

Now the good news, because this story has actual heroes. In Port Coquitlam, British Columbia, there's a company called Fit Foods, founded in 1996, that owns PVL, MUTANT, Whey Gourmet and North Coast Naturals. Family founded, run by the McMahon family, roughly 200 employees, and here's the part that matters: they manufacture in their own facility, over 200,000 square feet of it, just outside Vancouver. PVL's own page says it: family-owned and operated, every single product formulated, manufactured and distributed in-house, proudly Canadian. The only outside money we found in the chain is an investment from Westcap, which is based in Saskatoon. Canadian brand, Canadian family, Canadian plant, Canadian investor. You can walk into a Superstore, pick up a tub of PVL IsoGold, 27 grams of declared isolate per scoop, and every link of that chain stays in this country. After seven brands of Irish co-ops, numbered companies and Florida private equity, that sentence felt strange to type.

Number nine. Kaizen Naturals.

The Walmart-shelf Canadian. Kaizen belongs to TWC Nutrition, The Winning Combination, a manufacturer on Wilkes Avenue in Winnipeg, Manitoba, founded in 1999, which also owns Bodylogix. Again: own brand, own factory, own country. And since the countdown only has nine slots, let me give honourable mentions to the rest of the actually-Canadian roster, because this list is short enough to memorize. Believe Supplements, founded 2016 by Maxime Germain and Julien Haroun, who design, manufacture and test in-house in Saint-Jérôme, Quebec. Canadian Protein in Windsor, Ontario, which does something almost nobody else in this video does: it tells you where the milk comes from. Its New Zealand whey isolate page says exactly that, sourced from pasture-raised cows in New Zealand, packaged in Ontario, 89.99 a kilogram at the time of research. A Canadian company being honest that its whey is imported. Hold that thought, because it's time to talk about why almost all of it is.

Here's the machine behind the entire shelf. Whey is not really a product. It's what's left over when you make cheese. And in Canada, milk, and therefore cheese, and therefore whey, is produced under supply management, the quota system. There is only so much Canadian whey, because there is only so much Canadian cheese. So the industry imports, and this is where Canada's tariff schedule does something almost poetic. Bring ordinary powdered whey into Canada above the quota and the tariff is 208 percent, but not less than 2 dollars and 7 cents per kilogram. That's the dairy wall, same family of tariffs that protects milk and cheese. But whey protein concentrate has its own tariff line at under 5 cents per kilogram. And whey protein isolate, the expensive stuff, 80 percent protein or higher, isn't even classified as dairy at all. It ships under tariff item 3502.20 as milk albumin, and from the United States it entered duty-free. The more protein in the powder, the easier it walked past the wall. For years, the loophole was the supply chain.

Until four days before this video. On September 8th, 2026, Canada's counter-tariffs against the United States came into force, and the list, published by the Department of Finance, reads like the ingredients panel of your jug: whey protein concentrate, 50 percent. Powdered whey, 50 percent. Milk albumin, including concentrates of two or more whey proteins, 50 percent. Milk protein substances, 50 percent. Last year, American producers shipped about 12,800 tonnes of whey protein concentrate to Canada. That door now has a 50 percent toll on it, in the middle of a record whey market, with food-grade whey at all-time-high prices in Europe. Much of the whey in Canadian jugs starts as foreign milk, and the price of that arrangement just changed. If your tub costs more this winter, you now know the paper trail.

And one more shelf note while you're out there. When GNC went bankrupt in 2020, it closed 29 stores in Canada, and the chain itself was sold to Harbin Pharmaceutical Group of China. Meanwhile Popeye's Supplements, founded in 1989 as, by its own account, the very first sports nutrition retail store in Canada, now counts more than 140 locations coast to coast. The American chain retreated. The Canadian one is still growing. Sometimes the most Canadian brand in the supplement aisle is the store.

Which brings us to the ending this story deserves. Because here's what almost nobody standing in that aisle knows. The aisle itself is Canadian.

In 1936, in the family garage on Coloniale Street in Montreal, a teenager named Joe Weider started building barbells out of car wheels and axles. In 1940, Joe and his younger brother Ben began publishing a little magazine called Your Physique. In 1946, the brothers rented the Monument National theatre in Montreal, held the first Mr. Canada contest, and founded the International Federation of BodyBuilders that same night, an organization that grew to 139 member nations. In 1965 they created a contest you may have heard of, Mr. Olympia. In 1968, they brought a young Austrian bodybuilder named Arnold Schwarzenegger to America. Arnold's own words, when Joe died in 2013: he was the godfather of fitness, who didn't just inspire my earliest dreams, he made them come true the day he invited me to move to America. Joe even helped get Arnold his first movie role, reportedly by telling the producers his bodybuilder was a German Shakespearean actor. And the Weider supplement line was there at the beginning of it all. Their biggest seller, Crash Weight Gain Formula Number 7, a can of powder you mixed with a quart of whole milk. Sound familiar? That is the great-grandfather of every tub in this video, and it came from Montreal.

The empire, like everything else in this story, was eventually sold to Americans. The magazines, Muscle and Fitness, Flex, Shape, went to a US publisher in 2003 for 350 million dollars. The nutrition company became Schiff, and the Weider brand itself ended up, of all places, under the same ownership as Optimum Nutrition. But the brothers' endings tell you who they were. Joe died in Los Angeles at 93, the American dream he manufactured for everyone else. Ben never left. He got the Order of Canada in 1975. He spent his fortune proving a theory that Napoleon was poisoned, wrote books about it, and three weeks before he died in Montreal in 2008, in the city where he was born, he donated his entire Napoleon collection, more than 60 artifacts, to the Montreal Museum of Fine Arts. The industry was invented in a Montreal garage, incorporated in a Montreal theatre, and powered by a can of powder and a quart of milk. The brands, one by one, went to Ireland, to Missouri, to Florida, to Shandong Province, to a numbered company in Ontario. The industry is ours. The brands mostly aren't anymore.

So here's the scoreboard. Best value on the shelf, by pure label math: Kirkland, at roughly half the price per gram of protein of the big names, if you can live with not knowing who makes it. Most transparent label in Canada: Canadian Protein in Windsor, the only brand in this video that voluntarily names the country its whey comes from. And the all-Canadian podium, the only chains where the brand, the owner and the factory all stay in this country: PVL and the Fit Foods family in Port Coquitlam, Kaizen and TWC in Winnipeg, Believe in Saint-Jérôme. Three provinces, three factories, still ours.
