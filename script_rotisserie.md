# SCRIPT — ROTISSERIE CHICKEN EPISODE (post date: September 14, 2026)

TITLE: We Investigated 9 Rotisserie Chickens From Costco, Loblaws, Walmart & More (Cooked Costs LESS Than Raw)

ALTERNATES:
- We Priced Every Rotisserie Chicken In Canada (The Cooked One Costs LESS Than Raw)
- Why A Cooked Chicken Costs Less Than A Raw One In Canada (We Investigated All 9 Stores)

---

In April 2022, CBC's Cost of Living program did something simple at a Calgary Costco. They put a rotisserie chicken on a scale. Seven ninety-nine, already seasoned, already roasted, one point three five kilograms of cooked bird. Then they walked twenty feet to the meat cooler, where raw whole chickens were six forty-nine a kilogram. Do the math CBC did: to take that same bird home raw, you'd have paid about ten dollars and sixty cents. The cooked one cost seven ninety-nine. Buying the uncooked chicken — no seasoning, no roasting, no bag — cost thirty-three percent more per kilogram than buying the finished one. That is not a sale. That is a strategy. And when CBC asked Loblaws, Sobeys, Save-On-Foods, Costco and Metro to explain their chicken pricing, every single one of them declined to comment.

So we went looking ourselves. We priced every rotisserie chicken sold in Canada — Costco, Loblaws and Superstore, Walmart, Sobeys, Metro, Save-On-Foods, IGA, the premium grocers, and one chain that turns out to be hiding its price entirely — read every label we could get our hands on, weighed the claims against Statistics Canada's own price tables, and pulled the federal documents that explain what's actually going on: a pricing playbook executives have admitted to on the record, in their own words; a law that decides which chickens have to show you an ingredient list and which legally don't; and a tax rule at the till that almost nobody knows, which can change which chicken you should buy while you're standing there. Along the way we found the four-price family — one company selling what looks like the same chicken at four different prices under four different banners — and the seventeen-dollar delivery-app version of a thirteen-dollar bird. Stay to the end for the three winners — best value, best label, and the sleeper nobody talks about. We read every label. That's all we do.

First, the rules of our game, because this episode runs on arithmetic, not opinions. Rule one: the price per bird is marketing; the price per hundred grams of cooked chicken is the truth, because these birds are not the same size — not even close. Rule two: we read what's printed and nothing else. And rule three, straight from the federal labelling rules: a chicken roasted right there in the store must show you a net weight and a packaging date on the label — but it's legally exempt from carrying an ingredient list. That's not a scandal; it's how the regulations are written for food made on the premises. It does mean the hot counter plays by different rules than the rest of the store, and it means the labels we can read are worth reading carefully.

Number one: Costco.

The Kirkland Signature Seasoned Rotisserie Chicken. In the United States this bird is a legend — four ninety-nine, unchanged since 2009, and the company's longtime chief financial officer, Richard Galanti, explained why in language you rarely hear from an executive. In 2015 he told analysts, quote: when others were raising their chicken prices from four ninety-nine to five ninety-nine, we were willing to eat, if you will, thirty to forty million dollars a year in gross margin by keeping it at four ninety-nine. And then, four words that explain this entire episode: that is what we do for a living. In 2023 he called the chicken an investment in low prices to drive membership. Costco sold one hundred and fifty-seven point four million of these birds worldwide last fiscal year, by its own investor communications.

The price wasn't always sacred, mind you — it briefly hit five ninety-nine during the 2008 financial crisis, then came back down and never moved again. Galanti in 2014, on holding while competitors climbed: as prices changed dramatically and we saw the competition raising the price, it was a hot price. And the volumes grew like a legend: ninety-one million birds in 2018, about a hundred million in 2019, one hundred and six million in 2021, and one hundred and fifty-seven million last year. Somewhere on earth, roughly three hundred Costco chickens have been sold since this video started.

Canada's version costs more — seven ninety-nine to about nine dollars depending on when and where you check, per reporting this year — but hold it against the rest of this list. The label says one point two kilograms, which works out to roughly sixty-seven cents per hundred grams of cooked chicken. Remember that number: sixty-seven cents. It's about to make everything else in this video look expensive. Even against raw chicken it holds up: Statistics Canada's national average for a raw whole chicken this July was seven sixty-two a kilogram — meaning an average-sized raw bird costs twelve to thirteen dollars before you've turned on your oven. CBC ran the same comparison the rigorous way in 2022 and got four dollars and eighty-eight cents per raw-equivalent kilogram for the cooked bird, against six forty-nine for raw at the same warehouse. The famous American chicken plant story, by the way — Costco building its own four hundred and fifty million dollar poultry operation in Nebraska to protect the price, two million birds a week — is an American story; Canadian birds come through Canada's own quota system, which we'll get to, because it's half the answer to this whole mystery. Two more pieces of Costco lore worth checking on camera: American reporting says underweight birds are sold at a lower price or pulled for the ready-to-eat counter — a weight floor on a five-dollar chicken — and Canada actually got the flame-printed bag before the Americans did; when the US switched from clamshells to bags in 2024 and customers revolted, Canadians pointed out we'd been living the bag life for over a year.

What's in it? Costco told CBC News directly, in a statement: we use carrageenan and sodium phosphate to support moisture retention, texture and product consistency during cooking — both permitted additives in Canada. The photographed Canadian label lists 350 milligrams of sodium per hundred grams; we'll read the current bag on camera, along with the time sticker — American reporting says every bird is time-stamped out of the oven and pulled after two hours, and that employees ring a bell when a fresh batch lands. We'll be standing at a Canadian warehouse to see both for ourselves.

Number two: Loblaws and Real Canadian Superstore.

The President's Choice BBQ chicken at Loblaws: thirteen dollars. Sounds comparable until the scale comes out — it's a nine hundred gram bird. That's a dollar forty-four per hundred grams: more than double Costco per gram of actual chicken. And here's where it gets interesting, because this is all one company: Real Canadian Superstore sells a halal rotisserie bird, a full kilogram, for ten ninety-nine. Maxi in Quebec — also Loblaw — was documented at nine ninety-nine. And a Toronto outlet that compared the chains last year noted the same chicken sells for significantly less at No Frills. Same corporate parent, four different prices. The PC label, per the transcriptions we could find, is a short one — chicken, salt, spices, acetic acid, citric acid, mustard flavour, spice extracts — a genuinely different recipe from Costco's, and we'll count the ingredients on camera. One honesty note this episode insists on: this same week, a raw PC whole chicken was fifteen dollars on the flyer. So per bird, the cooked one wins. Per kilogram, the raw bird — typically almost twice the size — wins. Both things are true, and we'll say that at every store where it's the case.

Number three: Walmart.

Two birds here. The Your Fresh Market barbecue bird: eight eighty-seven for eight hundred grams — which makes it the second-cheapest chicken in Canada per bird, and quietly one of the better per-gram deals outside the warehouse club. And a bigger one point two kilogram version at fourteen ninety-seven. Notice the name on the label: Roasted Seasoned Chicken. Seasoned is not a mood — it's a regulated word on Canadian poultry, and it's the little flag that tells you salt and seasoning solutions are part of this product's story. Walmart doesn't publish the ingredient panel or sodium online, so the label read happens in the store.

A word on that Loblaws bird: a Toronto outlet's taste test last year gave it a perfect ten out of ten — crispy skin, moist meat, a container with a handle. Lovely. We don't do taste on this channel, because taste can't be verified and math can. What we can verify is that ten-out-of-ten chicken costs a hundred and fourteen percent more per gram than the warehouse bird.

Number four: Sobeys.

Twelve ninety-nine for a one-kilogram BBQ bird — a dollar thirty per hundred grams — plus half-chicken options, hot and cold, for smaller households. But credit where due: Sobeys' own delivery site publishes an actual nutrition panel for its hot oven-roasted chicken, and the printed number is striking — 110 milligrams of sodium per hundred grams, five percent of a day's worth. On the published figures we could find, that is the lowest sodium number of any rotisserie bird in the country. As printed, with their own disclaimer that the package is the final word — so the package is what we'll read. And per bird, the honest split again: a typical one point seven kilogram raw chicken at the national average runs about twelve ninety-five. Against the twelve ninety-nine cooked bird, that one's a photo finish.

Number five: Metro. Twelve ninety-nine for the Fresh 2 Go hot BBQ chicken — and no published weight at all, which means Metro meets the kitchen scale on camera. For what it's worth, a media taste test caught this bird at eleven ninety-nine in 2023; the price has crept, quietly.

Halfway point — and if the per-hundred-gram math is already changing how you'll shop this week, that like button tells YouTube to show this to the next Canadian standing at a hot counter wondering why the cooked bird is cheaper than the raw one. Four stores to go, then the tax secret and the winners.

Number six: Save-On-Foods. The western contender: thirteen ninety-nine for the Kitchen rotisserie bird, fourteen ninety-nine for the lemon herb, roasted in-store daily and sold hot from around eleven in the morning to seven at night — freshness by schedule, which is honest, and a schedule worth knowing if you like your bird straight off the spit. Weight unpublished — scale, camera, receipt.

Number seven: IGA. Quebec's bird was documented at twelve ninety-nine for nine hundred and fifty grams — and in the Quebec comparison test, it was the only chicken labelled as a product of Quebec. In a supply-managed industry, that little label is a story all by itself.

Number eight: the premium aisle — Longo's and Farm Boy. Longo's lists its rotisserie-style chicken at thirteen ninety-nine — with Raised Without Antibiotics printed right in the product name, a claim we report as the company's claim. Here's the beat your wallet needs: the same Longo's bird on a delivery app is seventeen fifty-nine. Sit with that. The whole point of this chicken — the reason it's priced below cost — is to get your body into the building where the rest of your basket lives. Order it through an app and you've paid a twenty-five percent premium to defeat the one part of the deal that favoured you. The app markup on a loss-leader chicken is almost the price of a second chicken. Farm Boy's price isn't published anywhere we could verify — we'll read the shelf tag on camera.

Number nine: Whole Foods. The most secretive rotisserie chicken in Canada. No Canadian price published online. Anywhere. We checked every channel — the American pages exist, the Canadian price doesn't. So the shelf tag at Whole Foods gets its close-up, and we'd bet our thumbnail it's the most expensive bird in this video. And two absences worth reporting: Giant Tiger, it turns out, has no rotisserie program at all — their cooked chicken is packaged, not hot-counter. And Calgary Co-op deserves a salute: when CBC came asking, they were the only retailer in Canada that explained itself on the record. Their spokesperson, Barb Munro, said it plainly: our rotisserie chickens are priced the way they are as a convenience to our members... our members are likely going to fill their baskets with other items. There it is. From the source.

Which brings us to the why — and none of this is speculation, because the industry has said it out loud.

Rowe Farms, the Ontario grocer, told CBC these chickens are a loss leader — sold below cost to get you in the store. A Rutgers business professor put the mechanism in one sentence: very few people simply buy the chicken and leave. They probably shop for other items that provide higher profit margins. A University of Guelph food economist, Andreas Boecker, explained the psychology: when people are under time stress, they take a lot of mental shortcuts — and they often do not do price comparisons. And is any of this against the rules? No — and we'll say so clearly: selling below cost to win customers is lawful in Canada; the Competition Bureau's own guidelines say vigorous competition on the merits — offering lower prices — is exactly what the law wants. The chicken is legal. The chicken is bait. Both true.

There's a second reason the cooked bird seems impossibly cheap, and it's hiding in plain sight on the scale: the birds are smaller. Rowe Farms again, on the record: raw chickens in the cooler run one point six to one point eight kilograms; the birds destined for the spit are about one point two. Casey Owens, a professor of poultry science, explained why to CBC: the ovens need uniform birds — a larger carcass may not reach temperature in the right amount of time, so uniform size matters for food safety purposes. Costco's own head of fresh foods said the same thing about their supply: we couldn't take a seven-pound bird or an eight-pound bird and make it work. So the eight-dollar chicken isn't just cheap; it's a smaller chicken than the one you're comparing it to in your head. That's the honest half of the paradox, and now you know it. And while you're at the counter, two label details worth your eyes, both required or defined by federal rules: every bird roasted in-store must carry a packaging date and a net weight — check both — and if you ever see the words previously frozen, that's not the store being chatty; that declaration is the law.

And behind all of it sits the most Canadian layer of the story: supply management. Chicken is this country's number one meat — thirty-five kilograms per person a year of production, roughly double beef — a four-billion-dollar-a-year farm sector grown by 2,834 licensed farmers. Chicken Farmers of Canada describes its own system in three pillars: production planning, import control, and producer pricing. The directors meet every eight weeks to decide how much chicken the country will grow; farmers collectively negotiate a minimum farm-gate price based on the cost of production; and the border does the rest — the Customs Tariff charges 238 percent on over-quota imported whole chicken, and 249 percent on imported cooked chicken, which is why nobody is trucking cheap rotisserie birds across the border. The industry's own pitch is that this delivers quality chicken at reasonable prices without government subsidies — their words, and your grocery receipt gets a vote. That's why the American four ninety-nine chicken doesn't exist here, why Statistics Canada's raw whole chicken price has climbed 48 percent since January 2017 — five fifteen a kilo then, seven sixty-two now, with chicken breasts up 29 percent over the same stretch — and why the eight-dollar Canadian bird, in context, is the retailers absorbing the difference to keep you walking through the door. One more label while we're here: Canada Grade A on a chicken grades the carcass — its shape, its skin, its lack of blemishes. The regulations grade how the bird looks. Nobody grades how it tastes.

Now the tax secret, because this one will genuinely change how some of you buy chicken. Federal law zero-rates basic groceries — your raw chicken has never been taxed. But the Excise Tax Act carves out, quote, food or beverages heated for consumption. And the Canada Revenue Agency's own guidance spells out what that means at the deli counter, nearly word for word: if it's sold from a heated cabinet, it's taxable. But — same document — if the cooked food is cold and sold from refrigerated shelves to be reheated at home, it's zero-rated. Read that again. The hot chicken pays tax. The identical chicken, cooled down in the next cooler over, does not. In Ontario, on a nine-dollar bird, that's about a dollar seventeen of HST that the cold chicken skips; in the Atlantic provinces it's more like a dollar thirty-five; in Alberta, forty-five cents of GST. And no, Ontario's famous tax break on prepared food under four dollars doesn't save you — that rebate stops at four dollars total, and your chicken blew past that line sometime around 2009. The cheapest rotisserie chicken in the store might be the cold one nobody looks at — and your till receipt, which we'll film at every store, is the proof. One footnote for the record: for two months last winter, during the federal tax holiday on prepared foods, the hot bird briefly rode tax-free too. It's back on the bill now.

Zoom out, and you see why every chain fights this fight: ninety-two percent of Canadians bought a grocery-store meal in the past year — about 420 million meals, three point six billion dollars' worth, averaging eight fifty-five a meal, by NielsenIQ's count. Eight fifty-five: almost exactly the price of these chickens. The cooked-chicken category is still growing, and when Mintel asked Canadians which prepared meal they actually buy, the answer, quote, by a substantial margin: roast chicken. A NielsenIQ vice-president described the rotisserie bird as a staple that drives a lot of traffic. This bird isn't a menu item. It's the entire front line of the grocery war.

And it didn't start with supermarkets. Canada has been lining up for spinning chicken for seventy-five years. In 1951, Hélène and René Léger opened the first St-Hubert on Saint-Hubert Street in Montreal — because, as Radio-Canada tells it, they'd seen the Sunday evening lineups already forming outside the Plateau's rotisseries — and a year later their fleet of yellow Beetles was delivering chicken to Montreal doorsteps, free home delivery, a first in Canada by the company's own account. By Expo 67 they claim they served eighteen thousand meals in a single day. In 1954, Swiss Chalet fired up its spit over an open flame on Bloor Street in Toronto and built English Canada's Sunday-dinner institution on it. Those two empires merged under one owner in 2016 for 537 million dollars — we told that story in our restaurant chains video. The supermarkets didn't invent Canada's chicken ritual. They just put it by the checkout and priced it at eight dollars.

So, the winners — three of them, for three kinds of shopper, all decided by the published numbers and nothing else. Best value in Canada, and it isn't close: Costco, at roughly sixty-seven cents per hundred grams on published figures — half the price per gram of nearly every competitor, cheaper than the average raw bird, and, on the published weights, the biggest bird on the list too. The cheapest chicken in Canada is also the largest one; that sentence shouldn't be possible, and it's the whole loss-leader strategy in eleven words. Best printed label number: Sobeys' hot oven-roasted chicken, at 110 milligrams of sodium per hundred grams as published — a third of the Costco label's printed figure, read your package to confirm. And the sleeper: Walmart's eight eighty-seven barbecue bird — the second-cheapest chicken in the country, hiding in plain sight with no fanfare at all; smaller bird, honest price. If your household is one or two people, that eight-hundred-gram bird might quietly be the rightest-sized deal in the whole store — and don't forget the cold-counter tax trick works at every one of these chains. The one thing no store will hand you is the full picture — so here's your homework, and it's the fun kind: drop the price of your local rotisserie chicken in the comments with your city, and let's build the real Canadian rotisserie chicken map, coast to coast. The next time that warm bag hits your cart, you'll know the whole story: a smaller bird than it looks, priced below cost on purpose, perfectly legally, by people who told the CBC exactly what they were doing — because the real product was never the chicken.
