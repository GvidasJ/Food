## 2. The rules

Every rule below is present in at least eight of the twelve scripts. Quotes are verbatim from the transcripts.

### Rule 1 — Open on the viewer's own habit, in the second person, then turn

The first sentence is never about the brand. It is about something the viewer did this morning.

- "You make yourself a coffee every single morning, but do you know what's actually in your cup?"
- "Ever sit down at a steakhouse in Canada, glance at the menu, see $58 for a ribeye and think 'This better be life-changing.'"
- "Have you ever walked into Dollarama for one quick thing and somehow walked out with a basket full of stuff you didn't even know you needed?"
- "Ground beef is the most purchased protein in Canada. It is in your spaghetti, your burgers, your tacos."

Then the pivot word, always within the first four sentences: "But here's the uncomfortable question most Canadians never ask."

### Rule 2 — Stack three to five teases before the first item, none of them naming a brand

This is the single most consistent device. The viewer is given the shocks as a list, stripped of the names, so they have to stay to attach them.

- "Glass found in jars, brands you thought were competing secretly owned by the same company, a Canadian icon sold off to foreign investors for nearly a billion dollars, cheap beans, no testing, plastic in your cup every morning."
- "One chain serves burgers so thin customers thought they were embarrassed to see them amongst so many vegetables. Another microwaves pre-cooked patties right before they hit your tray. And one ranks dead last with a 2.1 out of five."
- "Dollarama raised its price ceiling by 400%. Walmart quietly swapped name brands for cheaper private labels. Loblaw got caught fixing bread prices for 20 years."

### Rule 3 — Say the count out loud, exactly as the title says it, then plant one positioned tease

"Seven brands you should stop buying, and two that are actually worth your money." Then: "And that species fraud angle, it's coming at brand number two. Don't skip ahead." Or: "Secret number one is a triple play that turns this store into the cheapest place to shop in Canada."

The count in the script must match the title. The Costco reference video promised four skips in the intro, five in the title, and delivered four; its comments noticed.

### Rule 4 — Before item one, teach the yardstick

Every strong script pauses for sixty to ninety seconds to explain one or two things "every person buying X needs to understand," and then measures every item against them. This is what turns a list of opinions into a test.

- Sardines: "the word sardine has no single fixed meaning… over 20 species" and "the oil in the can is not a minor detail."
- Ice cream: "Canadian law requires at least 10% milk fat to earn that name."
- Ground beef: how trim works, the CFIA fat bands, and "water added during grinding does not have to be declared."
- Burgers: "When a chain says 100% beef, that means no other meat. Doesn't tell you quality cuts or how long it's been frozen."
- Fake foods: "This is exactly what CFIA officially defines as food fraud."

For Canadian Counter the yardstick is almost always one of: unit price, country of ownership, what the label word legally means, or what the regulator's own data says.

### Rule 5 — Every item runs the same six-beat pattern

1. Name it and anchor it in familiarity or nostalgia. "That red tin has been sitting in Canadian kitchens for decades. It feels like a classic, feels safe."
2. Pivot. "But here's the reality." / "But here's who's actually taking your money."
3. Two to four checkable facts, each with a number or a proper noun: the parent company and its country, a dollar figure, a date, a percentage, a price per unit, a label phrase quoted exactly.
4. One vivid comparison or quote. "McDonald's patties weigh 1.6 oz, thinner than two credit cards stacked together." "a mass-caught, mass-produced tin of nothing."
5. A verdict line in a rhythm of three or a hard contrast. "Cheap beans, no organic, no testing, pre-ground and stale. You're paying for the name, not for what's inside it." "127 years of Canadian branding. Zero years of Canadian ownership." "The label implies premium. The standard behind the label is minimal."
6. A bridge tease into the next item. "The next brand on this list is something millions of Canadians drink every single day. And it was sold off to foreign investors for nearly a billion dollars while Canadians had no idea."

### Rule 6 — The Canadian betrayal beat is the emotional engine

The device that shows up in every Canadian top performer: a brand that looks, sounds and feels Canadian, followed by who actually owns it, followed by the dollar figure.

- "Nabob… founded in Vancouver in 1896… owned by Kraft Heinz, the same American company that owns Maxwell House."
- "Brunswick… started in Black's Harbour, New Brunswick, back in the 1880s… acquired by Bumble Bee Seafoods."
- "Van Houtte. Founded in Montreal in 1919… sold to an American company called Green Mountain Coffee Roasters for 915 million Canadian dollars."
- "Tim Hortons was bought by Burger King for 12.5 billion dollars. The deal was funded by 3G Capital… based in Miami, Florida."

The scripts always say this is legal and not wrongdoing, then let the fact sit. That is the correct handling and it is house style.

### Rule 7 — Convert every price into a unit and a year

- "Kicking Horse costs more, around $16 to $19 for 454 g, about 35 to 40 cents per cup. Maxwell House costs about 13 cents per cup… Over a full year, drinking two cups a day, that's about $180 more."
- "The average Canadian household spends somewhere between $400 and $600 a year on ground beef alone."
- "36 cents per 100 grams" / "$1.76 per 100 g" / "Giant Tiger was not just cheaper, it was 32% cheaper than No Frills on the same loaf of bread."

Per 100 g, per litre, per cup, per load, per sheet, per year. Then the arithmetic is shown, not asserted.

### Rule 8 — Use the regulator, the court or the filing as the witness

The credibility of the whole genre rests on sounding like the video read the documents.

- "the Canadian Food Inspection Agency conducted its own study… They tested 101 canned fish samples collected from retail stores in 11 major cities."
- "In the 2024 to 2025 reporting year, CFIA tested 886 samples for authenticity and ran 362 label verifications."
- "In 2017, Bumble Bee Foods and two of its executives were criminally charged and convicted for price fixing."
- "A Quebec court just confirmed… 74 counts of false advertising… $1.29 million settlement."

The named source is spoken in the sentence. Not "studies show." "The CFIA tested 240 samples and found 22%."

### Rule 9 — Decode a label word in every video

"Product of Canada," "100% beef," "frozen dessert," "natural," "Angus," "wild-caught," "sardines," "extra lean." The script quotes the label, then says what the law actually requires for that phrase, then says what the viewer assumed it meant. The gap is the content.

"For a product to carry the product of Canada label, the last substantial transformation must have occurred in Canada. For ground beef, grinding counts."

### Rule 10 — Put a fairness clause inside the attack, not after it

Every strong script concedes something mid-item. This is what keeps the video alive in the comments and out of trouble.

- "Now, it's important to note that Dollarama has stated that its products meet Canadian safety regulations."
- "To be fair, recalls happen in almost every retail environment."
- "Now, canned tuna is separate from sardines, and the price fixing conviction doesn't mean sardine quality is compromised. But it does say something about the culture inside a company."
- "I want to be clear about something. We are not a government agency. We are not the Canadian Food Inspection Agency. This is not a peer-reviewed study."

### Rule 11 — The good list is shorter, and each good item is built on structure, not praise

Every "worth it" entry carries the same four things: where it is made and by whom, with a city and a year; what is structurally different about it (private ownership, direct trade, cream listed first, grinds daily); the honest price per unit with the words "costs more"; and where the viewer can actually buy it.

"49th Parallel Coffee Roasters. Founded in Vancouver in 2004. Roasted in Burnaby, BC… Around 18 to 22 dollars for 340 grams." / "Tip: Kicking Horse shows up at Costco regularly."

### Rule 12 — Say what NOT to buy even in a positive video, and what IS good even in a negative one

Giant Tiger's video, which is a love letter, spends a full segment on "what you should never buy at Giant Tiger, because being honest about this is what separates us from an ad." The Dollarama video ends on "some of the best deals in the country are inside that store, but so are some of the worst." This balance is the single most-liked structural feature in the comments of every video studied.

### Rule 13 — Give the viewer a rule to take home, in threes

- "Three questions to ask before any sardine tin goes into the cart. What species is actually in this tin? What oil is it packed in? And is this can BPA-free? If the label can't answer all three, put it back."
- "Part one. Check the cook down… Part two. Read the actual fat percentage, not the marketing word… Part three. Buy from a source that controls the grind."
- "Here's the Canada Eats Fake Food Check. Five things in under 30 seconds."
- "Here's the simple rule. If the product's job is to be used up, Giant Tiger is almost always the right call. If the product's job is to last or to keep you safe, do more research."

### Rule 14 — One mantra, repeated three times

"The cheap beef is the expensive beef. Write that down." The line appears at the butcher result, in the buying rule, and in the sign-off. Giant Tiger's is "check the price tag, not the logo." The Costco script we wrote uses "the people who do well at Costco aren't the ones who buy more, they're the ones who divide."

### Rule 15 — CTA goes at the hinge, never at the top

The subscribe ask sits at the moment of maximum curiosity: just before the list flips from bad to good, or just before the top three. "Before we reveal the six chains actually worth your money, hit that subscribe button." "Before we get to the top four, subscribe. We've covered Dollarama, Tim Hortons, Loblaws, and now we're inside Giant Tiger." One sentence, then straight back in.

### Rule 16 — The comment prompt asks for data the viewer already has

"Tell me where you buy your ground beef and how long you have been buying it there. I want to know if what we found matches what you have experienced in the pan. The data from 100 kitchens is better than the data from one test kitchen." "Drop your best Giant Tiger find in the comments." Never "what do you think." Always a price, a place, a receipt, a brand.

### Rule 17 — Close in four moves

Thesis in one paragraph ("Seven brands, three foreign corporations, glass in jars, plastic in pods, a Canadian icon sold to Miami, and two real Canadian roasters still doing it right") · the take-home rule · the comment ask · the next-video tease and a sign-off catchphrase ("Stay sharp, spend smart, and always check the price tag, not the logo").

### Rule 18 — Sentence-level style

Short declaratives. Fragments are allowed and used for verdicts: "Dead last." "Easy skip." "Write that down." Numbers are always exact and spoken in full. Every technical term is defined in the same sentence it appears. Contrast pairs and triplets carry the rhythm. One concrete scene per video from the narrator's own life, generic enough to be true: "sometimes it is 9:00 on a Tuesday and the butcher is closed and I am standing in the Costco aisle making peace with my life choices." No jargon, no adjectives doing the work a number should do. Between 2,300 and 4,500 words; Canadian Counter's house band of 21,000 to 23,000 characters lands at roughly 3,600 to 3,900 words, which is 15 to 17 minutes at the channel's pace.

## 3. What the competitors do that Canadian Counter must NOT copy

These are the things that make the competitor scripts vulnerable, and which their own comment sections punish.

1. Health and ingredient claims as the spine. The sardine, coffee, ice cream and burger scripts lean on BPA, "endocrine disruptor," acrylamide, microplastics, sodium percentages, palm oil and "artery clogging." Under house rules none of that is available. Every one of those beats can be rebuilt on price, ownership, origin, label language or shrinkflation, and the rebuilt version is more checkable.
2. Invented testing. "Laboratory analysis puts its milk fat content at 9.8%… melts in 38 seconds." "We sent samples from six of the 11 stores to an independent food testing laboratory." No lab is named, no report is shown. If Canadian Counter says it tested something, it films the test.
3. Unattributed review quotes. "Multiple food critics and sardine reviewers have noted…" "Independent reviewers and food critics have noted a consistent and steady decline." Name the outlet or cut the line.
4. Wikipedia as a primary source. "Wikipedia documents this directly." Go to the company's own page.
5. Stating a price without a date, a store and a city. The Costco reference's $7.99 rotisserie chicken was challenged in its own comments within a day.
6. Guilt by association. "It does say something about the culture inside a company" is a step past what the record supports. Attribute, or stop at the fact.
7. Miscount. Promise four in the intro, five in the title, deliver four.
8. Stock footage that gives away the country. Right-hand-drive cars and English-only packaging were called out by name in the comments of two of the videos studied. "AI slop" was the top comment on a 143,000-view Costco video.

## 4. THE REUSABLE PROMPT

Copy everything between the lines. Fill the bracketed fields. Paste your research notes where indicated. The prompt is written so that it can be used with any capable model; the constraints are the ones that produced the channel's best-performing scripts.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

You are writing the narration script for a faceless Canadian consumer-investigation YouTube video for the channel Canadian Counter. The audience is Canadian grocery shoppers, mostly over 40, who feel that everything they buy has quietly got worse and want someone to lay out exactly how, with numbers. Write in plain spoken English for a single narrator.

VIDEO
Title: [PASTE THE EXACT TITLE]
Format: [choose one: AVOID-AND-WORTH-IT list · STORE "UNTIL YOU WATCH THIS" secrets countdown · RANKED WORST TO BEST · BUY-VS-SKIP · "JUST GOT CAUGHT" named-handle exposé]
Item count: [N to avoid / M worth it — must match the title exactly]
Countdown direction: [ascending 1→N for buy/skip and store secrets; descending N→1 for avoid lists and exposés; worst→best for rankings]

RESEARCH NOTES
[Paste every fact you want used. For each item give: the brand or store, who owns it and where the owner is based, the exact label wording you are quoting, the price with the size, the store, the city and the date it was captured, any regulator finding or court record with its date and name, and any corporate statement with the speaker's name and where they said it. Mark anything unverified as UNVERIFIED so it is left out.]

HARD CONSTRAINTS
1. Length: between 21,000 and 23,000 characters of spoken narration. Count only the words the narrator says.
2. Format: plain readable prose paragraphs. No markdown, no asterisks, no bullet points, no headings, no stage directions, no timestamps. Item numbers are spoken inside sentences ("Number seven is…", "Skip number three.").
3. No health claims of any kind. Do not say or imply that any food, ingredient, additive, oil, sugar, sodium level, packaging material or chemical is healthy, unhealthy, safe, unsafe, toxic, harmful, "clean," or linked to any condition. Do not give nutrition commentary. If an ingredient or a nutrient number is a declared label fact it may be read out as a label fact, with no commentary about what it does. Build every item instead on price, unit price, ownership and where the owner is based, country of origin, what a label word legally means versus what people assume, shrinkflation, and what regulators, courts or the company's own filings and statements say.
4. Sourcing: every number carries its source in the sentence ("according to Costco's own website on the nineteenth of September," "in Loblaw's annual report," "the CFIA tested 240 samples"). Every corporate claim is attributed to the company or the named executive. Never say "studies show," "experts say," "reviewers have noted" or "reports suggest." Never cite Wikipedia. Never invent a test, a lab, a quote or a price. If a fact in the notes is marked UNVERIFIED, do not use it.
5. Legal posture: never state or imply uncharged wrongdoing. Foreign ownership, price increases, smaller packages and legal label wording are facts, not crimes, and the script says so out loud. An allegation is not a finding, a finding is not a settlement, a settlement is not an admission; use the right word every time.
6. Prices are spoken with their date, size, store and city, and the script says once near the top that prices vary by location and should be checked on the shelf.
7. The item count spoken in the script equals the item count in the title.

STRUCTURE — FOLLOW THIS ORDER
Cold open, under 200 words: open on something the viewer did this week, in the second person. Pivot with "but." Then stack three to five of the video's most surprising specifics in a single run, WITHOUT naming any brand or store. Then state the count exactly as the title has it. Then plant one positioned tease ("the one at number two is the one that made me check the maths twice"). Do not open with the channel name or a greeting.

The yardstick, 100 to 180 words: explain the one or two things the viewer needs to understand to judge every item that follows: the unit that matters, what a label phrase legally means, who owns what, or what the regulator actually measures. Every later item is measured against this.

Items: each item runs six beats in this order. One: name it and anchor it in something familiar or nostalgic. Two: pivot with "but here's…". Three: two to four checkable facts, each carrying a number or a proper noun, each with its source in the sentence. Four: one vivid comparison or a short quote with a named source. Five: a verdict line, either a rhythm of three or a hard contrast, under fifteen words. Six: a one-sentence bridge that teases the next item without naming it. Include at least one Canadian-ownership beat in the video: a brand that looks Canadian, followed by who owns it and where they are, followed by the dollar figure or the year, followed by the sentence that this is legal and not wrongdoing.

Fairness inside the attack: at least three items must contain a concession or a limit stated mid-item ("to be fair," "I want to be careful here," "that's their sentence, not mine," "nobody has shown me that and I'm not going to say it"). Where the company has published a response or a policy, quote it.

The CTA: exactly one subscribe line, one sentence, placed at the hinge, immediately before the list flips from the bad half to the good half, or immediately before the final three. Nowhere else.

The good half (if the format has one): shorter than the bad half. Each good item gives the maker, the city and the year, what is structurally different about it, the honest price per unit including the words "costs more" where true, and where to buy it.

If the video is about a store: include a "what never to buy here" segment inside a positive video, or a "what is genuinely good here" segment inside a negative one.

Forward teases: at least five across the script, each pointing at a specific later position ("wait until number three," "the last one on the skip list").

Unit arithmetic: whenever a price appears, show the division on screen in the narration: per 100 grams, per litre, per cup, per load, per sheet, per year. Then say what the gap is worth over a year for an ordinary household.

Decode one label word: quote the phrase from the package, state what the law requires for that phrase, state what most people assume it means. Let the gap sit.

Mantra: one line under twelve words that carries the video's thesis, spoken three times: once at the first big reveal, once in the take-home rule, once in the sign-off.

Close, in this order: a one-paragraph synthesis that lists the video's specifics again in a run; a take-home rule in three parts; a comment prompt that asks for a number, a place or a receipt the viewer already has (never "what do you think"); a one-sentence tease of the next video; a sign-off catchphrase.

STYLE
Short declaratives. Fragments allowed for verdicts. Second person throughout. Exact numbers spoken in full. Define every technical term in the sentence it first appears. Contrast pairs and triplets carry the rhythm. One concrete first-person scene per video, generic enough to be true of anyone. No adjectives doing the work a number should do. No "shocking," "insane," "crazy," "toxic," "poison," "scam" or "exposed" anywhere in the narration. No sentence longer than thirty words. No paragraph longer than six sentences.

OUTPUT
Return the title on the first line, then the narration as plain prose paragraphs, then a line reading NOTE ON LENGTH followed by the character count of the narration and a list of every fact you were given that you did not use and why.

## 5. How to use it with the ideas from the competitor report

For each idea, fill the RESEARCH NOTES block from the corresponding research dossier in this repository (research_costco_buyskip.md, research_timhortons.md, research_honey.md, or whichever is built next).
