Which brings us to number seven — the worst offender in the aisle, and it's not a brand. It's a single letter. S. Canadian food law, the CFIA's own labelling rules: the name "sea salt" — singular — refers to salt from the sea that must meet the composition standard for salt, and if it's sold for table use, it must be iodized like everything else. But "sea salts" — plural — is legally a different product: "a mixture of sodium chloride and other mineral salts." And in the CFIA's own words: "Sea salts do not have to meet the standard for salt and as such, do not have to be iodized." One letter. Singular, the law follows the salt. Plural, it doesn't. The same escape works through the product's stated purpose: call it "salt for pickling," call it a "finishing salt," name it for any use except the table, and the 1949 mandate never touches it. This is a category where the Canadian label genuinely wins.
