# SCRIPT — Soy Sauce

There are two completely different products standing side by side in the soy sauce aisle of every Canadian grocery store. Same shelf, same dark bottles, similar prices. One of them was made the way soy sauce has been made for centuries: soybeans and roasted wheat, a culture called koji, salt water, and then months — up to half a year — of slow fermentation in tanks, while microbes build the flavour one compound at a time. The other one was made in a matter of days, by breaking soybean protein apart with acid, and then stirring in caramel colour, sugar and salt to imitate what the fermentation would have created for free. Here's what most Canadians don't know: our food law doesn't separate them. Canada has no legal standard for soy sauce — none — so both products get to wear the same name. China, of all places, is stricter than we are: since 2019, Chinese law says if it isn't fermented, it cannot be called soy sauce at all. Japan grades brewing methods right on the label by law. In Canada, you get one clue, and only one — but it's a good one, because our labelling rules force it to be printed. Two words in the ingredient list: "hydrolysed soy protein." That phrase is the fingerprint of the acid method. It cannot legally hide. And once you know to look for it, the aisle rearranges itself — because the bottle your mother kept next to the stove, the one with sixty years of Canadian dinners behind it, carries those two words. So does the beloved bottle from the Prairies and the East Coast. So does a bottle in the health-food aisle that costs four times more. Today: eight "soya sauce" brands on Canadian shelves that were never brewed at all — in order, starting with the most Canadian bottle of them all — and then the three that actually earn the word, including one that's cheaper than the chemical stuff, and one with a Canadian secret hiding inside it that almost nobody knows. Let's get into it.

Number one — VH Soya Sauce. This is Canada's default soy sauce. The bottle with the red cap that showed up when Canadian families started making chop suey and stir-fries at home, the one that's been in the fridge door of this country for generations. It feels like it's been here forever — actually, the history is more precise than that: a man named Vincent Harrison — the V.H. — started the company in Montreal in 1958, and the sauces are still prepared and bottled in Quebec today, with the "Aliments préparés au Québec" certification on the line. Now flip the bottle, because the ingredient list is the whole story, and I'll read it exactly: "water, salt, caramel, hydrolyzed soy protein, corn syrup, glucose solids, sodium benzoate, dimethylpolysiloxane" — that last one is an antifoaming agent. Look at the order. Salt is the second ingredient. The caramel colouring comes before the soy. The actual soybean content of Canada's soy sauce is an acid-processed protein sitting fourth on its own label, behind the colouring. And every other item on that list is a patch for something fermentation would have produced naturally: corn syrup and glucose because the shortcut makes no natural sweetness, caramel because it makes no natural colour, a preservative because there was no fermentation to do the preserving. There's even a quiet confirmation hiding in the marketing: VH advertises this sauce as gluten-free. Real brewed soy sauce is roughly half wheat — it can't be gluten-free. VH contains no wheat because VH was never brewed. Two more things belong on the record. First, the label: VH runs about 1,160 milligrams of sodium in a single tablespoon — the saltiest bottle in this entire video, roughly half of an adult's daily sodium in one spoon — and under the new federal front-of-package rules that took effect this January, that bottle now has to wear the "High in sodium" warning symbol on the front. Second, the fairness clause: hydrolyzed soy protein is a legal, federally compliant food ingredient, this product is safe, and it has seasoned Canadian kitchens for sixty-plus years. The ownership, though, isn't Canadian anymore — VH belongs to Conagra Brands of Chicago. The jobs stayed in Quebec; the profits didn't. And the next bottle is the one where this gets personal for half the country.

Number two — China Lily. If you grew up in Atlantic Canada or on the Prairies, this is not a condiment, it's a family member. The square bottle, the yellow label, the thick dark sauce on Christmas-party chicken balls and homemade fried rice for seventy years. I want to be careful with this one, because the story is genuinely beautiful — but the label comes first. Verbatim: "Blended from hydrolyzed soya and wheat protein, water, sugar, salt, caramel colour, corn starch, sodium benzoate." There it is again — hydrolyzed. China Lily is not brewed. And that famous thickness, that darkness, that gentle sweetness people are loyal to? Read where it comes from: sugar, caramel colour, and corn starch. The texture Canadians love is a recipe, not an aging process. One precision note, because fairness matters on this channel: China Lily is actually one of the lighter sodium options on the shelf — 570 milligrams a tablespoon, lower than genuinely brewed Kikkoman — so nothing here is a health warning. This is a what-are-you-actually-buying story. And here's the redemption arc, because China Lily has something no other bottle in this aisle has: it is entirely Canadian. Lee's Food Products was founded in Toronto in 1947 — the same year Canada finally repealed the Chinese Exclusion Act — by a founder who had arrived from Guangdong back in 1913, in the era of the head tax. The family has run it for three generations. For seventy-three years the sauce was made in the same little factory on Queen Street East, putting Chinese ingredients into mainstream Canadian grocery stores decades before anyone said "international aisle," until production moved to Scarborough in 2020 — and Toronto spent 2022 fighting over whether the old factory deserved heritage status. One asterisk for the record: in March 2023 some bottles were recalled in the Prairie provinces for leaking and bulging — a Class 3 packaging issue, the lowest-risk category, handled through the normal process. So here's the honest verdict: it's the most Canadian bottle in the aisle, and it isn't brewed soy sauce. Both things are true. Buy it for the memories, knowingly — not for the word on the front. There's even a lesson in why the loyalty is so fierce: China Lily doesn't taste like Kikkoman because it was never trying to be Kikkoman. It's sweeter, thicker, milder — a flavour built in Toronto for the Canadian kitchens of the 1950s, and it became the taste that "soy sauce" simply means in half this country's family recipes. People aren't wrong to love it. They're loving a Canadian original that happens to wear a borrowed name. Now — what about the bottles with no story attached at all?

Number three — the house brands: no name, Compliments, and Selection. Loblaw's, Sobeys' and Metro's own soy sauces, all around two dollars, all about forty-four to fifty cents per hundred millilitres — the cheapest bottles on the shelf. Flip all three and something interesting happens: they're essentially the same sauce. no name: "water, hydrolyzed soy protein, sugar, salt, caramel colour, lactic acid, sodium benzoate." Compliments: "water, hydrolyzed soya protein, salt, caramel colour, sugar, lactic acid, sodium benzoate." Selection, in French: same list, shuffled. Three banners, one formula — water, acid-processed protein, sugar, colour, salt. And here's the history that explains why this recipe even exists. During the Second World War, imports of fermented soy sauce to North America were cut off, and manufacturers substituted hydrolyzed vegetable protein — the acid shortcut — to keep "soy sauce" on shelves. The war ended. The substitution didn't. Your grandparents' soy sauce is a wartime workaround that simply never got un-substituted, and eighty years later it's still the default recipe of every Canadian house brand. That's the strange truth under this whole aisle: nobody set out to trick anyone. A shortage created a substitute, the substitute was cheap, the cheap version became the taste people grew up on, and three generations later the substitute owns the shelf while the original costs extra. It's the story of margarine, instant coffee and processed cheese, replayed in a dark bottle. And at these prices you can see why it stuck: a family that stir-fries once a week burns through maybe three bottles of soy sauce a year — the house brands cost six dollars a year, and the difference up to the best brewed bottle on this list is about the price of a single fast-food combo, once, annually. Hold that thought for the winners. The store brands aren't pretending to be anything — no heritage stories, no brewing claims, two bucks. Honest, in their way. In fact, Loblaw itself quietly tells you the truth about no name — because they also sell a President's Choice sauce named "Naturally Brewed Soy Sauce." Think about why that product needs to exist. If you're finding this useful, a quick like genuinely helps more Canadians learn to flip the bottle. Because the next brand doesn't just use the shortcut — it uses the shortcut and then borrows the real thing's name.

Number four — Great Value. Walmart's soy sauce, and the single sneakiest ingredient list in this entire video. Read it slowly: "water, hydrolyzed soy protein, sugar, caramel colour, salt, naturally brewed soya sauce — in brackets: water, soybeans, wheat, salt, sodium benzoate, mould culture — lactic acid, sodium benzoate." Did you catch it? The words "naturally brewed soya sauce" do appear — as the sixth ingredient, inside brackets, after the water, the acid-processed protein, the sugar, the colouring and the salt. A splash of genuinely brewed sauce, stirred into a bottle of the imitation, and suddenly the phrase "naturally brewed" is legally present on the panel. It's a blend — overwhelmingly the shortcut, seasoned with a spoonful of the real thing. Canada has no rule about how much brewed sauce a blend needs before borrowing those words, because — remember — Canada has no rules for soy sauce at all. Japan would make this bottle declare itself a blend. China wouldn't let it say soy sauce. In Canada, it's two dollars and twenty-three cents, no questions asked. To be fair: nothing on this label is false. Every word is accurate. It's just arranged the way a magician arranges a card trick. And if you think that trick is expensive at two dollars, wait until you see what it costs in the health-food aisle.

Number five — Bragg Liquid Aminos. This is the one that will genuinely surprise people, because Bragg isn't sold as soy sauce at all — it's sold above it. The health aisle. The yellow spray bottle at nine dollars, the "liquid aminos" name, the wellness aura. The ingredient list is two items long: "vegetable protein from soybeans and purified water." That brevity reads as purity. Now here is how Bragg's own website describes the process, quote: "Instead of fermenting, they're made by gently treating soybeans with an acidic solution that breaks the proteins into amino acids." Read that twice. Not fermented. Treated with an acidic solution that breaks the protein into amino acids. That is a description of hydrolysis — the same family of process as the two-dollar no name bottle — reported by health media as done with hydrochloric acid, then neutralized. The differences are real and worth stating fairly: Bragg uses non-GMO soybeans, adds no colouring, no sweeteners, no preservatives. But the core transformation — acid instead of fermentation, days instead of months — is the budget-shelf method, at a dollar-ninety per hundred millilitres: roughly double VH, more than four times no name. And the "healthier" halo has one more soft spot: the label says 310 milligrams of sodium, which looks like a third of the others — until you notice the serving size. Soy sauces state sodium per tablespoon. Bragg states it per teaspoon. Convert it, and a tablespoon of Liquid Aminos lands around 930 milligrams — Kikkoman territory. Same process family as the cheapest bottle, premium price, and a serving-size asterisk. The health aisle should read labels the way it tells the rest of us to. And while we're in that aisle, a quick word on the bottle one shelf up: coconut aminos — the soy-free alternative made from fermented coconut sap and sea salt. Credit where due, it genuinely is fermented, and it genuinely is lower in sodium. It's also about four dollars and fifty-five cents per hundred millilitres — roughly ten times the price of no name — for something that, taste-wise, is a sweeter, milder cousin rather than a replacement. If sodium is the actual goal, a less-sodium brewed soy sauce gets you a bigger cut per dollar; if avoiding soy is the goal, fair enough — just know you're paying the aisle's highest price for it. One more avoid entry — the bottle with the most stamps in its passport.

Number six — Amoy. The "Hong Kong classic" in Chinese-Canadian kitchens, born in the city of Xiamen — Amoy — in 1908. Two things to know. First, the label: Amoy's standard light soy sauce is built on "soy sauce extract, 51 per cent" — a genuinely brewed base — then topped up with water, sugar, salt, caramel colour, acidity regulators and two flavour enhancers, E631 and E627. It's the polished version of the blend: half real, half dressing room. Second, the passport: this heritage Chinese brand has spent thirty years being passed between multinationals — sold to Danone of France in 1991, to Ajinomoto of Japan in 2006, and since 2018 it's belonged to CITIC Capital, a Chinese state-linked investment firm. Still made in Hong Kong; hasn't been a family company in a generation. So that's the avoid list — and before the winners, here's the sixty-second version of everything the labels just taught us. Canada has no standard for soy sauce. I searched the Food and Drug Regulations and all nineteen volumes of the federal food compositional standards — the documents that legally define what butter, honey, bread and even curry powder must be — and the words "soy sauce" appear nowhere. Curry powder has a federal recipe; soy sauce doesn't. Japan's law recognizes honjozo — genuine brewed — and forces blends to identify themselves. Taiwan added mandatory brewed-versus-chemical labelling in 2019. China threw the unfermented products out of the name entirely. Canada's only rule is the general one: don't mislead. Even "naturally brewed" on a front label here is a voluntary claim a company must merely be able to substantiate if an inspector asks — it's not a grade anyone assigned. So the only protection you actually have is two words the law does force onto every label: "hydrolysed soy protein." The acid method those words describe is legal and safe — Health Canada even maintains a specific maximum for a process contaminant called 3-MCPD in these sauces, one part per million, brought in after international testing in 2001 caught bad imports; surveys since show the industry cleaned up, and traditionally brewed sauce barely produces the stuff at all. So this was never a danger story. It's a value story: whether the bottle you're paying for was crafted for months or assembled in days. And as of this January, there's one more label to watch: the new federal "High in sodium" symbol, which now has to appear on the front of virtually every regular soy sauce in the country — the trigger is 230 milligrams a tablespoon, and these sauces run four times that. Now: the three bottles that actually earn the word "brewed."

Best option number three — Lee Kum Kee Premium Soy Sauce. Here's the reveal that breaks the aisle's biggest assumption: the real thing can be cheaper than the imitation. Lee Kum Kee — the Hong Kong family company founded in 1888 — sells its Premium Soy Sauce, brewed by what it calls traditional and natural methods, for about three-ninety-eight at Walmart. Per hundred millilitres, that's eighty cents — cheaper than VH's hydrolyzed ninety-four. The ingredient list starts the right way: water, salt, soybeans, sugar, wheat flour — fermented, not acid-processed. Full honesty, as always: Lee Kum Kee tops up its brew with two MSG-family flavour enhancers, disodium inosinate and guanylate, so it's brewed-plus-boosters, not purist. But if the only thing standing between you and real fermented soy sauce was price — that excuse is now gone.

Best option number two — San-J Tamari. This one is for a specific reader of labels: anyone in your house avoiding gluten. Remember the VH tell — real soy sauce is half wheat. Tamari is the branch of the family brewed the old Japanese way from soybeans alone, and San-J has been at it since 1804. The ingredient list: "water, soybeans, salt, alcohol" — and that trace of alcohol isn't an additive, it's a fingerprint of genuine fermentation, made by the yeast itself. Six months of brewing, no wheat, naturally gluten-free — the deliberate version of what VH's gluten-free badge only accidentally admits. It's the expensive shelf, about two-eighty per hundred millilitres, but for celiac households it's the real brewed option, full stop. Darker, rounder, less sharp than regular soy sauce — many people never go back.

Best option number one — Kikkoman. The red-capped dispenser, the four-ingredient benchmark: "water, soybeans, wheat, salt." Nothing else doing the work — because six months of koji fermentation does all of it, a process Kikkoman says builds more than three hundred separate aroma compounds, and one the company has been running since the 1600s in Noda, Japan, where eight brewing families merged into one company in 1917. Two things about your actual Canadian bottle. First: it wasn't brewed in Japan. It was almost certainly brewed in Walworth, Wisconsin — the plant Kikkoman opened in 1973, one of the first factories built in America by a Japanese company, barely a generation after the war, and today one of the biggest soy sauce breweries on Earth. Check the fine print on your bottle. Second — and this is the reveal I promised at the start: what feeds breweries like that is North American farmland, and increasingly, that means us. Canada is now the world's fourth-largest soybean exporter, and Canadian food-grade soybeans — identity-preserved, grown in Ontario and Quebec — held about forty-four per cent of Japan's food-grade soybean import market in recent USDA figures. In 2024 alone, Canada shipped four hundred million dollars of soybeans to Japan, destined for tofu, miso, natto — and soy sauce. These aren't ordinary commodity beans, either: they're grown under a certification called the Canadian Identity Preserved Recognition System — seed to ship, segregated and traceable, the kind of paperwork Japanese brewers demand — and it's a business Canadian farmers have been building since the very first soybeans in this country were planted at the Ontario Agricultural College in Guelph in 1881. Sit with that: the "authentic Japanese" bottle on your shelf may contain more Canadian farming than the no name bottle beside it. One caution flag so the comments don't have to raise it: Kikkoman itself says only that its American breweries use North American-grown soybeans and wheat — it doesn't publish whether Canadian beans are in the mix — so the certain statement is about Japan's soy industry as a whole, and it's certain enough: Canadian fields feed the world's most demanding soy-food makers. The premium for all this? About four dollars and seventy cents more than no name — the price of one fancy coffee for the difference between days and months. And if the sodium is your worry, Kikkoman's Less Sodium green cap is the same six-month brew with forty per cent of the salt removed after aging — not a different recipe, the same sauce, minus salt.

So here's the whole aisle in one test — thirty seconds, standing in the store. Turn the bottle. If the ingredient list starts "water, soybeans, wheat, salt," it was brewed for months, and everything on that short list had a job. If you see the words "hydrolysed soy protein" — anywhere — it was made in days, and everything after those words is a patch: sugar for the missing sweetness, caramel for the missing colour, enhancers for the missing umami. That's the entire test, and Canadian law guarantees the fingerprint will be printed. Nothing you saw today was a safety scare — every bottle in this video is legal and safe, and the only recalls in a decade were leaky lids. But do glance at one more thing while the bottle's turned around: the sodium line. The average Canadian already eats about 2,760 milligrams of sodium a day against a 2,300 target, half of Canadians over sixty have high blood pressure, and one tablespoon of soy sauce — brewed or not — can carry forty per cent of your day. That's exactly why every one of these bottles now wears the new black-and-white "High in sodium" symbol on the front: the label is finally doing the scanning for you, and a less-sodium version cuts it nearly in half. One last thing, because this aisle is older than the country: the first soy sauce advertised for sale in Canada appeared in a Saint John, New Brunswick newspaper in 1831 — thirty-six years before Confederation. A hundred and ninety-five years later, most of the bottles wearing the name skip the part that takes time. Now you know how to spot the ones that didn't. Flip the bottle in your own kitchen tonight — then send this to the person in your family who swears by the bottle with the yellow label, because now you can tell them what's actually in it, and why they love it anyway.
