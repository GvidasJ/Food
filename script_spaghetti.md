# SCRIPT — Spaghetti

TITLE: Canadians Must AVOID These 6 Spaghetti Brands (Only 3 Are ACTUALLY Canadian)

---

The spaghetti you bought this week is probably not real pasta. That's the claim in a video almost seven hundred thousand people just watched — that most spaghetti on North American shelves is built from cheap common wheat flour instead of the grain pasta is supposed to be made from, with an Italian name on the front doing all the work. And for American shelves, they had a point. So I did what they did. I went into a Canadian grocery store and I flipped every single box of spaghetti on the shelf — the hundred-and-fifty-year-old heritage brand, the blue box, the store brands, the two-dollar bag at the very bottom. And here's the thing nobody expected. Every single one of them passed the viral video's number one test. Even the cheapest bag in the entire store — two dollars — lists durum wheat semolina as its only ingredient. In Canada, the fake spaghetti scandal doesn't exist. But what I found on those boxes instead is honestly stranger. Canada's oldest pasta brand — founded the same year as Canada itself — isn't Canadian anymore. The most Italian-looking box in the aisle is made in Iowa. There is a bag on Canadian shelves that is a physical souvenir of a trade war fought over Saskatchewan wheat. And the American brand that viral video named as its number one villain? As of last December, it's owned by a company in Winnipeg. Six brands where the story on the front of the box does not match what's actually going on — in order, starting with the one in the most Canadian pantries — and then the three brands that are actually still Canadian, because there are only three left. The biggest reversal in this whole story is saved for the end. Let's get into it.

Number one — Catelli. Catelli is not just the most popular pasta brand in Canada. It is practically a piece of the country's furniture. Carlo Onorato Catelli was an eighteen-year-old Italian immigrant who started making pasta on Saint-Paul Street in Old Montreal in 1867 — the year of Confederation. Canada's first pasta company is literally the same age as Canada. That is a hundred and fifty-nine years of trust, and when you hold the box, everything about it says Canadian institution. So flip it. First ingredient: durum wheat semolina. It passes the viral test without breaking a sweat — this is real pasta, made in Montreal, and it always has been. The problem is the flag flying over it. On January 29th, 2021, Catelli was sold to Barilla — the Italian pasta giant — for a hundred and sixty-five million dollars. And here's the detail almost nobody knows: Barilla didn't buy it from a Canadian company. They bought it from Ebro Foods — a food conglomerate in Spain. Canada's heritage pasta brand had already quietly stopped being Canadian; Barilla just changed which foreign flag it answers to. To be completely fair: nothing closed. The Montreal plant kept running, and Ottawa actually put three and a half million dollars into a new production line there in 2023 — the pasta is still made by Canadian workers from Canadian wheat. But there's one more thing on this box worth reading on camera, because Catelli's own materials can't seem to agree with themselves. Their website lists one ingredient — durum wheat semolina, "all-natural," with iron at about three percent of your daily value. Retail listings for the same spaghetti show it enriched — niacin, ferrous sulphate, thiamine, riboflavin, folic acid. Same brand, two different vitamin stories. Hold that thought — that iron number is about to become the single most useful line on any pasta box in Canada, and it's the key to the two-dollar bag coming up at number three. But first, the box with the actual Italian flag on it.

Number two — Barilla. The blue box. The world's biggest pasta maker, the one that sells itself as "Italy's number one brand of pasta," the box Canadians grab when they want to upgrade from the store brand to something authentically Italian. Flip it. First ingredient: semolina, durum wheat. It passes. It's real pasta. Now find the fine print, because Barilla's own Canadian website answers a question most people never think to ask — where is it actually made? Quote: "Barilla pasta that is sold in Canada is made in plants in Ames, Iowa and Avon, New York." Iowa. The most Italian-looking box in the Canadian aisle is, for the most part, American pasta imported north. And Barilla knows exactly how much that image is worth, because in the United States they are being sued over it — a class action arguing that "Italy's number one brand" on American-made pasta misleads shoppers. That lawsuit was certified in 2024, though a judge has since signalled it may be dismissed, so we'll see where it lands. But for Canadian viewers, there's a much better Barilla story, and it's about our wheat. Canada is the largest exporter of durum wheat on Earth — roughly six of every ten tonnes of durum traded anywhere on the planet is Canadian, and three quarters of it grows in Saskatchewan. Italy is our single biggest customer — on average about four hundred and twenty-one million dollars of Canadian durum every year. Italian pasta has been partly built on prairie wheat for decades. And in the late 2010s, in the middle of an Italian political firestorm over foreign wheat, Barilla cut its Canadian durum purchases by about a third and set itself a private purchasing limit for glyphosate residue of ten parts per billion — that is five hundred times stricter than Canada's own legal safety limit — while, according to Reuters, openly admitting that Canadian durum is of exceptional quality. Keep that story in your pocket, because at number five, you'll be able to hold that entire trade war in your hand. Next: the bag the viral video would have bet its life on.

Number three — no name. The yellow bag. Two dollars for nine hundred grams — the cheapest spaghetti in the entire store, about twenty-two cents per hundred grams. If the viral video's premise applied anywhere in Canada, it would be here. The bottom-shelf budget bag is exactly where the cheap flour is supposed to be hiding. Flip it. One ingredient. "Durum wheat semolina." That's it. That's the whole list. The two-dollar bag is one hundred percent real durum pasta — the same base grain as the box four times its price — with ten grams of protein per serving, right in line with everything else on the shelf. The viral premise doesn't just fail in Canada. It fails hardest on the cheapest bag in the store. But the yellow bag is on this list, because there is something it doesn't have, and this is the most useful thing I can teach you in this whole video. In the United States, that viral video complained about "enriched wheat flour." In Canada, enrichment is the thing you actually want to look for — and here, it's voluntary. Federal law — B.13.052 of the Food and Drug Regulations — says a pasta can only call itself "enriched" if it adds all five: thiamine, riboflavin, niacin, folic acid and iron. But no law says pasta has to be enriched at all. And here's the paradox: for white flour, enrichment is mandatory — it is literally illegal to sell unenriched white flour in Canada, which is why every loaf of bread in the country carries added folic acid. But durum semolina doesn't legally count as white flour. So the folic acid Ottawa decided your bread must contain is completely optional in your spaghetti. The no name bag opts out — no enrichment, nothing added. And you can check any box in the aisle in five seconds without reading a single ingredient: look at the iron line in the nutrition table. Enriched pasta shows iron around fifteen to twenty percent of your daily value. Unenriched pasta shows three to eight. That one number is the tell. Ten grams of protein, real durum, twenty-two cents per hundred grams — but if pasta night is where your family gets its B vitamins, the two-dollar bag quietly isn't carrying them. If you're finding this useful, a quick like genuinely helps more Canadians see what's on their shelves. Now — the brands that make no promises at all.

Number four — the store brands: Great Value, Compliments, Selection. Walmart's, Sobeys' and Metro's house spaghetti, the middle of the bottom shelf, no heritage story, no Italian hills on the box, no promises beyond the price. Flip them. Durum wheat semolina, plus the full enrichment package — thiamine, riboflavin, niacin, folic acid, iron. By the logic we just walked through, the humble store brands are actually more complete than the two-dollar bag and arguably more honest than half the premium shelf. So why are they on the avoid list? Because of what some of them print on the front. "No preservatives" — you'll see it on pasta packaging across this aisle. Dry pasta is about twelve percent moisture. Nothing grows in it. No dry pasta anywhere on Earth contains preservatives, because none needs any — it's like selling bottled water labelled "no bones." And while we're reading fronts of boxes: "bronze-cut," "slow-dried," "artisan" — none of those phrases has any legal definition in Canada. Which brings us to the strangest fact in this entire video. I went looking for the Canadian law that defines what spaghetti is — the compositional standard that says spaghetti must be made from durum. Italy has one: by law, Italian dry pasta must be one hundred percent durum. The United States has one: a federal standard of identity for macaroni products. Canada has nothing. I searched the Food and Drug Regulations and the entire Canadian food compositional standards — the words "spaghetti," "durum" and "semolina" appear nowhere. There are exactly two pasta rules in this country: egg pasta needs four percent egg-yolk solids, and the enrichment table we just covered. Legally, a Canadian company could sell you spaghetti made from the cheapest flour on the market tomorrow. The loophole from the viral video is wide open here — wider, actually. And yet nobody uses it. Every brand on this shelf chooses durum anyway, because Canadian shoppers grew up next to the best durum on the planet and the market won't accept less. Canada is the country where the pasta out-behaves the law. Now let me show you what that wheat is worth — printed on a bag as a battle flag.

Number five — La Molisana. The deep-blue premium import, made in Campobasso, Italy since 1912, and printed proudly across the front: "one hundred percent Italian wheat." That line reads like a simple quality boast. It is not. It is a war memorial — and the war was fought over Canada. Remember: Canada is the world's number one durum exporter, and Italy is our biggest customer. Italy's own pasta industry has to import roughly thirty percent of the wheat it needs, and for years Canada was its top foreign supplier. Then, starting around 2015, Italy's most powerful farm lobby began a campaign against Canadian durum, centred on trace pesticide residues. In June 2017 it reached its peak: a ship carrying roughly fifty thousand tonnes of Canadian durum was seized in the port of Bari after toxicity claims. A certified laboratory re-tested the cargo. It was within safety limits. The ship was released — but the "contaminated wheat" headlines had already run across Italy, and Italy's own millers' association publicly called the whole thing a demonization campaign against foreign wheat. That same summer, Italy brought in a law forcing pasta makers to print where their wheat was grown. Canadian durum sales to Italy collapsed — from around one point four million tonnes a year down to three hundred thousand at the low point. And that is the moment "one hundred percent Italian wheat" appeared in giant letters on bags like this one. It is a marketing position built specifically against the wheat growing in Saskatchewan. Now, the part the labels don't say. Canada's legal limit for glyphosate on wheat is five parts per million — that is half the European Union's limit of ten, and one sixth of the American limit. When our food inspection agency tested more than three thousand foods, over ninety-eight percent were fully compliant and Health Canada found no health risk even in the exceptions. In 2023, Europe's own food safety authority found no critical areas of concern and the EU re-approved glyphosate for another decade. And in 2020, an Italian consumer lab tested famous one-hundred-percent-Italian spaghetti brands and found glyphosate traces in seven of them — all within legal limits, exactly like ours. This was never a safety story. It was a trade story. And the punchline? Italy quietly came back. Over a million tonnes of Canadian durum in the 2022-23 season, nearly a million last year — while the "Italian wheat" flags stayed printed on the bags. When you pay the premium for La Molisana, part of what you're funding is the campaign that ran against the wheat your own country grows. One last group to avoid — and it's the brands the viral video told you to buy.

Number six — the viral video's own winners. That video sent seven hundred thousand people out to buy premium imports — Rummo at four to five dollars a box, Garofalo, and brands above them. In America, stepping up from fake flour pasta to real durum pasta, that advice made sense. In Canada, the math breaks. Here, the two-dollar bag is already durum. So what does the premium import actually buy you? Flip the boxes and compare. Canadian nutrition labels use a standard serving of about eighty-five grams of dry pasta — and be careful comparing with American videos, because US labels use fifty-six grams, which makes any American number look smaller. On Canadian labels: mainstream spaghetti runs ten to eleven grams of protein per serving. The premium imports? Eleven to twelve. The great protein chasm from the viral video — in Canada, it is a one-to-two-gram gap, for double or triple the price. And two footnotes the premium shelf would rather you didn't read. Garofalo — one of the imports that video praised — is owned by Ebro Foods of Spain. The same conglomerate that owned Catelli and sold it to Barilla. The "authentic Italian alternative" and the "sold-out Canadian heritage brand" trace back to the same corporate parent. And the only spaghetti recall in Canada in the past three years? Not the two-dollar bag. Not a store brand. It was Fiorfiore — a premium imported Italian line — pulled in April 2025 for insects. Which brings us to the reversal this whole video has been building to. The viral video's number one villain — its final boss — was Ronzoni, the most famous pasta name in America, condemned as cheap flour hiding behind a trusted label. On December 1st, 2025, Ronzoni was bought — for roughly three hundred and seventy-five million US dollars — by Richardson International. A grain company headquartered in Winnipeg. Read that again. Italy owns Canada's oldest pasta brand. Canada now owns America's most famous one. While everyone argued about authenticity on the front of the box, the entire continent's pasta quietly changed hands — and the country that ended up holding the most cards is the one growing the wheat. So who's left? Three brands on Canadian shelves that are still actually Canadian-owned — and they happen to be genuinely good pasta. From solid to the best.

Best option number three — De Cecco. Yes, an Italian import in the honourable mentions — because De Cecco breaks the single biggest assumption in the aisle: that premium has to cost more. This is the pale-blue box from Abruzzo, family-run since 1886, bronze-die pasta with the matte, slightly rough surface you can see on the dry strand — the texture sauce actually clings to — and around fourteen grams of protein per hundred grams, the highest number on the shelf. Now the price. Statistics Canada tracks the national average price of a bag of dry pasta: three dollars and thirty-nine cents as of this June. I found De Cecco at Metro for two ninety-nine. The hundred-and-forty-year-old bronze-die Italian import can sit below the average price of an ordinary Canadian bag. Gram for gram it undercuts it. Premium is a shelf position, not a price law — and once you know that, the whole aisle reads differently. One honest note: De Cecco is unenriched — check that iron line — so it's the taste-and-texture pick, not the vitamin pick. Treat it the way the viral video treated its top choice: buy it once as a calibration purchase, taste what bronze-die pasta does with sauce, and then judge every other box against it.

Best option number two — Primo and Unico. Two brands, one story, and it's the best-kept secret in the aisle. Both are owned by Sun-Brite Foods — the Iacobelli family, an Italian-Canadian family business out of Ruthven, Ontario — and the pasta has been made in Toronto since 1956. But here's why they earn this spot: flip the box and read the first line. Primo: "semolina from Canadian amber durum wheat." Unico: "Canadian durum wheat semolina." They print the origin of the wheat inside the ingredient line itself. Not a maple leaf logo, not a vague "prepared for" address — the actual legal ingredient declaration names Canadian wheat. Almost nobody else on the shelf commits to that in writing. Fully enriched, folic acid included, Italian-Canadian family owned, Toronto made, Canadian wheat named on the label — usually at a mid-shelf price. When it's on sale, this is quietly the best value in the entire category.

Best option number one — Italpasta. Brampton, Ontario. Founded in 1989, still independent, around two hundred employees — the largest Canadian-owned pasta maker in the country, and after everything you've just heard, that phrase should hit differently: of the big names on a Canadian pasta shelf, this is what's left flying the flag. And the pasta backs it up. One hundred percent Grade Number 1 Canadian amber durum — and that grade is a real, brutal specification: to earn Number 1, Canadian durum needs at least eighty percent hard, glassy, vitreous kernels and meets a protein floor most countries' wheat can't touch. Last year's crop averaged fourteen and a half percent protein. This is the exact wheat Italian mills cross an ocean to buy — milled in Ontario, shaped in Brampton, with the full enrichment package: that iron line reads nineteen percent, so the folic acid your bread carries, this pasta carries too. A "Made in Canada" badge, ten grams of protein, and both federal governments have noticed — Ottawa's regional development agency invested in the Brampton operation the same way it invested in Catelli's Montreal line. Everything the premium imports charge double for — the grain, the protein, the story — is sitting here at a domestic price, from a company that never got sold. If this video puts one box in your cart, make it this one.

So here's what a Canadian pasta shelf actually is. It is not the scandal from the viral video — nobody here is hiding cheap flour behind an Italian flag; even the two-dollar bag is the real thing, and it's real because this country grows the best durum on Earth and ships six of every ten traded tonnes of it to the world. The real stories are quieter and stranger: a heritage brand wearing a foreign flag, an Italian box made in Iowa, a trade war printed on a bag, vitamins that are mandatory in your bread and optional in your noodles, and a price that tells its own story — Statistics Canada had the average bag at two thirty-six in 2017, it spiked forty-six percent to a peak of three sixty-four after the 2021 prairie drought — a drought so severe it raised pasta prices in French supermarkets — and it has never come back down, even though durum prices have fallen by half since. At today's wheat price there is roughly fourteen cents of durum in a three-dollar-and-thirty-nine-cent bag. So don't pay for the front of the box. Here are your four checks, thirty seconds, standing in the aisle. One: first ingredient — durum wheat semolina. In Canada it almost always will be; if you ever find one that isn't, you've found the unicorn this country's law technically allows. Two: the iron line — fifteen percent or more means enriched, folic acid and all; three to eight percent means it's just wheat. Neither is wrong — but know which one you're buying, especially if kids eat pasta every week. Three: look for origin written in the ingredient line itself — "Canadian durum" printed there is a stronger promise than any logo on the front. Four: price per hundred grams, not per box — because the two-dollar bag is twenty-two cents, the average is about sixty-eight, and some of the fanciest boxes on the shelf quietly sit below average while some of the plainest sit above it. And one last thing — reportedly, Canadians buy about one point seven million boxes of Kraft Dinner a week, more mac and cheese per person than any country on Earth, and yes: that macaroni is made in Montreal, from Canadian durum. We were never the country with the fake pasta problem. We're the country the pasta comes from. Go read the box in your pantry — check the first ingredient, check the iron line — and then send this to someone who's been paying double for an Italian flag, because now you know what it's actually flying over: Saskatchewan.
