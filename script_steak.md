# SCRIPT — DON'T BUY STEAK AT THESE 5 CANADIAN GROCERY STORES (HERE'S WHY)

TITLE: Don't Buy Steak at These 5 Canadian Grocery Stores (Here's Why)

TITLE NOTE: The working title is kept because every element is supported. Five banners are named, and each carries at least one dated document that gives the shopper a specific reason to check the label or the weight before paying: a CBC scale test naming the store, a federal warning on strip loin, a class action naming the company, a retailer's own flyer that prints no grade or country, or a retailer's own web page that contradicts its tray. The script says on air that the verdict is "check before you pay," not "never buy," which is the same fairness line the February beef video used. No purchase, taste, or test is claimed anywhere. Ranking is by strength and date of the documents, weakest to strongest, so Costco is fifth and its thin record is stated openly.

The steak in your cart has a grade on it. Or it doesn't. Here's what I found when I read every beef steak line in this week's flyers from twenty-three Canadian grocery banners. Ten of them printed a grade and a country on every steak. Seven printed neither on a single one. And the dearest striploin sold by the pound in the whole set, twenty-five ninety-nine, was one of the ones that said nothing. Same week, a Halifax Superstore had already been caught selling six out of six beef briskets underweight, and a B.C. Superstore had a federal warning on file for underweight strip loin. Five stores. Five sets of documents. That's the video.

Here's how this was done, and what it isn't. I didn't buy a steak and I didn't cook one. Nothing here is about taste, and nothing is about safety. Every verdict rests on a document: the store's own flyer, its own website, a Statistics Canada price series, a federal regulation, or a CBC scale test with a store name and a date on it. The flyers were pulled on the twenty-third of September through a flyer platform, for postal codes from Vancouver to Halifax, so every price needs to be re-checked on the shelf on camera, and I'll say so as we go. A steak here means a beef cut sold to grill or pan-fry, so no ground beef and no burgers. And the five stores are ranked by the strength and the date of the paper, not by how bad the story sounds. Before the list, sixty seconds on the one thing that makes every label make sense.

Beef grading in Canada is voluntary. The Canadian Beef Grading Agency, the industry body that does the grading, says it in one sentence: grading is not mandatory, but the grade is the commonly accepted criteria used to establish market value. In the week ending the twelfth of September, federally inspected plants processed just under fifty-two thousand cattle, and about four per cent of them left ungraded. That's legal. The packer marks the box Ungraded Beef and off it goes.

For the rest, there are thirteen grades, and four of them are the ones you'll ever see: Canada A, double A, triple A and Prime. The difference between them, on the rulebook's own table, is the amount of marbling in one muscle at one rib. Traces for A, slight for double A, small to moderate for triple A, slightly abundant and up for Prime. Canada's own beef marketing agency says those minimums are the same ones the Americans use for Select, Choice and Prime. So triple A is Choice. Not a rare thing, either: that same week, about seventy-three per cent of youthful carcasses graded triple A, seven per cent Prime, eighteen per cent double A.

Now the part nobody tells you. The grade is stamped on the carcass, never on your steak. The Canadian Food Inspection Agency's labelling rule says beef grades do not apply to individual beef cuts prepackaged by the retailer, so a store may only say what carcass the cut came from, and it has to say it as Cut from Canada AAA Beef. If the tray could have come from more than one grade, the store must print the lowest grade plus the words or higher. Hold onto that phrase. And the only rule that forces a grade into a beef advertisement applies to carcasses and portions of seven kilograms or more, so a flyer line for a steak with no grade on it breaks no rule at all. One more: Angus is a breed. It has no federal definition, and it is not a grade. Now the five.

Number five, Costco. And I'll be straight about why it's fifth: the record is thin, and I'm not going to pad it. Costco is not one of the stores CBC caught selling underweight meat, in either of its two rounds, and it isn't named in either class action. What there is, is a label, and a page.

The label. On the twenty-ninth of August, CTV reported on Randy Thompson of Gravenhurst, Ontario, who bought Kirkland Signature beef short ribs at the Orillia warehouse. The front sticker read, in CTV's words, Kirkland brand cut triple AAA Canada beef. The back sticker read Cut from USDA Choice beef, Product of USA, processed in Canada. Both stickers, one package. CTV went to another Costco, found beef labelled correctly, and wrote that Thompson's package may have been an accidental labelling error. Costco did not respond to CTV's request for comment. One package is not a pattern, and I won't call it one. But here's the rule it bumps into. The CFIA says imported beef must carry the foreign grade, so American beef is Cut from USDA Choice and never Canada AAA, and imported meat must say Product of, and the country, next to the name. The front of that tray and the back of that tray can't both be right.

The page. Costco Canada's website has a page called Canada AAA Beef. On the twenty-third of September it displayed: Content is currently unavailable, please check back soon. There is no Kirkland Signature fresh steak listed online at all. Every beef steak on costco dot ca is a third-party brand, the Canadian ones say Canadian triple A or Canada Prime, and every one of them showed out of stock the day I checked. Which means the grade and the country on the Kirkland tray in the warehouse exist in exactly one place: the sticker on the tray. Verdict: no weight record, no penalty, one reported label with two countries on it, and a grade page that's blank. Read the back of the tray, not the front.

Number four, Metro, and this one is about what the flyer doesn't say. Metro is one of Canada's three largest grocers, and its beef pitch is a brand. The Platinum Grill page on metro dot ca says, and this is Metro talking, made from one hundred per cent Canadian AAA Angus beef. Fine. Now open the flyers.

Toronto, this week: one steak line, Platinum Grill inside blade, and it does say Cut from Canada AAA Grade. Eleven ninety-nine a pound, twenty-six forty-three a kilo. Good. Montreal, the same company, this week and next: eight steak lines. Seven of the eight print no grade at all, and none of the eight prints a country. The one exception has triple A inside the brand name, AAA Platinum Plus rib steaks, twenty-six ninety-nine a pound. Then the discount banner. Super C, Metro's discount chain in Quebec, has a line in next week's flyer for what the flyer calls a bone-in striploin steak, eight eighty-eight a pound, nineteen fifty-eight a kilo, marked fifty-nine per cent off a regular of forty-eight eighty-five. That is the cheapest striploin in this whole video. The flyer says nothing about what grade it was cut from and nothing about what country. Not double A, not ungraded, not or higher. Nothing.

And look at the small packs. Metro Montreal's ready-to-cook two-hundred-gram Thai hanger steak at eleven ninety-nine works out to fifty-nine ninety-five a kilo, the highest per-kilo figure on any Metro line, above the Platinum Plus rib steak, and the pack states no grade. The fairness: Metro has no meat-weight finding against it, it wasn't in either CBC round, it isn't in either lawsuit, and the only weight complaints on its file are a ham and a lamb at a Food Basics in Sault Ste. Marie years ago, both fixed with no fine. Verdict: the brand promises triple A; the Quebec flyers, on seven of eight lines, promise no grade at all. Find the words Cut from on the tray before you trust the brand name.

Here's a label that's on some of those trays, and the reason I know is the flyer. Two of the Quebec lines this fortnight carry the word tenderized: IGA's tenderized steak or sandwich steak at ten ninety-nine a pound, and Metro's tenderized sandwich cutlet steaks at twelve ninety-nine. Tenderized is a flyer word. The law has a different one. Since the twenty-first of August, twenty fourteen, the Food and Drug Regulations, section B fourteen point zero two two, say no person shall sell mechanically tenderized beef unless it's identified as mechanically tenderized where it's offered for sale, and the label carries the words mechanically tenderized on the front panel in type at least as legible and prominent as the name of the cut. Blades, needles, or an injected marinade all count, and the CFIA says that includes fast-fry and minute steaks. The same guidance says the requirement does not apply to advertisements. So the flyer can say tenderized, or say nothing; the tray must say mechanically tenderized, as big as the name. That's the label to photograph. A viewer on our February beef video wrote that Costco's higher-end meat is tenderized and to read the small print. I couldn't verify that from a desk, and I'm not stating it. But it's a two-second check in any of these stores.

Number three, Walmart Canada. Two documents, nine years apart. The first is Walmart's own press release, May eleventh, twenty seventeen: starting that day, every cut of beef Walmart sells is one hundred per cent Canadian AAA Angus beef. Its words. The second is what its online listings say now. On the twenty-third of September, the Walmart steak listings in the flyer platform's index came in two families. Walmart's own-cut lines, like the AA striploin steak at seventeen fifty-nine a tray, say double A and Canadian in the copy: cut from AA Canadian boneless striploin. Double A. Not triple A. The Your Fresh Market lines, the striploin at twenty-four ninety-nine, the rib steak at twenty-two forty-six, the tri-tip at twenty-six twenty-three, say triple A Angus beef, and none of them names a country in the listing I could read. So the line that says Canadian says double A, and the line that says triple A doesn't say Canadian. An online listing isn't a tray; the tray may well say Product of Canada. Film it. One more thing you can't do from those listings: work out the price per kilo. Walmart lists steak by the package with a weight range, point one three to point four of a kilo for that striploin, so the same seventeen fifty-nine is anywhere from forty-four to a hundred and thirty-five dollars a kilo depending on the tray you pick. The per-kilo rate is on the label. Read it there.

Then the scale. In January twenty twenty-five, CBC reported on a Walmart in Richmond, B.C., where meat packages appeared to have been weighed with the packaging: a calculated overcharge of three dollars and seven cents, six point nine per cent, on a forty-seven dollar bill. Walmart's spokesperson said the company takes concerns of this nature very seriously and pointed to third-party partners who weighed the meat in the stores. Two weeks later, the company said the third party had told it the issue was limited to select products and happened during a two-week period in December. CBC noted a customer's photo of an underweight package dated the eighth of October. Walmart is a named defendant in the proposed Federal Court class action on meat weights. That's an allegation, it isn't certified, and nothing has been found. Verdict: one store, one visit, one explanation that didn't match the photo, and a triple A promise whose Canadian half has gone quiet in the listings I could read. Check the country line and the per-kilo line before the price.

Number two, Sobeys, and the banners its parent Empire owns: Safeway, Thrifty Foods, FreshCo, IGA, Farm Boy, Longo's. This is the company that prints the grade best and has the longest weight record, and both halves are documented.

The printing first, because it's to Sobeys' credit. Every steak line in this week's Sobeys, Safeway and Alberta IGA flyers states a grade and a country. The Sterling Silver striploin: cut from Canada AAA grade beef, twenty-four ninety-nine a pound, fifty-five oh nine a kilo. And on the cheap line, the cross rib simmering steak at eight ninety-nine, it prints Canada AA. Sobeys is the only chain in this video that prints the lower grade on the cheaper cut instead of leaving it blank. But the same company, two provinces over, prints nothing. IGA in Quebec, which is Sobeys Quebec, ran six steak lines across this week and next, including the very same Sterling Silver striploin at twenty-three ninety-nine, and not one of the six states a grade or a country. Thrifty Foods in B.C., same brand, same company: Sterling Silver top sirloin, thirteen ninety-nine a pound, no grade. So the words exist. Whether you get them depends on the province.

And about that brand. Sobeys' pages say Sterling Silver is cut from Canada AAA beef and aged for a minimum of fourteen days. Sterling Silver's own site, which belongs to Cargill Meat Solutions, says a minimum of twenty-one days, the top two-thirds of triple A by marbling, and that its beef comes from three facilities in the U.S. and two in Canada. Both are company claims. They disagree on the days, and only one of them mentions the United States. The country on your tray is the tie-breaker.

Now the scale. Late twenty twenty-four, CBC found underweight meat at a FreshCo in Toronto, seven per cent over on a thirty-eight dollar bill. January twenty twenty-five, Pete's Frootique in Halifax, six per cent. Then, in April this year, CBC went back. Two Safeways and a Thrifty Foods in North Vancouver, and two Farm Boys in the Toronto area, all Empire. At Farm Boy, eight of nine packages tested weighed less than their labels claimed, even with the packaging included, and CBC wrote that Farm Boy yielded the highest overcharges. Sobeys' spokesperson, Karen White-Boswell, did not dispute the findings. She said that on occasion when errors occur, the company investigates the issue so it can be corrected, and that Farm Boy's product is packaged and weighed at a CFIA-certified facility. The CFIA told CBC it does not certify facilities. Sobeys is a named defendant in the proposed Federal Court class action; allegation, not certified. Verdict: the best grade printing in the country in Ontario and Alberta, none in Quebec and B.C., and five Empire banners in the weight record across two years. Weigh it when you get home.

Here's where the money is, and why the grade line matters more this year than any year before. Statistics Canada's monthly retail price for beef striploin cuts was thirty-eight thirty-six a kilo in July. Two Julys ago it was twenty-nine eighteen. Up thirty-one and a half per cent in two years. Top sirloin, twenty-eight twenty-two. Rib cuts, thirty-three seventy-five. Ground beef, sixteen forty-nine. Behind that: the herd. Eleven point one million cattle on Canadian farms on the first of January, and that was the first year-over-year increase since twenty eighteen, off a herd that a year earlier was the smallest since nineteen eighty-eight. And eighteen federally inspected cattle plants in the whole country, three of which, run by Cargill and JBS, handle eighty-five per cent of the capacity, according to the chair of the House agriculture committee, quoted by CBC in July.

Now put the flyers on top of that average. This week's striploin at Sobeys: fifty-five oh nine a kilo, Canada AAA. Save-On-Foods: fifty-seven thirty, no grade, no country, the dearest striploin sold by the pound in the set, and Save-On isn't on this list only because that flyer line is the only document on its file. IGA Quebec: fifty-two eighty-nine, no grade. Every one of those is thirty-eight to forty-nine per cent above the national average, on sale. Under the average: Super C at nineteen fifty-eight, no grade, and T and T at thirty-seven forty-six, no grade. The closest thing above it with a grade printed is Longo's at forty-one eighty-seven, Cut from Canada AAA, on sale from a stated eight dollars a pound more. So this week, in this set, a printed triple A striploin cost you between forty-two and fifty-five dollars a kilo, and an unprinted anything cost you between twenty and fifty-seven. The blank line isn't a discount. It's a blank. That's the payoff of the whole exercise, and if you want it done for whatever's in your cart next, subscribe. One store left, and it's the one with the most paper.

Number one, Loblaw. Loblaws, Real Canadian Superstore, No Frills, Fortinos, Zehrs, Provigo, Maxi. Most paper, most recent, and the only steak-specific enforcement in the file.

Start with the words, because Loblaw does print them. Almost every random-weight beef line in a Loblaw flyer carries a grade line. But read it. Superstore Toronto, outside round marinating steak, twelve dollars a pound: cut from Canada AA or USDA Select grade beef or higher. No Frills Halifax, top blade, eleven dollars: Canada AA or USDA Select grade beef or higher. Provigo Montreal, top sirloin, twenty-one dollars a pound, forty-six thirty a kilo: the same clause, in French. Remember the rule. Or higher means the store is committing to the lowest grade that could be in the tray. So the flyer's promise is double A, or the American Select, and the country is Canada or the United States, either one. It's legal, it's honest, and it tells you almost nothing. The lines that said Canada AAA or higher this week were two: the tri-tip at Loblaws and Fortinos in Toronto, ten ninety-nine. Two Loblaw lines print no grade at all: the blade steak at Superstore out west at fourteen dollars a pound, and the New Zealand striploin three-hundred-gram packs at No Frills, fourteen dollars each, forty-six sixty-seven a kilo. Loblaws Ottawa and Zehrs sell a flank as cut from Australian grade beef, which is a country, not a grade. And Maxi, the Quebec discount banner, prints neither on either of its steak lines.

Now the scale, and this is the file. Late twenty twenty-three: a Winnipeg shopper, Iris Griffin, weighed her Superstore ground beef, found it a hundred and thirty-four grams short, and complained to the CFIA. In January twenty twenty-five, CBC reported, in its words, that the Loblaw grocery chain overcharged customers by selling underweighted meat across eighty stores for an undisclosed period that ended in December twenty twenty-three. Loblaw said it was a small number of products, caused by an error involving a change in packaging, and that it was fixed. The CFIA didn't visit a store and didn't issue a fine, because the grocer reported it had fixed the problem. The same month, CBC found more underweight items at a Loblaws in Toronto and a No Frills in Calgary, five per cent on a hundred-and-seven-dollar bill, and Loblaw apologized and said it had refreshed its in-store training.

Then April this year, and this is steak. CBC bought three beef briskets at a Real Atlantic Superstore near Halifax and three more at a Superstore in downtown Halifax. All six were underweight, almost four dollars over on the six combined, four point seven per cent. And in British Columbia, a Real Canadian Superstore sold underweight beef strip loin. The CFIA issued a warning. No fine. That warning, on strip loin, is the only piece of federal enforcement on a steak in this entire video, and it's a warning. Loblaw's response: the problem was limited to one product at a small number of stores, protocols reviewed with staff, and, in its words, we are truly sorry this happened, we take weight accuracy seriously. Loblaw is a named defendant in both proposed class actions, the Federal Court one and Iris Griffin's in Manitoba, which alleges the company has included the packaging weight in the prices charged. Both are allegations. Neither is certified. No court has found anything.

And the enforcement picture around all of it. The CFIA did six meat-weight spot checks in the whole of fiscal twenty twenty-three, twenty-four. Since January twenty twenty-five, two hundred and thirty-six. Result: warnings to seven retailers, zero fines, and a fine limit the government says it's reviewing. If a mis-weighed steak scans at the till, the Scanner Price Accuracy Code that gives you the item free won't help you, because the code excludes items priced by weight. Verdict: the most complete flyer disclosure in the country and it's a floor clause, an eighty-store weight episode, six briskets out of six, and a federal warning on strip loin. Weigh it. Every time.

Now the part the title doesn't say. This doesn't mean never buy a steak at these five. It means each of them has a document that tells you which line to check before you pay. And the chains that aren't on the list aren't clean by verdict; they're off it by paper. Co-op in the west prints Canada AA and aged fourteen days on every line and has no record. T and T prints nothing on four lines and has no record. Giant Tiger, FreshCo and Food Basics had no steak in their flyers this week at all.

So, by what you're doing. If you're buying the cheapest steak on the flyer: Super C at nineteen fifty-eight with a blank grade, or Sobeys cross rib at nineteen eighty-two with Canada AA printed. The second one tells you what you're buying. If you're buying a triple A striploin: Longo's at forty-one eighty-seven printed it, Sobeys at fifty-five oh nine printed it, Save-On at fifty-seven thirty didn't. If you're buying at Walmart or Costco, the per-kilo rate and the country are on the tray and not reliably anywhere else, so that's where you read them. If the flyer says tenderized, the tray must say mechanically tenderized, as big as the name. And if you're buying at a Loblaw or an Empire banner, the record says put it on a kitchen scale, tray off, and compare it with the net weight printed on the label, which under federal rules can't include the packaging.

Here's the one thing to do. Next time you pick up a steak, photograph the label. Three lines: the grade line, whether it says cut from Canada AAA, double A or higher, or nothing; the origin line, whether it says Product of Canada, Product of USA, or nothing; and the net weight, next to the number on your own scale with the tray off. Then tell me in the comments which store, which of those three lines was missing, and whether your scale matched. Every claim in this video is a document with a date on it, listed below. If yours shows something different on the shelf, that's the next video. Read the tray before you trust the sign.
