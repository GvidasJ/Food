TITLE: We Tested Steaks From The Keg, Montana's, Moxies & 6 More Canadian Chains | These FAILED

Three criteria, and we set them before we read a single menu. One: what is actually in the steak — the grade, the origin, the aging, and whether the chain will tell you any of it. Two: does the kitchen practice match what the menu implies — because there is one thing Canadian steakhouses are legally allowed to do to your steak without ever telling you, and we will get to it. Three: does the price match the plate — because Canadian beef has gone through the roof, and the number on that menu is now a serious question. Nine chains, three criteria, ordered from the biggest steakhouse in the country down. A steak is the most unforgiving test in restaurant food. There is no sauce to hide behind, no breading, no seasoning system — either the cut was good and the cook was precise, or it wasn't. And here is the fact that frames everything tonight: in Canada, restaurant food is exempt from the labelling laws that govern every package in the grocery store. A steakhouse does not have to publish an ingredient list, does not have to publish nutrition, and — with one exception we will get to — does not have to substantiate a single quality claim it prints on the menu. Ontario forces calories onto the menu boards of chains with twenty or more locations, and that is the entire legal obligation. Everything else — "AAA," "aged," "Canadian," "hand-cut" — is a claim the chain chose to make, or chose not to. So tonight we did the only test that actually works: we read what every one of these chains says about its own beef, in its own words, on its own website, and we checked those words against Canada's actual grading system and Canada's actual regulations. Some chains tell you everything. One of the biggest tells you almost nothing. Let's start at the top.

Chain one: The Keg. Ninety-nine locations across Canada, the biggest steakhouse chain in the country, founded in 1971 in North Vancouver by George Tidball as the Keg 'n Cleaver — a genuine Canadian institution, the place where this country goes for anniversaries and graduations and the good news you want to celebrate over a filet. So here is what we found, and it surprised us more than anything else in this video. We went through The Keg's entire public website — every menu, every dish description, every steak page — searching for the words that describe beef quality in Canada. Canada Prime: not there. AAA: not there. Canadian beef: not there. Certified Angus: not there. Dry-aged, wet-aged, a number of days: not there. The only "Angus" anywhere on The Keg's website is on the wine list and the beer list — Angus the Bull Cabernet, a Black Angus IPA. Their entire sourcing claim, repeated on every menu in the country, is this sentence: "our steaks are always fresh, aged for extra tenderness and flavour, and grilled to your liking using our signature Keg steak spice." Aged. That is the whole claim. Not aged for a number of days, not dry-aged, just aged — and as we will show you in a few minutes, "aged" has no legal definition in this country whatsoever. And watch the language on their most famous dish: the Keg Classic Prime Rib. Prime rib is the name of a cut — the rib roast. It is not the grade Canada Prime. Two completely different words that sound identical at a table after a glass of wine, and the menu copy calls it "well marbled" without ever naming a grade. Now the prices, from their own current Ontario menu: a six-ounce top sirloin is $38, the twelve-ounce New York is $56, a seven-ounce filet is $55, and the twenty-ounce bone-in rib steak is $72 — and that rib steak is 1,380 calories before the potato. And while we are here, the ownership story almost nobody has reported, because it happened quietly last year. For twenty-three years, ordinary Canadians could literally own a piece of The Keg — the Keg Royalties Income Fund traded on the Toronto Stock Exchange from 2002, paying unitholders a slice of every steak sold. In August 2025, Fairfax Financial bought that fund out at $18.60 a unit and delisted it. Then, a month later, on September 16th, The Keg was spun out of Recipe Unlimited entirely and made a standalone company again — still under Fairfax, but with its own board, chaired by Richard Jaffray. If that name is familiar: Jaffray co-founded Cactus Club, in North Vancouver, in 1988 — seventeen years after The Keg was founded in the same suburb. So the man who built The Keg's most direct upscale competitor now chairs The Keg. Canadians can no longer buy a unit of it, and the royalty-fund era of Canadian restaurants — A&W collapsed its fund in 2024 — is effectively over. Here is the fair part, and it matters: nothing in that menu sentence is false. The Keg has been grilling steaks for over fifty years, the execution is famously consistent, and plenty of people would tell you it is the best steak they have ever had. But at $72 a steak, the country's largest steakhouse is the only chain in tonight's entire lineup that will not tell you what grade of beef it serves. Verdict: fails criterion one — not on quality, on disclosure. When a smaller chain charging half the price names a grade and The Keg doesn't, that silence is a choice.

Chain two: Montana's BBQ and Bar. Ninety-six locations, part of Recipe Unlimited — which is owned by Fairfax Financial of Toronto, and which also owns Kelseys, Original Joe's, Swiss Chalet, Harvey's, St-Hubert and East Side Mario's, more than 1,100 restaurants in total. So a big chunk of tonight's list answers to one Toronto holding company; keep that in mind. Montana's makes the boldest aging claim in the entire Canadian casual-dining category, right on the menu: their top sirloin is "aged for 50 days." Fifty days. At a family barbecue chain where that steak is one of the cheapest on this list. Now — dry-aged or wet-aged? The menu does not say. And that is the whole question, because those are two completely different processes with two completely different price tags, and the difference is worth explaining right now, because it applies to half the chains tonight. Dry aging means hanging beef in open air in a temperature and humidity controlled room, where it loses ten to thirty percent of its weight in moisture and grows a hard crust that has to be trimmed off and thrown away. That is why dry-aged steak is expensive: you are paying for the beef that evaporated. Wet aging means the beef sits vacuum-sealed in its own juices in a plastic bag. It loses nothing, costs almost nothing, and here is the key part — beef is *already* shipped in vacuum bags. Wet aging is, functionally, time passing while beef travels and waits. And we searched the Canadian Food Inspection Agency's own standards document for meat products, cover to cover, for "aged," "dry-aged," and "wet-aged." Zero results. There is no Canadian legal standard for any of those words. The only rule is the general one: claims must be truthful and not misleading. So here is the tell you can use forever: when a restaurant dry-ages its beef, it says "dry-aged" on the menu in bold letters, because it costs a fortune and it is the best marketing they have. When a menu says only "aged" — fifty days, twenty-eight days, any number of days — it means the bag. Credit where it is due: Montana's publishes full nutrition, which most of this list does not, and their seven-ounce sirloin comes in at 420 calories and 390 milligrams of sodium, which is genuinely modest. Verdict: honest nutrition, unspecified aging. Fifty days in a vacuum bag is a long time in a bag.

Chain three: Kelseys Original Roadhouse. Sixty-six locations — and here is a fact even most Ontarians don't know: sixty-four of those sixty-six are in Ontario. It is effectively a provincial chain wearing a national jacket, also under Recipe Unlimited, also under Fairfax. And Kelseys gave us the strangest single finding in this investigation. On their menu, the eight-ounce sirloin with balsamic mushroom is described as "Fresh AAA 8oz. sirloin steak." The eight-ounce sirloin with creamy cajun shrimp: "Fresh AAA 8oz. sirloin steak." And the plain eight-ounce sirloin, the one with no sauce on it? "Fresh 8oz. sirloin grilled to your liking." No AAA. Same chain, same size, same cut, same menu page — the grade claim appears when there is a sauce on it and disappears when there isn't. Now, the honest reading, and we are going to be fair here because that is how this channel works: this is almost certainly inconsistent menu copywriting, not two different steaks. We are not alleging the plain sirloin is a lesser cut, because we have no evidence of that and neither does anyone else. But it is a perfectly reasonable thing to ask your server, and it illustrates the entire problem with quality claims on Canadian menus: nobody is checking them. A nutrition claim — "low fat," "source of fibre" — legally triggers mandatory disclosure the moment a restaurant prints it. A quality claim — AAA, aged, Canadian, hand-cut — triggers absolutely nothing. That asymmetry is the loophole this whole video lives inside. Verdict: mixed, and worth one polite question at the table.

Chain four: Moxies. Forty-nine Canadian locations, owned by Northland Properties — the Gaglardi family of Vancouver, one of the largest privately held hospitality companies in North America, which also owns Chop Steakhouse, Denny's Canada, Sandman Hotels, Revelstoke Mountain Resort, and — genuinely — the Dallas Stars of the National Hockey League. Your steak dinner in Burnaby helps fund a hockey team in Texas. Moxies makes a real, specific, checkable claim, printed at the top of their steak section: "100% of our steaks come from Canadian Ranches." That is origin, stated plainly, and it deserves credit — it is more than The Keg says. What they don't name is a grade or an aging process. And the number that matters here is sodium, because Moxies publishes a full nutrition guide and most chains don't: their plain eight-ounce sirloin carries 1,400 milligrams of sodium — sixty-one percent of your entire daily limit — before a single side. The rib eye: 960 calories, 1,550 milligrams. That is the steak alone, not the meal. Prices: sirloin $45, New York $57, rib eye $63. Verdict: origin claimed and credited, grade unstated, and a salt load worth knowing about before you order. If this is the kind of menu-reading you want done every week, hit like — it genuinely helps other Canadians find this before their next night out.

Chain five: MR MIKES SteakhouseCasual. Roughly fifty locations, overwhelmingly in Alberta and B.C. — the western Canadian entry, and one that keeps growing while the sector shrinks. Trade coverage reports MR MIKES serving "Canada AAA steaks such as centre-cut sirloin, rib eye, and filet mignon," aged twenty-eight and a half days. We want to be straight about our sourcing: we could confirm that claim in industry press, but not on MR MIKES' own website, which doesn't state it in text we could pull — so treat it as reported rather than verified, and look at the in-store menu when you go. If it holds, it is a specific, mid-priced, grade-named claim from a western chain, which is more than the biggest name in the country offers. Verdict: promising, pending the menu in your hand.

Chain six: Milestones Grill and Bar. Forty-five locations, and this is the shortest entry in the video, because we could not find a single beef claim anywhere on their site — no grade, no origin, no aging, no Angus, nothing, including in their own published ingredient listing document. Their menus are locked behind a location gate and a bot check, so we could not pull steak descriptions or prices at all. We are not going to guess. Verdict: the same grade we gave Dairy Queen in our burger video — fails the show-your-work test, not because of what we found, but because of how little there was to find.

Chain seven: Jack Astor's. Thirty-five locations, owned by SIR Corp of Burlington, Ontario — and here is the entry that proves everything else on this list was a choice. Their steak section header reads, verbatim: "Jack's signature classics. 100% Canadian AAA Steaks, aged 28 days, grilled to your liking." Read that sentence again, because it is the most specific mainstream steak claim in Canada: origin — Canadian. Grade — AAA. Aging — a number. From a bar-and-grill chain best known for loud music and cheap wings. One-third the size of The Keg, and it tells you three things The Keg tells you zero of. Now the counterweight, because Jack Astor's also publishes sodium, which we respect, and the number is enormous: their eighteen-ounce bone-in AAA ribeye, at $69.97, carries 1,349 calories and 2,742 milligrams of sodium. That is a hundred and nineteen percent of your entire recommended daily sodium — in one entrée, before fries, before a drink. Verdict: the best disclosure of any big chain in the country, and a plate you should split or budget your whole day around.

Chain eight: Bâton Rouge Steakhouse and Bar. Thirty-two locations, about two-thirds of them in Quebec — the most French-Canadian entry on the list, and now owned by MTY Food Group, the Montreal restaurant conglomerate, which acquired it in 2018 as part of a $248-million takeover of Imvescor. Their claim is "our AAA Canadian beef" — grade named, origin named, aging not specified. That puts them ahead of most of this list on transparency. Verdict: solid claim, worth ordering, and Quebec's answer to the national chains holds up better than the national chains do.

Chain nine: Chop Steakhouse and Grill. Nineteen locations, the other Northland Properties brand — the same Gaglardi family as Moxies — and the most complete claim in the mainstream category. Verbatim from their menu: "Our steaks are sourced from Canadian ranchers. Seasoned with Old Chicago steak spice and seared in our 1800-degree Montague grill." And on their site: "Sourced from Canadian ranchers, aged 35-days or more," plus a stated membership in the Canadian Roundtable for Sustainable Beef. Origin, aging duration, a named grill temperature, and a third-party sustainability body. What is still missing is a grade — no AAA, no Prime. Prices are competitive with everyone above: seven-ounce sirloin $39, New York $52, rib eye $62, filet $53. Verdict: the most information per menu inch from any large chain here, and priced under the market leader that tells you less.

Now the two decoders, because you cannot judge any of those claims without them. First: the grading ladder, and this is the one that changes how you read every steakhouse menu in Canada forever. Our grades, top to bottom, are Canada Prime, Triple-A, Double-A, and A — set by marbling, the fat inside the muscle. Here is the part nobody tells you: **Canada AAA is the equivalent of USDA Choice.** Not Prime — Choice. The middle. The Beef Cattle Research Council states the equivalence plainly: Canada Prime equals USDA Prime, and Canada AAA, AA and A equal USDA Choice, Select and Standard. So when a chain advertises AAA, they are advertising the good middle of the market — genuinely decent beef, just not the top. And Canada Prime, the actual top? In 2023 it was **4.8 percent** of graded beef from fed cattle in this country. One steak in twenty. Which is why the only chain in tonight's lineup that claims Canada Prime is Hy's Steakhouse — five locations, founded in Calgary in 1955, seventy years old this year, and the only name here whose menu says Prime out loud. Second decoder: Angus. Angus is a **breed**, not a grade. It describes the cow's family tree, not the quality of the meat. A steak can be one hundred percent Angus and grade Canada A — trace marbling, the bottom of the quality ladder. The good news for tonight: not one chain in this lineup leans on Angus branding, so nobody here is running that particular play.

And now the thing they are all legally allowed not to tell you — and this is the most important two minutes of this video. It is called mechanical tenderization: running beef through a machine that punctures it with blades or needles, or injecting it with a solution, to make a cheaper cut chew like a more expensive one. Health Canada's own words: "It is not apparent by just looking at the meat product whether it has undergone this process." And the reason it matters is not tenderness, it is bacteria. On an intact steak, any contamination sits on the surface, where the grill kills it in seconds — which is why a rare steak is safe. Blade tenderizing drives that surface bacteria into the middle, where a rare or medium-rare cook never gets hot enough. Health Canada's 2013 risk assessment found — their number — "a fivefold increase in risk from MTB products when compared to intact cuts of beef." This is not theoretical, and it is Canadian: it came out of the 2012 XL Foods E. coli outbreak in Brooks, Alberta — eighteen Canadians sickened, the largest beef recall in this country's history — during which, in Health Canada's words, "several cases were considered to be likely associated with the consumption of mechanically tenderized beef." So in 2014, Canada acted: since August of that year, mechanically tenderized beef sold at retail must carry the words "mechanically tenderized," plus cooking instructions — "Cook to a minimum internal temperature of 63°C," and for steaks, "Turn steak over at least twice during cooking." Go to any grocery store in Canada tonight and you can find that label. Now here is the sentence, straight from Health Canada's guidance on that same rule: it "does not apply to advertisements or to menu labelling in any food service establishment." Restaurants are exempt. The grocery store must warn you in writing. The steakhouse, cooking the identical product to the medium-rare you requested, does not have to say a word. And Health Canada's own regulatory analysis estimates tenderized beef at twenty to thirty-seven percent of overall beef production and explicitly names food service as one of the industries that has used the process for years. Two things must be said clearly. First: we found no evidence that any chain in tonight's video mechanically tenderizes its steaks — we searched all nine, and not one of them mentions it either way. That is precisely the point. Not one of them tells you, and under Canadian law, none of them has to. Second, the fairness that keeps this honest: when the CFIA tested 975 mechanically tenderized steaks from retailers in eleven Canadian cities, E. coli O157:H7 was **not detected in a single sample**, and 99.7 percent were assessed as satisfactory. The Canadian beef supply is genuinely well run. This is not a scare — it is a consent question. You are choosing a doneness for a steak whose history you are not allowed to see. If you order rare or medium-rare, you are entitled to ask one question: "is this steak whole-muscle, or is it tenderized?" A good steakhouse will know the answer instantly. And by the way — the same exemption covers meat glue: Health Canada actually proposed banning transglutaminase in solid cut meat in 2016, citing the exact risk of a formed product being cooked rare, then reversed course in 2017 and chose labelling instead. Packaged formed meat must say so in its name. Menus, again, are exempt.

Now, the money — and the part where these chains deserve a fair hearing. Canadian beef prices have gone vertical. Statistics Canada's own retail figures: striploin averaged $27.33 a kilogram in 2024 and is running $37.78 in 2026, up thirty-eight percent in two years. Ground beef hit a record $16.61 a kilo this June. Calves that went for $2.20 a pound before the pandemic now fetch $7. The cattle herd hit its smallest point since 1987 — though here is the genuinely good news nobody reported: on January 1st of this year, the Canadian herd **grew** 2.5 percent, the first increase since 2018, which is the first real signal that relief is coming, slowly, because as one economist put it, the beef cycle is measured in years. And now the number that reframes the entire menu: beef prices are up about twelve percent year over year, while restaurant menu prices are up under three. These chains are absorbing most of the shock, not passing it to you — and it is destroying them. Restaurants Canada reported this spring that thirty-six percent of Canadian restaurants are operating at a loss or merely breaking even, triple the 2019 level, with seventy-one percent reporting declining profitability. So when your Keg bill feels brutal, understand what is actually happening: it went up far less than the beef did. As the Guelph analyst Kevin Grier put it when asked who to blame for beef prices: "look in the mirror" — Canadians keep buying it, because we love it.

The final count. Passed on all three criteria: Chop Steakhouse — origin, aging duration and sustainability membership stated, priced below the market leader — and Hy's, the only chain in the country that names Canada Prime, if your budget stretches to Calgary's 1955 institution. Passed on disclosure with a warning on the plate: Jack Astor's, the most specific claim in mainstream Canada — 100% Canadian AAA, aged 28 days — attached to the highest sodium number we found. Passed on origin, incomplete on grade: Moxies and Bâton Rouge. Mixed: Kelseys, where the AAA appears and disappears with the sauce, and Montana's, whose fifty days almost certainly happened in a bag. Failed the show-your-work test: Milestones, which says nothing, and The Keg — the biggest, most beloved, most expensive steakhouse in the country, whose entire quality claim is one unregulated word. And one last piece of Canadian trivia to take with you: Outback Steakhouse closed all nine of its Ontario restaurants in 2009 and never came back — two tourist-strip locations in Niagara Falls are all that remain, and they don't even appear on Outback's own store locator. Texas Roadhouse and LongHorn, the two chains that dominate this format in the United States, have zero Canadian locations. The chains in tonight's video are, almost entirely, ours. So here is the whole night in one habit that costs nothing: before you order, read the steak section for three words — a grade, an origin, and a number of days. The chains that print all three have made a decision about what you deserve to know. The chains that print none of them have made a different one, and it is not an accident. Then, if you like your steak pink, ask the tenderizing question.
