Every December, a report comes out that tells Canadians what their groceries will cost next year. It gets a day of headlines, a number gets repeated on every morning show in the country, and then everybody forgets about it.

Last December the number was four to six per cent. Canada's Food Price Report, sixteenth edition, Dalhousie with Guelph, UBC, Saskatchewan and four other universities. A family of four, they said, would spend up to seventeen thousand five hundred and seventy-one dollars on food in 2026. Nearly a thousand more than last year.

Here's what actually happened. January came in at four point eight per cent. February, four point one. July, three point one. And then on the fourteenth of this month, Statistics Canada published August, and the number was two point eight.

That's not just below the forecast. Here is the sentence Statistics Canada wrote, word for word: "For the first time since July 2024, grocery price growth increased at a slower pace than the all-items CPI in August 2026."

Groceries are now rising slower than everything else you buy. For the first time in over two years.

So let me say the thing this kind of video usually refuses to say. Nothing is running out. There is no shortage in this country. Supply has been fine all year, and anyone telling you otherwise is selling something.

This is a video about a window. Because a calm price today almost never means a calm price next year. It usually just means nobody has noticed the pressure building yet.

Every item here had to pass one test, and it's a test Canadian law actually defines. Under the Food and Drug Regulations, a prepackaged food only has to carry a best-before date if its durable life is ninety days or less. The Canadian Food Inspection Agency spells it out: foods with a shelf life greater than ninety days "are not required to be labelled with a best before date."

So everything here is a food that, by Canada's own rules, doesn't need a date on it. That's the filter, and it rules out more of the grocery store than you'd expect.

Fifteen foods, counting down from the least urgent to the one I'd act on today. Real Statistics Canada numbers, real tariff schedules, real harvest data. Let's start at fifteen.

Number fifteen. Pasta.

It earns the bottom spot for the best possible reason. It's getting cheaper and almost nobody has noticed. Statistics Canada's index for dry or fresh pasta is down seven and a half per cent year over year. In a store where the basket has gone up twenty-nine per cent in five years, pasta has gone backwards.

Here's why, and it's counterintuitive. Canada's durum crop — the specific wheat pasta is made from — is twelve point two per cent smaller this year. But Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada says world durum supply is up six per cent and the global stockpile is climbing another twenty per cent. And Algeria, Canada's biggest durum customer at one point four million tonnes, grew enough of its own and stopped buying.

Our crop shrank, the world's pile got bigger, and the price came down.

Buy plain dry durum semolina pasta and check that "durum wheat semolina" is the first ingredient. Skip anything labelled a pasta mix — Statistics Canada tracks those as a separate, far more expensive line.

Number fourteen. Sugar.

Two kilograms of white sugar in Canada cost two dollars and sixty-four cents in July. A year earlier it was three twenty-three. That's down eighteen point three per cent, and it's the cheapest this series has been since March of 2023.

Over ninety per cent of Canada's refined sugar comes from raw cane imported from South and Central America, duty-free. The only Canadian sugar beet operation is at Taber, Alberta.

And here's what makes it a buy-now item. Rogers Sugar has capitalized a hundred and seventy-eight million dollars so far on an expansion at its Montreal refinery, and in the company's own second-quarter filing the expected in-service date is the first half of calendar 2027. The capacity that would keep sugar loose is not here yet.

And watch the split, because it's strange. Raw sugar is down. Confectionery — the things made out of sugar — is up six and a half per cent, accelerating from two per cent in July. Cheap sugar is not producing cheap chocolate bars.

Buy the plain two-kilo paper bag. Skip the blends.

Number thirteen. Peanut butter.

One kilogram of peanut butter in Canada: five dollars and sixty-two cents in July, down from six sixteen a year earlier. That's down eight point eight per cent, and per kilogram it is the cheapest protein staple in this entire video.

There's a Canadian story behind the jar. Kraft Peanut Butter is made at the Mont Royal plant in Montreal — the same building that makes Kraft Dinner and Philadelphia cream cheese, more than a thousand workers in it. In March, Kraft Heinz announced a two hundred and fifty million dollar investment to modernize it. Their own brand page says it: since 1960, Kraft Peanut Butter has been a Canadian classic.

Made in Montreal. The peanuts are a different question. Dr. Sylvain Charlebois, the food economist, has publicly stated that Kraft Peanut Butter is made in Montreal but with peanuts sourced from the United States. That's his statement, not the company's — Kraft Heinz's own pages don't say.

Buy the conventional shelf-stable jar, not the natural style. The natural jars carry a keep-refrigerated instruction — under Canada's labelling rules, exactly the class of product that has to declare storage different from room temperature. The conventional jar is the one that sits on a shelf.

Number twelve. Flour.

This one is a trap, and the trap is instructive.

Look at the year-over-year number and flour looks calm: Statistics Canada has flour and flour-based mixes down one point nine per cent. Now look at the direction inside this year. Since December, that same index is up eight point two per cent.

The annual figure is a lagging artifact. The in-year direction is not, and it's going the other way. That's the whole thesis of this video, on one line of one table.

Underneath it, the Canadian wheat crop is down ten point nine per cent to thirty-six point one million tonnes, on both smaller acreage and weaker yields. And Parrish and Heimbecker, the largest Canadian-owned milling company, is opening a two hundred and forty-one million dollar flour mill at Springbrook in Red Deer County this fall. Seven hundred and fifty tonnes of wheat a day, two hundred and thirty thousand tonnes of Alberta wheat a year. A brand new mill, opening into a crop that's eleven per cent smaller.

Buy all-purpose or bread flour in the biggest plain paper bag you'll use. Not self-rising, not flour-based mixes — Statistics Canada tracks those separately and they're the line carrying the added ingredients.

Number eleven. Rice.

Canada grows no rice. Not commercially, not anywhere. Every single bag in every Canadian store was imported.

And this year the world rice market came apart in the best possible way. The FAO estimates record world production. India, which handles roughly forty per cent of all rice traded on earth, is sitting on a record government stockpile, with prices reported down over thirty per cent this year.

So what happened in Canada? Statistics Canada's rice index moved up zero point six per cent.

Thirty per cent off the world price, and your bag moved six-tenths of one per cent. And to be precise, because you'll see wilder claims than this: two kilos of white rice averaged six dollars and forty-six cents in June of 2024, hit ten dollars and twenty cents in March of 2025, and has never come back down. It was nine sixty-two in July.

Here's the part to pay attention to. That calm rests on one government's export policy. India removed its minimum export price on basmati in September 2024 and Pakistan removed theirs within days. No planting cycle, no drought — one decision, which can be reversed just as fast.

Buy white, not brown, if you're buying to store. The bran layer on brown rice carries oil, and per kilogram white rice at four eighty-one beats brown at six fifty-seven.

Number ten. Canned tomatoes.

This is the best buying rule in the entire video, and it's written into federal regulation.

Open the Canadian Grade Compendium, Volume Three, section forty-seven. Whole, diced or chopped canned tomatoes in Canada carry one of three grades: Canada Fancy, Canada Choice, Canada Standard. Canada Fancy must contain not less than sixty-five per cent drained solids. Canada Choice, not less than sixty. Canada Standard, not less than fifty.

Fifteen percentage points of actual tomato, in the same size can, depending on one word on the label.

And here's the second half, which is the part nobody tells you. Under the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations, whole and diced tomatoes have to be graded and carry that grade name. Grading is optional for purée, pulp, paste, stewed tomatoes and ketchup. So on those there is no legal quality signal at all.

Ontario grows this. Three plants process it: Highbury Canco at Leamington, Sun-Brite at Ruthven, and Conagra at Dresden — and that Dresden plant makes Aylmer, Hunt's and Ro-Tel, all three, out of Ontario tomatoes processed within twenty-four hours of harvest. About five hundred and nineteen thousand tonnes this season.

A seven hundred and ninety-six millilitre can averaged two dollars and twenty-seven cents in July, up four point one per cent. Buy whole or diced, look for Canada Fancy, skip the purée.

Number nine. Lentils and dried peas.

Canada is the largest lentil exporter on earth. Saskatchewan alone accounts for eighty-five per cent of Canadian lentil production.

Now here's the number that should make every Canadian pause. Last crop year Canada exported two and a half million tonnes of lentils. Total Canadian domestic use, according to Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada: a quarter of a million tonnes.

Ten to one. We grow the world's lentils and eat almost none of them.

And the supply is turning right now. Statistics Canada, in its March seeding report, wrote that farmers expect dry pea acreage to fall twelve point three per cent, and named the reason in its own words: "lower returns relative to other crops because of tariffs in place from importing countries." India is the buyer, and India put duties on.

So acreage fell. And Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada now forecasts lentil production down twenty-six per cent next year and dry peas down twenty, and says directly that the average price for both is expected to be higher than this year.

That is the federal department telling you, in a published outlook, that the price goes up. Right now dried lentils are three sixty-three for nine hundred grams.

Buy dried, not canned. And buy red over green: green carried a forty-five dollar a tonne premium last year, green acreage is falling while red rose, and red is where Canadian supply is deepest.

Number eight. Black pepper.

Pepper is quiet right now. Statistics Canada's condiments, spices and vinegars line is up zero point seven per cent — one of the categories the agency specifically named as slowing grocery inflation in August. And it is the clearest example in this video of a calm price hiding a structural problem.

Vietnam supplies well over a third of the world's pepper, and Vietnamese output for 2026 is projected down fifteen to twenty per cent. That would normally correct itself: farmers see a high price, farmers plant more.

Except they haven't. The reporting on the ground is that replanting remains limited, because other crops pay better and land is tight. And a pepper vine takes three to four years to reach full production.

So the price of pepper in 2029 was already decided, in 2026, and the decision was no.

Whole peppercorns hold their character for years in a sealed jar. Ground pepper does not. Buy whole and grind it yourself.

That's eight down and seven to go. Number seven. Oats.

This number was published the morning I wrote this.

Statistics Canada, September sixteenth, 2026: Canadian oat production is three million tonnes, down twenty-two point seven per cent. Harvested area down nineteen point nine per cent to two point one million acres.

And it gets tighter underneath. Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada says the 2026 seeded area is twenty-one per cent below the five-year average and the second lowest on record, and carry-out stocks are projected to fall to four hundred thousand tonnes — among the lowest levels on record.

Saskatchewan grows over forty per cent of it, Alberta over twenty-five.

And here's the competing buyer, which is what makes this a Canadian story rather than a weather story. Of Canada's raw oat grain exports, the United States took almost eighty per cent. Of Canadian oat product exports, nearly ninety-five.

The oats grown in Saskatchewan are mostly going south.

Buy large-flake or steel-cut whole oats in the plain bag, not flavoured instant sachets. The whole-grain forms are what the Canadian mills actually produce from that Prairie crop, and there's nothing added to degrade.

Number six. Maple syrup.

Quebec produces about seventy-two per cent of the world's maple syrup and ninety per cent of Canada's. And Quebec operates something that exists nowhere else on the planet: an actual strategic reserve of maple syrup, run by the producers' own marketing board, in warehouses, in barrels.

The director of it, Kevin Gauthier, says it plainly on camera: "Welcome to the world's maple syrup strategic reserve."

Surplus syrup in a good year gets pasteurized, sealed and warehoused, then released in a bad one. A sixth-generation producer named David Hall put the logic better than an economist could: "ever since God was a boy scout, you have had good years and bad years in the forest."

This spring was nearly a bad one. In early April the season was running roughly thirty per cent behind on a long winter. It recovered — Quebec's producers reported a 2026 crop of two hundred and twenty-nine and a half million pounds at their May annual meeting, a hundred and eighty-one million of that headed for export.

The reserve is the shelf-life demonstration: a multi-year storage system run by the industry itself. Buy a sealed tin or glass bottle marked Canada Grade A, and note the colour class on the front — Golden, Amber, Dark or Very Dark.

Number five. Canned fish.

Canned salmon in Canada is up eight point one per cent year over year and twenty point seven per cent over two years — the worst of the three canned-fish lines Statistics Canada tracks. At retail a two hundred and thirteen gram tin went from four seventy-four to five thirty-two in a single year. Twelve point two per cent, the second-biggest one-year jump in the whole retail price table, behind only ground beef.

Now, where does it actually come from.

Clover Leaf filed a report under Canada's supply chain transparency act, and it's one of the most useful corporate documents a Canadian shopper can read. The company states it owns a sardine processing facility at Blacks Harbour, New Brunswick, with four hundred and thirty-nine employees, supplied by twelve of its own vessels. And then it says this: "The remainder of our products are produced by third-party packers outside Canada. The primary processing locations outside of Canada for products sold in Canada are Thailand, the United States, Poland and Morocco."

It also says, and I'm quoting: "Clover Leaf does not source its tuna directly."

So the sardines are genuinely Canadian. The tuna isn't made here at all. The company tells you that itself, in a federal filing.

And the sardine side has a cap on it. In 2024 the Minister of Fisheries and Oceans set the Bay of Fundy herring quota at sixteen thousand tonnes for the 2024 through 2027 seasons — down from fifty thousand tonnes in 2016. DFO's spokesperson explained the Atlantic herring stock there had been in the critical zone for a sixth consecutive year and reductions were needed to rebuild it. That quota is still binding in 2026.

Buy the Blacks Harbour sardines if you want the Canadian one, and buy canned salmon now rather than in the spring.

Number four. Cooking oil.

Two stories in the same aisle, pointing opposite ways.

Canola first, because Canada grows it. Three litres of canola oil went from nine fifty-one to ten seventy-seven in a year. Up thirteen point two per cent — the fastest-rising shelf-stable staple in the Canadian basket that this country actually produces itself.

And the reason is not the harvest — Canada planted a record twenty-three point four million acres of canola this year. The reason is who's bidding for it. Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada raised its forecast for domestic canola crush to a new peak of thirteen point seven million tonnes, twenty-eight per cent above the five-year average, and attributed it to, in their words, "continued strong demand of canola oil as a feedstock." Seed exports are forecast down twelve per cent, because more of the crop is staying home to be crushed. Cargill opened a plant at Regina this year that can process a million tonnes annually, for food oil and renewable fuels.

The three-litre jug on your shelf is bidding against a refinery.

Olive oil is the opposite: down two point three per cent this year to eleven sixteen a litre, though still up forty per cent over five years. But it has a different problem, and it's a labelling one.

The Canadian Food Inspection Agency's Food Fraud Annual Report for 2024 to 2025 ran targeted sampling of imported olive oil. Of eighty-five samples, twenty-eight came back unsatisfactory. Thirty-three per cent. What they were testing for, in CFIA's words: "Adulteration with lower value oils, including misrepresentation of claims such as 'extra virgin,' 'virgin,' 'cold pressed' and 'unrefined.'"

And the caveat CFIA puts on it, which has to be said in the same breath: "Results of targeted sampling are not representative of overall compliance in the Canadian marketplace." They deliberately aim at higher-risk products. This is not a statement about a third of the shelf.

But it tells you the buying rule. A harvest date and a single named country of origin tell you more than the words "extra virgin" ever will.

On that list is a line that reads: zero four zero nine point zero zero point zero zero. Natural honey. Fifty.

Honey stayed on.

Number two. Coffee.

This is the worst number in the Canadian grocery basket, and it isn't close.

Statistics Canada's index for roasted or ground coffee: up four point six per cent this year, up forty-one point four per cent in two years, and up eighty-six point five per cent over five. Three hundred and forty grams that cost five dollars and forty-five cents in August of 2021 cost nine fifty-four in July.

And it happened almost all at once. The index went from a hundred and twenty-nine in January 2025 to a hundred and seventy in July 2025 — thirty-two per cent in six months — and it has never come back down.

Now here's the part that should bother you.

The world price came apart. Arabica hit an all-time record of four dollars and thirty-eight cents a pound in October of last year. On the fourteenth of this month it closed at two eighty-seven — thirty-five per cent off the record, and down almost seventeen per cent in the last thirty days alone, because Brazil is heading for a record crop.

The world price fell by a third. Your tin went up four point six per cent.

Two reasons, both documented. The Bank of Canada and RBC, independently, both put the lag between a cost shock and a Canadian shelf price at six to nine months. And Canada's own Food Price Report says it explicitly: commodities with short shelf lives move through quickly, "whereas previously tariffed items with long shelf lives like coffee will take more time." The second is the loonie at a dollar thirty-nine — because every bean is imported.

Buy whole bean, not pre-ground, and buy the vacuum brick rather than the open tub. That split tells you where the damage is landing: ground coffee up eighty-six per cent over five years, instant up forty-two.

And number one. Salt.

I'm ending on the least dramatic item in the store, on purpose, because this is the one where the regret isn't about a price at all.

Under Lake Huron, eighteen hundred feet down, off Goderich, Ontario, is the largest underground salt mine in the world. Compass Minerals, which owns it, describes it exactly that way, and adds a detail that lands for anyone who's stood in downtown Toronto: the mine is as deep as the CN Tower is tall. It's been running since 1959. There's a second operation on the same site, an evaporation plant, going since 1867 — older than the country.

Canada sits on more salt than it could ever use. This is the one item on this list this country genuinely cannot run short of.

Which makes what happened in January strange. Ontario municipalities ran low on road salt while Canadian salt was going south. A contractor named Dave Gerow put it the way anyone would: "It does seem odd that we have the biggest salt mine in the world, and we can't get salt here." London declared operational adjustments. Pickering said remaining supplies had to be used carefully.

To be precise, and this matters: that was road de-icing salt, not table salt. Different production line entirely — what's in your shaker comes off that 1867 evaporation plant, or the brine wells near Windsor. Canada did not run out of table salt.

And then in August the United States put a fifty per cent tariff on roughly five hundred and sixty-nine categories of Canadian goods. Canadian salt was inside that net and got pulled out at the last minute, excluded effective the fifteenth of September — last week.

Buy the plain non-iodized drum, not the flavoured seasoning salts. For the Canadian product, look for Sifto out of Goderich, or Windsor.

And the reason it's number one is not the price. Salt barely moves and it isn't going to. It's number one because it's the one thing on this list where the regret is getting halfway through a Sunday dinner, reaching for the box, and finding it empty. Two dollars fixes that permanently.
