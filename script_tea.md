# SCRIPT — TEA EPISODE

TITLE: We Investigated 8 "Orange Pekoe" Tea Brands In Canada (Only 1 Is Still Canadian)

ALTERNATES:
- Canadians Must Read These 8 Tea Boxes Before Buying (Only 1 Is Still Canadian)
- We Investigated Canada's Favourite Tea Brands (The Box Doesn't Say What You Think)
- The 2 Biggest Words On Your Tea Box Are Legally Undefined (We Checked The Law)

---

Take a look at the biggest words on this box. Not the brand name — the words under it. "Orange Pekoe." Every one of us has said those words a thousand times. Red Rose Orange Pekoe. Tetley Orange Pekoe. It's on the shelf tag, it's on the flyer, it's what your mother asked for by name. So here's a question we thought would take five minutes to answer: where does Canadian law define what "orange pekoe" actually is?

We looked in the Food and Drug Regulations. Nothing. We looked in the Canadian Food Compositional Standards — the document where our federal food standards live. Nothing. We looked in the Canadian Grade Compendium, the official book of food grades — it has volumes for eggs, honey, maple syrup, even fish. There is no tea volume. The Canadian Food Inspection Agency's own guidance says, and this is their wording, "It is not permitted to use a Canadian grade on a product for which the regulations do not prescribe a grade standard." There is no grade standard for tea. Which means the biggest words on the best-selling tea in this country are not a grade, not a flavour, and not a legal standard of any kind. They're a tradition. And here's the part that got us: "orange pekoe" has nothing to do with oranges. Nothing. It's an old trade term for the size of the leaf.

Now — the words "black tea" on that same box? Those ARE defined in Canadian law. There's an actual chemistry exam a tea has to pass to be called black tea in Canada, with percentages and an official test method, and we're going to read it to you. And by the end of this video, we're going to show you which of these boxes is still blended in Canada — because it's exactly one of them, it's probably not the one you think, and it's been run by the same family since before Confederation ended its first decade.

We went through the tea aisle the way we always do. We read every label, we looked up who actually owns every brand, and we read the law those labels have to follow. We're not going to tell you tea is good for you or bad for you — you'll notice we never do that on this channel. We read boxes. That's all we do. And the tea aisle, quietly, is one of the strangest aisles in the store — because almost every brand on it sounds British, most of them are owned somewhere else entirely, and not one leaf of any of it grew in Canada. Tea doesn't grow here commercially. Every single bag is imported. So every Canadian-sounding word on every one of these boxes is doing careful legal work — and once you know the code, you can read it in five seconds.

First, the definition, because everything else hangs off it. Under Canada's food standards, tea is — and this is the standard's wording — "the dried leaves and buds of Camellia sinensis prepared by the usual trade processes." One plant. That's it. If it's not that plant, it is legally not tea. Which is why your peppermint box and your chamomile box say "herbal tea" — and why, if you flip to the French side, they don't say "thé" at all. They say "tisane." French shoppers get told the truth in one word: a tisane is not a thé. There's no tea in Sleepytime. We read the ingredient list on Celestial Seasonings Sleepytime, the herbal tea half this country grew up with: "Chamomile, spearmint, lemon grass, tilia flowers, blackberry leaves, orange blossoms, hawthorn and rosebuds." Lovely. Not one leaf of actual tea in the box, and legally there can't be a plain "tea" on the front of it.

And that black tea chemistry exam we promised? For decades it sat in the Food and Drug Regulations, section B.20.002, and it said black tea "shall contain, on the dry basis, not less than 30 per cent water-soluble extractive" — that's the part of the leaf that actually dissolves into your cup — "and not less than four per cent and not more than seven per cent total ash." There's even an official government test method for it, dated October 15, 1981. Green tea's bar is actually higher — 33 per cent. And "decaffeinated" is a real legal claim too: caffeine has to have been removed, and what's left can't be more than 0.4 per cent caffeine. So the small words on the box are law. The big words are marketing. Keep that in your pocket for the whole aisle.

One more thing before the brands, because it happened recently and almost nobody noticed. In December 2024, the government repealed the entire tea division of the Food and Drug Regulations. Gone. If you look up the tea standard today it just says "Repealed." Don't panic — the rules didn't disappear, they moved. They now live in a document called the Canadian Food Compositional Standards, run by the CFIA, same numbers, same test. But when they moved it, they quietly fixed something wonderful: the old law defined tea as the leaves of "Thea sinensis" — a botanical name scientists stopped using generations ago. For about seventy years, Canada's tea law was named after a plant that had been renamed. December 2024, someone finally updated it. Your tax dollars, eventually, at work.

Now let's read some boxes, in the order Canada actually buys them.

Brand one: Tetley. By the industry's own accounting, this is the number one tea in the country. That claim comes from the owner — Tata Consumer Products says, and we're quoting their own brand page, "Tetley is the market leader in Canada and has been voted Canada's Most Trusted Brand of tea." Their press releases call it "Canada's #1 tea brand." We couldn't find an independent public market-share table to check that against — nobody publishes one for free — but nobody seriously disputes that Tetley and one other brand we'll get to have owned this aisle for decades. So take the number one spot as the company's claim, but a believable one.

Here's what the box doesn't shout. Did you catch the name of the owner? Tata. Tetley — the most British-sounding tea in the aisle, the company that literally invented the British tea bag in 1953 and the round bag in 1989 — has been owned since February 2000 by India's Tata Group. They paid 271 million pounds for it, and at the time it was the biggest overseas takeover an Indian company had ever done. There's something almost poetic about it: for two hundred years Britain shipped India's tea around the world under British names, and now the most British tea brand in Canada sends its profits to Mumbai. And where is Canada's number one tea actually packed? We chased that down: Tata's own announcements celebrate the Tetley factory in Eaglescliffe, in the north of England, which — their words — makes products "for the UK, Canada and Europe." So the market leader of the Canadian tea aisle: owned in India, packed in England, leaves from wherever the blend needs. At the time of filming, the 72-bag box of Tetley Orange Pekoe was $6.99 at Loblaws, and the big 216-bag box was $16.99 — just under eight cents a bag. Hold that number; the store brands are coming for it. And the ingredient list on that box, in full: "Black (Orange Pekoe) Tea." Three words, one of which is legally defined, two of which are not.

Brand two. You knew it was coming. Red Rose. If Tetley is the market leader, Red Rose is the country's tea — the one with the slogan burned into every Canadian brain over about fifty: "Only in Canada, you say? Pity." Twenty-plus years of television ads with proper British gentlefolk discovering, to their horror, that this tea could only be had in Canada. It might be the most effective piece of Canadian-pride advertising ever made. And the pride had roots: Red Rose was founded in Saint John, New Brunswick — the company's own history says 1894 — by a New Brunswicker named Theodore Harding Estabrooks. His big idea was radical at the time: sell tea already blended and packaged, consistent every time, instead of scooped loose out of a chest at the general store. It worked so well that for decades there was a Red Rose factory right there in Saint John. And from 1967 to 2018 the boxes came with those little Wade porcelain figurines — over 300 million of them given away. Check your grandmother's china cabinet. They're there.

So. "Only in Canada." Let's read the fine print in the year 2026. We looked up who owns Red Rose today, and the answer is genuinely stranger than we expected: there are two Red Roses, and they are not the same product, and they are not owned by the same company, and neither owner is Canadian. Follow the bouncing teapot. Estabrooks retired and sold to England's Brooke Bond in 1932. Unilever bought it in 1985. Then Unilever sold the American rights separately — so in the United States, Red Rose today belongs to Redco Foods, a subsidiary of Teekanne, a German family tea company. The Canadian Red Rose stayed with Unilever — until 2022, when Unilever sold its entire global tea business to a private equity firm, CVC Capital Partners, for four and a half billion euros. That business is now called Lipton Teas and Infusions, headquartered in the Netherlands. That's who owns Canadian Red Rose as we film this. A Dutch-based, private-equity-owned tea giant here; a German-owned company there. Two different companies, two different continents, one rose.

And they're genuinely different teas. The Canadian blend is all orange pekoe grade; the American "Original Blend" mixes in broken-leaf grades, and their box waxes on about leaves "from Africa's Great Rift Valley to the flourishing tea estates of India to the lush green mountains of Ceylon." Here's our favourite part: Walmart in the United States sells the Canadian version, imported, labelled "Canada Import," at a premium — because American expats insist the Canadian one tastes better and pay extra to get it. Only in Canada, you say? Pity. The slogan outlived the ownership by forty years, and Americans are now paying import prices for our version of a tea that isn't ours anymore.

If you're enjoying this — if you're the kind of person who's now squinting at a tea box in your kitchen — do us a favour and hit the like button, and subscribe if you haven't. We read the labels, we read the law, we name the owners, and that's the whole channel. It genuinely helps more Canadians find these videos. Now: the company whose name is on that Dutch tea giant.

Brand three: Lipton. This one is a corporate riddle worthy of its own episode. The world's biggest tea brand — and in Canada's hot tea aisle, honestly, the third name at best. But pay attention, because the name "Lipton" in your grocery store now means two completely different companies depending on which aisle you're standing in. The tea bags — Yellow Label, the orange pekoe boxes — belong to Lipton Teas and Infusions, that CVC private-equity company we just met, the one that also owns Red Rose Canada and PG Tips and Pukka and Tazo. But the Lipton Iced Tea in the drinks aisle? That never left. It belongs to a joint venture between PepsiCo and Unilever, and it was specifically excluded when Unilever sold the tea business. Same name, same logo, two owners, two aisles of the same store. There is no possible way to know that from the shelf — we only know it because the sale documents say, in black and white, that the deal "excludes... Unilever's interests in the Pepsi Lipton ready-to-drink Tea joint ventures." At the time of filming, Lipton Yellow Label was $8.37 for 100 bags at Walmart — about eight and a half cents a bag. Ingredient list: "Black Tea." Two words. At least it's honest work.

Which brings us to the brands with no press releases at all. The store brands — and in the tea aisle, this is where the arithmetic gets loud. At the time of filming: No Name black tea, 100 bags, $3.50 on sale at Loblaws. That is three and a half cents a bag. President's Choice Orange Pekoe, the 216-bag club pack, listed at $9.29 — call it four point three cents. Remember Tetley at the same banner? Just under eight cents for the identical two-word ingredient list: black tea. The retail description of PC's blend even tells you the origin — "a blend of the finest black teas from Sri Lanka and Kenya" — which is more geography than most of the name brands volunteer. Who actually blends and packs the store brands? The boxes don't say, and nobody will tell you — that's the store-brand game we've hit in every single episode of this channel. But we'll note one fact and let you draw your own map: there is exactly one tea company left blending tea in Atlantic Canada, it's family-owned, and its own website says it does private-label manufacturing. We'll meet them at the end. Meanwhile, Costco does something in this aisle nobody else does: the Kirkland Signature green tea box openly co-brands with Ito En — the Japanese tea giant that makes it — and brags "100% Japanese tea leaves." The one store brand in the aisle that names its maker, and it's the warehouse store. Credit where due.

Brand five: Twinings. The aristocrat. "Est. 1706," the shop on the Strand in London, purveyors to the palace, three hundred years of heritage on every box — all true, by the way. What the front doesn't mention is that R. Twining and Company has belonged since the sixties to Associated British Foods, the conglomerate whose other famous label is... Primark. Fast fashion and fancy tea, one holding company. Twinings is also where the aisle's price gravity flips. At the time of filming, a 20-bag box of Twinings Earl Grey was $5.99 on sale at Loblaws. That is thirty cents a bag — eight or nine No Name cups for one Twinings cup. And what does thirty cents buy? The box says: "Light and aromatic blend of fine black tea, scented with bergamot, a citrus fruit." Now flip any flavoured tea over — this is the label lesson of the aisle. Tetley's Chai Vanilla, for instance, lists: "black tea, clove, natural flavours, black pepper, cinnamon, chicory, ginger, cardamom, cardamom husk, and vanilla pods." See "natural flavours" sitting in the middle of all that real spice? Canadian labelling law has a precise fork here, and it's worth thirty seconds of your life. If the flavouring was made from actual plant or animal raw material, the list may simply say "flavour." If chemical synthesis was involved anywhere, it must say "artificial flavour." That single word — "artificial" — is the legal tripwire. And long-standing CFIA guidance goes further: for a short list of products where everyone is presumed to understand it's just a flavour — the list literally includes "flavoured teas" alongside jelly powders and hard candy — a peach tea doesn't need to clarify that there are no peaches. The peach on the front of a tea box is legally decorative. The truth is in one word on the back.

Brand six — and this one's a ghost story. Salada. If you're over sixty, that name just did something to you. Salada was founded in Toronto in 1892 by a Montrealer named Peter Charles Larkin, and his innovation actually beat Red Rose to the punch: he sealed tea in foil packets so every pound tasted the same, at a time when tea came loose from a chest and tasted like whatever else the shopkeeper stored beside it. Larkin got rich enough that he was called "the Tea King of America," and Canada liked him so much we made him High Commissioner to the United Kingdom in 1922 — the Tea King, representing Canada, in London. Since then, the brand has been passed around like a fruitcake: Shirriff-Horsey in the fifties, then — of all companies — Kellogg's from 1969 to 1988, then Unilever. And today? Canadian Salada belongs to Lipton Teas and Infusions. Yes — the same Dutch private-equity company that owns Red Rose Canada. The two grand old rivals of Canadian tea, Estabrooks' rose and Larkin's foil packet, ended up in the same portfolio, and here's the punchline — the same thing happened in America, where US Salada and US Red Rose both landed with the German-owned Redco. The century-long tea war ended with both armies owned by the same people, twice, on two continents. Go find Salada on a shelf today. At the time of filming it was still listed at Walmart, but it's thin on the ground and blends have been quietly discontinued — the CBC once covered an Innu community in Quebec searching for a discontinued Salada blend they'd used for generations. When a brand fades in Canada, that's what it looks like: not an announcement. Just fewer shelves, then one, then none.

Now, while you digest the fact that basically the whole aisle is three owners in a trench coat, let us tell you the single best thing we found in this entire investigation. It's about the United States, and we promise it's relevant. From 1897, America had a federal law called the Tea Importation Act, which created a Board of Tea Experts — actual, salaried, official government tea tasters, who set "uniform standards of purity, quality, and fitness for consumption" for every tea entering the country. In 1996, Congress passed a law — the genuinely, officially named Federal Tea Tasters Repeal Act — and fired them. The whole standards regime, repealed, to save money. And in a detail we couldn't make up if we tried, the FDA didn't get around to formally deleting the tea board from its own rulebook until September 2023 — twenty-seven years after Congress abolished it. So as it stands: the United States fired its tea tasters and repealed its tea standards; Canada kept a legal definition of tea, a chemistry test for black tea, and a decaf ceiling, continuously, this entire time. Whatever else you take from this video — our tea aisle has a law under it. Theirs has a memo.

And the economics of this aisle deserve one honest minute, because tea is quietly the best deal in the beverage section. Statistics Canada tracks a standard 20-bag box: $3.42 in early 2017, $4.63 as of this summer — up about 35 per cent, which sounds bad until you look at what coffee did. That StatCan box works out to about 23 cents a cup. The big value boxes we priced earlier get you under a nickel. Even the fancy stuff is thirty cents. And one more label-law gem while we're here: look at a box of plain tea and try to find the Nutrition Facts table. It's not there — and that's legal. The regulations exempt products where every line of the table would read zero, and dried tea leaves in hot water qualify. Plain tea is one of the only things in the store with literally nothing to declare. Meanwhile — same word, different aisle — a bottle of sweetened iced tea is Health Canada's own textbook example of a drink that must now wear the new black-and-white "high in sugars" magnifying-glass symbol that became fully enforceable this past January. "Tea" spans the entire spectrum of Canadian food labelling: from nothing to declare, to wearing the warning label. Same four letters. Oh — and if a coffee shop pours your tea for you, it's taxed; the CRA's own memo says beverages "dispensed at the place where they are sold are taxable... regardless of size and whether hot or cold." The box is a zero-rated grocery. The cup is not. The kettle remains the last tax shelter in Canada.

Your grandparents knew all this in their bones, by the way. In 1942, wartime Canada rationed tea — one ounce per adult per week, or four ounces of coffee, not both, with a coupon in a ration book. An ounce of tea is maybe a dozen cups. Families saved leaves, re-steeped, stretched. Tea and coffee came off the ration in September 1944, and the country exhaled. When your mother refused to waste a tea bag, that wasn't frugality for its own sake. That was policy, remembered.

So — who wins the aisle? Here's where we landed after all of it.

If you want the one mainstream tea still blended in Canada by a Canadian company, it's been hiding in plain sight the whole time, mostly east of Quebec: King Cole. The company behind it, G.E. Barbour, was founded in Saint John, New Brunswick, in 1867 — the year of Confederation — survived the Great Fire of Saint John a decade later, launched King Cole tea in 1910, and has been blending it in New Brunswick ever since: Saint John first, then Sussex, where they moved in 1966. Family-owned — the Brenan family since 1950 — and by the CBC's account the only tea manufacturer left in Atlantic Canada. The brand copy is the plainest in the aisle: "a quality tea blend made with full-sized premium tea leaves. It's a consistent, quality cup of tea every time." Full-sized leaves — an actual claim about the actual tea, from the one box on the shelf whose blender you can drive to. The leaves are imported, of course — every tea leaf in Canada is — but the blending, the packing, the jobs and the ownership are in Sussex, New Brunswick. If your Sobeys carries it, that's the box that does what Red Rose's slogan only promised.

Two honourable mentions, both genuinely Canadian. DAVIDsTEA, from Montreal — the mall tea shop — nearly died in 2020, restructured under creditor protection, closed most of its stores, and has quietly fought its way back to profitability and is opening stores again this year. A Canadian tea company that almost vanished and didn't. And Four O'Clock, from Trans-Herbe in Saint-Bruno-de-Montarville, Quebec — a Quebec family company whose founder, the story goes, named it after hearing Big Ben chime four o'clock from a London teahouse patio. Their herbal blends read like a garden — the rooibos chai lists rooibos, cinnamon, ginger, star anise, cloves, black pepper and cardamom, all organic, no mysteries. You'll pay specialty prices. You'll also be able to pronounce everything.

And honestly? Red Rose still deserves a place at the table — the Canadian blend is still its own recipe, still all orange pekoe, still the cup this country was raised on. Just know, when you buy it, that the rose is Dutch-owned now, the pity is ours, and the only box in the aisle still answering to a Canadian address is the King from Sussex.

So here's your five-second tea aisle routine from now on. Front of the box: enjoy the fiction — the orange pekoe that isn't a legal grade, the peach that isn't in there, the British lion owned from Mumbai. Back of the box: read the two words that are law, check for the word "artificial," find the "blended in" line if there is one, and do the cents-per-bag math, because the same two-word ingredient list runs from three and a half cents to thirty.
