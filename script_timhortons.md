Number nine. The Franchise Price Lottery.

On the eighteenth of September, at sixteen minutes past seven in the evening Eastern, we pulled the menu price for one item — a large Original Blend coffee — from six Tim Hortons restaurants across the country, in the same minute, from Tim Hortons' own ordering system.

Toronto, Queen Street West: two fourteen. Mississauga, Courtney Park Drive: one ninety-seven. Those two stores are about twenty-five kilometres apart. Seventeen cents.

Halifax, two fifteen. Calgary, two fourteen. Winnipeg, two fourteen. Vancouver, two twenty-one — the highest in the country on every single line we checked.

Now go to the dozen donuts. Toronto, fourteen forty-nine. Mississauga, eleven ninety-nine. Same province, same company, same minute, two dollars and fifty cents apart. A Boston cream is a dollar seventy-nine downtown and a dollar forty-nine in Mississauga.

Here is why, and it is not a scandal. Ninety-nine point three per cent of Tim Hortons restaurants in Canada and the United States are franchised. Out of four thousand five hundred and eighty-six, Restaurant Brands International operates thirty-one itself. Thirty-one. Everything else belongs to an independent owner, and those owners set their own prices.

So there is no national Tim Hortons price. There never was. The advertising is national, the cup is national, the brand is national — and the number on the board is whatever the person who owns that particular building decided it should be this week. Nobody tells you that, and it is the single most useful thing in this video for your actual wallet: the store you drive past might be cheaper than the one you stop at.

Number eight. The Delivery Price Book. This is the one nobody has reported.

Same store. Same timestamp. Same system. We changed one thing — from pickup to delivery — and pulled the menu again.

The medium coffee at Queen Street West goes from one eighty-three to two twenty-nine. That is up twenty-five point one per cent. The large goes from two fourteen to two fifty-nine. A single donut goes from a dollar seventy-nine to two oh nine. The dozen goes from fourteen forty-nine to seventeen thirty-nine — twenty per cent.

And I need you to understand exactly what that is, because it is easy to hear it wrong. That is not a delivery fee. The delivery fee is three ninety-nine and it is charged on top of this. That is not tax. That is not a tip. That is not Uber Eats or DoorDash or Skip taking their cut. This is Tim Hortons' own first-party app, and the menu price itself is different before a single fee is added.

At the Mississauga store it is a flat ten per cent across the entire menu instead of twenty-five. So it is not one national policy either. It varies.

Now, the fair part, and it matters. Delivery genuinely costs money to run, and a business is entitled to price it accordingly. Plenty of chains do exactly this. Nobody is hiding it from investors. The question is narrower: does the customer standing in their kitchen at nine at night, tapping through the company's own app, understand that the coffee got more expensive before the delivery fee was even added?

I pulled the app's own code to check. There is a line in there that reads, and I am quoting the software: "Menu prices higher on delivery. Terms and fees apply." That line is tagged for the United States. The two Canadian lines I found disclose the three ninety-nine fee and the nine dollar minimum order.

And I am going to stop exactly there, because I cannot prove a negative from a code file. There may well be a disclosure somewhere in the Canadian checkout that I did not trigger. So I am not going to tell you Tim Hortons hides this from Canadians. I am going to tell you what we found, and then I am going to open the app on camera in this video and show you the actual checkout screen, and you can decide for yourself what you were told.

Number seven. The Cup That Changed Its Name.

In January of 2012, Tim Hortons did something that a lot of Canadians still describe, wrongly, as shrinking the cups.

Here is what actually happened. They added a new twenty-four ounce cup at the top and called it Extra Large. Then every existing size moved down one name. The old Extra Large, twenty ounces, became the Large. The old Large, fourteen ounces, became the Medium. The old Medium became the Small, and the old Small became an Extra Small.

Nothing shrank. A fourteen ounce cup was still fourteen ounces the next morning. The volumes did not change at all — only the word printed on the side.

But follow what that does to a person who has ordered the same thing for twenty years. Somebody who walked in and said "large" the way you say your own name went from getting fourteen ounces to getting twenty ounces, and paying for twenty, unless they noticed and changed their order. That is not fraud. Nobody was short-measured. CBC actually went and tested the cups afterwards and confirmed the sizes were exactly what Tim Hortons said they were.

It is just that a brand built entirely on habit quietly changed the meaning of the one word its customers use most, and let habit do the rest.

Number six. The Seven-To-Twenty Switch.

In March of 2019, Tim Hortons launched Tims Rewards, and it was genuinely simple: buy something on seven separate visits, get a free coffee or a free baked good. Seven visits. That was it. The company put out a full corporate press release announcing it.

On the twenty-first of February, 2023, one second after midnight Eastern, that changed. The program moved to points. Ten points for every dollar you spend. And the free coffee went from seventy points to four hundred points.

Do that arithmetic. Seventy points used to be about twelve dollars of spending. Four hundred points is about forty. In visits, you went from roughly seven coffees to earn a free one, to roughly twenty.

So how was that announced? It was not. There was no press release. The change was posted as a notice at the bottom of the app and the website.

That is the beat, and it is a small one, but it tells you something. When the program got more generous, Canadians got a press release. When it got roughly three times harder, Canadians got a footer. And I want to be careful here, because a company is entirely within its rights to change the terms of a loyalty program it paid for. Nobody is owed free coffee. But there is a difference between changing your terms and hoping nobody does the multiplication.

Number five. The Always Fresh Question. And this is the one your parents will argue with you about.

Here is the date, and I have not seen anybody get this right. Late 2003. That is when it happened. The plant was Maidstone Bakeries in Brantford, Ontario, built as a fifty-fifty joint venture Tim Hortons entered into in 2001. From then on, the donuts stopped being made from scratch in each restaurant overnight and started arriving partially baked and flash frozen, to be finished in the store in the morning.

I went through about five thousand comments on other people's Tim Hortons videos while making this. People date the end of in-store baking to the late nineties, to 2002, to 2010, to 2014. Nobody had it right. Late 2003. Brantford, Ontario.

Here is the part that will actually surprise you. Tim Hortons does not own that bakery. In August of 2010 they sold their half of it to Aryzta, a Swiss company, for four hundred and seventy-five million dollars in cash, on an investment of about seventy-five million. They have not owned the place that makes the donuts for sixteen years.

The franchisees sued over the switch. The claim was reported at two billion dollars. And I want to be completely straight with you about how that ended, because it matters: the court dismissed the entire action on summary judgment in 2012, the Ontario Court of Appeal upheld the dismissal, and the Supreme Court of Canada refused to hear it in 2013. The franchisees lost, at every level. Any cost figure you have seen quoted from that case is an allegation that failed.

The judge, Justice Strathy, wrote something worth reading out. He said that in order to keep a franchise system healthy and competitive, the franchisor "must be permitted to introduce new products, new methods of production or sale, and new techniques." He is right, and that is the law.

So nothing here is illegal. Par-baking is standard across the industry. The question is narrower and I think more interesting than the one the internet asks. The brand's promise is a single word — fresh — and that word has been doing a different job since 2003 than it did in 1964. Nobody has ever been required to explain that at the counter. And nobody has.

That is five of nine, which is halfway, so if you have made it this far, put the number five in the comments. It is the only thing I ask for in the middle of a video and it genuinely tells me whether the middle is holding.

Number four. The Landlord You Didn't Know You Had.

Everybody knows Tim Hortons restaurants are franchised. Almost nobody knows what that actually means here, and it is in Restaurant Brands International's own annual filing, in a table anybody can read.

Of four thousand five hundred and fifty-five franchised Tim Hortons sites, the company owns seven hundred and sixty-five outright and leases and subleases another two thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine to franchisees. Add those up. Three thousand five hundred and thirty-four out of four thousand five hundred and fifty-five. Seventy-seven point six per cent.

So for more than three quarters of Tim Hortons restaurants, the company you pay royalties to is also your landlord.

And the filing spells out the terms. Royalties typically run three to six per cent of gross sales, and the filing says that rate depends in part on whether the company owns or subleases the property to that franchisee. Advertising fund contributions are mandatory and run two to five per cent of gross sales. And rent, where the company is the landlord, is typically eight and a half to ten per cent of monthly gross sales.

Stack the ranges. That is somewhere between thirteen and a half and twenty-one per cent of everything that goes through the till, before the owner has bought a single coffee bean or paid a single person.

And then there is the part that genuinely surprised me. The biggest line in Tim Hortons' revenue is not royalties. It is supply chain sales — the company selling coffee and food and supplies to its own franchisees. In the last full year that was two point nine billion dollars out of four point two billion. Sixty-nine per cent. Royalties and property were under a quarter.

None of that is hidden and none of it is unusual for a franchise system. But it reframes the business. When you buy a coffee, most of what flows back up to head office does not flow as a cut of your coffee. It flows as the wholesale price of everything the owner had to buy from head office in order to sell it to you.

Number three. The Quiet Quarter.

For years the only Tim Hortons story anyone could tell you was that it was untouchable. Then you read the filings.

Canadian comparable sales at Tim Hortons in the second quarter of 2025: up three point six per cent. Same measure, same quarter, this year: up nought point one per cent. Essentially flat.

And here is the detail I would not have believed if I had not opened the document myself. In Restaurant Brands International's own results release for that quarter, the chief executive gives a quote about the company's performance. He names Burger King. He names the International division. Tim Hortons — forty-five per cent of the company's revenue — is not mentioned in it at all.

On the earnings call, asked about Canada, the chief executive said, and I am quoting him: "our calendar didn't drive the growth we've come to expect from Tims." That is the company attributing the slowdown to its own marketing calendar, not to Canadians. Take them at their word on that.

Now the honesty, because this is where most videos on this subject fall over. Tim Hortons Canada grew two point eight per cent across the whole of last year. The company is opening around eighty new Canadian restaurants this year against about fifty last year, and renovating roughly four hundred more. Its chief operating officer pointed out that the restaurant count has been basically static since 2019 while Canada's population grew about ten per cent.

So if you came here for the collapse of Tim Hortons, it is not in the numbers, and anybody telling you otherwise has not read them. The lineups are real. What changed is the direction of travel in one market, and the fact that the people at the top stopped leading with it.

If you are finding this useful, subscribing genuinely helps, because this is the kind of video where I have to read four hundred pages of filings to get you nine facts.

Number two. The Sixty-Four Cent Cup.

This is where Canadians have actually gone, and it is not where you think. It is not Starbucks — Starbucks has been closing stores. It is not Second Cup, whose international division filed for creditor protection in May of last year. Canadians did not switch chains. They went home.

The Coffee Association of Canada surveyed this. Seventy-one per cent of Canadians drank a coffee yesterday. Eighty per cent have noticed coffee prices rising. More than half say they are cutting back on non-essential spending.

So here is the arithmetic, and this is our calculation, not anybody else's, and I will put the working on screen. Statistics Canada publishes the average price of a three hundred and forty gram bag of roasted or ground coffee in this country. As of July, that is nine dollars and fifty-four cents. A Tim Hortons medium is fourteen ounces, which is four hundred and fourteen millilitres. At standard brewing strength that needs about twenty-three grams of ground coffee. Twenty-three grams out of a nine fifty-four bag comes to sixty-four cents. That bag makes about fifteen mediums.

Sixty-four cents of coffee, against — well, go and look at your own receipt, because as we established at number nine there is no national price.

And here is the part I insist on saying out loud, because leaving it out would make me exactly the kind of creator I am trying not to be. That sixty-four cents is only the coffee. The two dollars and change buys the cup, the lid, the person who handed it to you, the rent on that corner, the power, the drive-thru, and the fact that you did not have to do anything at all. That is not a rip-off. That is a service, and services cost money. The only question worth asking is whether the gap has moved, and which way.

And on that question specifically, Tim Hortons has a better case than it gets credit for. In October of last year they raised coffee prices for the first time in three years, by about three cents a cup. That is roughly one and a half per cent against about seven per cent of inflation over the same three years, while the wholesale price of green coffee went from about a dollar fifty-eight a pound to three dollars ninety. Two Canadian university economists went on the record saying that was defensible. They are right. Whatever has gone wrong here, gouging you on the price of a coffee is not it.

Number one. The App That Followed You Home.

On the first of June, 2022, the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada, together with the Commission d'accès à l'information du Québec and the privacy commissioners of Alberta and British Columbia, published the findings of a joint investigation into the Tim Hortons app. The file number is PIPEDA Findings 2022-001. You can read the whole thing tonight.

They found that from May of 2019, the app used a third-party service to track and collect the location of users' devices. And I want to read you their own words, because mine are not as good.

The regulators found that the app would, quote, collect and process the user's device location, as often as every few minutes, to infer the location of a user's home and place of work, and when they were travelling, and to identify when the user was visiting a Tim Hortons competitor.

As often as every few minutes. Mostly while the app was closed.

The report records the journalist whose reporting triggered the investigation finding that the app had logged his exact latitude and longitude more than two thousand seven hundred times in under five months — including while he was in the Netherlands and in North Africa, where Tim Hortons does not operate.

The regulators found the company contravened the federal privacy act and the privacy statutes of all three provinces. They found Tim Hortons had told users, in its own permission screens and its own frequently asked questions, that location would only be collected while the app was open — and the regulators described those as misleading statements, not consistent with how the app actually worked.

The Privacy Commissioner of Canada at the time, Daniel Therrien, said this: "Following people's movements every few minutes of every day was clearly an inappropriate form of surveillance."

Now. Three things, in fairness, and all three are as true as everything above.

There was no fine. There was no penalty and no order. Canada's privacy law at the time did not give the Commissioner the power to impose one. The regulators made recommendations, and the disposition they recorded, in their own words, was "well-founded and conditionally resolved."

Second, Tim Hortons stopped. It permanently ceased collecting that data in August of 2020, after being notified of the investigation, and removed the software from the app the following month. The four class actions that followed were settled nationally in September of 2022 — a free hot drink and a free baked good for every member of the class, which the court valued at about sixteen point two million dollars. In that same judgment, the court recorded that the company contested the regulator's finding on consent.

And third, a year later, in June of 2023, the Privacy Commissioner said publicly that it was satisfied the company had met its commitments. That file is closed, and leaving that out would misrepresent where things stand today.

But it happened. A federal regulator and three provincial ones looked at an app that eight and a half million Canadians had downloaded to buy a coffee, and concluded it had been following them home.

So — what do you actually do with all this.

Three practical things. One: check the board at more than one location, because there is no national price and we measured a seventeen cent gap between two stores half an hour apart. Two: order pickup, not delivery, because on the company's own app the menu price itself can be twenty-five per cent higher before the three ninety-nine fee. Three: if you use a reusable cup, it is ten cents off every size, every time, and that is the only discount in this entire video that requires nothing from you but remembering.

And now the part where fairness cuts both ways, because it has to.

Three things Tim Hortons genuinely does well, and I mean genuinely. First, the Foundation Camps. Founded in 1974, in Tim Horton's own memory, after he died. The company says it has now sent nearly three hundred and fifty thousand young people to camp at no cost to their families, and raised two hundred and seventy-five million dollars through Camp Day since it started — thirteen million this year alone, from restaurant owners handing over every cent of their coffee sales for the day. That is real and it is not marketing.

Second, the Smile Cookie. Thirty-five point six million dollars raised last year, over a hundred and fifty million since 1996, supporting more than twelve hundred local organisations. And here is the detail almost nobody knows: the charity is chosen by the person who owns your local store. Not head office. The hospital or the food bank that gets your five dollars was picked by somebody who lives in your town.

Third, the sheer scale of what it still is. Roughly four thousand restaurants, over a hundred thousand employees, about forty-five per cent of them between fifteen and twenty-four years old, which makes it one of the largest employers of young Canadians in the country. Three hundred and seventy thousand kids played Timbits hockey and soccer last year. There is no other private company in Canada that shows up in that many small towns.

None of that cancels the app. The app does not cancel any of that either. Both are true, and any video that tells you only one of them is selling you something.

So here is what I want from you, and it is not an opinion.

Go and find your oldest Tim Hortons receipt. A photo, an old bank statement line, anything with a date on it. Post the date, the town, the province, and what a medium coffee cost you then. I am going to put every single one of them on a chart, and we are going to work out together exactly when the price left the country behind.

And if I have got anything wrong here, post it with a source and I will pin the correction at the top. That offer is open and I mean it.
