Number six, the gummy brands — Vitafusion and L'il Critters, both owned by Church & Dwight, plus the pastel Instagram bottles like Olly, which, by the way, is Unilever. Gummies are now how millions of Canadians take vitamins, and the format has three problems the marketing never mentions. First: sugar — typically two to three grams per serving, taken every single day. Second: potency. Gummy nutrients degrade from the moment they're made — heat, moisture, time — so manufacturers deliberately overfill them, twenty to fifty percent above the label, hoping they decay down to the stated dose. When American testing in 2023 checked gummy multivitamins, every single one tested was off its label — one low, three at nearly double the stated folic acid. Same brands, American testing — but the same physics applies to the gummy in your cupboard, because a gummy's shelf life is one to two years against three to five for a tablet. Third: the format itself. In April 2021, Health Canada recalled lots of L'il Critters and Vitafusion gummies in Canada because a filter screen failed at the factory and stainless steel wire fragments — up to twenty-five millimetres, an inch of wire — could be in the gummies. Children's gummies. And the candy problem is measurable in Canada now: the Ontario Poison Centre's calls about children five and under swallowing too much melatonin went from 160 in 2017 to 438 in 2021 — rising every single year. Manitoba's centre went from 19 to 64. Nobody's toddler eats a handful of tablets. Gummies, they eat. There's even a Canadian recall that captures the whole gummy era in one notice: in November 2023, Health Canada recalled every Herbaland gummy vitamin sold loose in bulk-refill stores — scooped into your own jar like trail mix — because sold that way, there's no label at all: no dose, no ingredients, no warnings. The agency flagged the risk as most serious for children and pregnant people. A supplement had literally become bulk candy, and it took a federal recall to say so.

And now the worst one on this list. It isn't a brand. It's the number printed on every bottle we just talked about. In 2015, CBC's Marketplace decided to test what that eight-digit licence is actually worth. They invented a children's remedy from nothing — called it Nighton, which is an anagram of "nothing" — and applied to Health Canada for a licence, claiming it treated fever, pain and inflammation in children and infants. As evidence, they submitted photocopied pages from a homeopathy book published in 1902. Health Canada issued the licence. A government number, the same class of number your pharmacy uses to decide what goes on the shelf, granted to a product that did not exist, for use in babies, on the strength of a century-old book. That was the system working as designed — because for traditional and homeopathic claims, the bar can be as low as two old references. And if you think that was a one-off embarrassment that got fixed, here's what the federal Auditor General's office found when it audited the program in 2021. Of seventy-five licensed products it sampled, eighty-eight percent were being advertised with misleading information, and fifty-six percent had misleading information on the label itself — wrong doses, missing risk warnings, safety text printed in four-point font. Health Canada had issued more than ninety-one thousand licences — and by its own estimate, only about half of those products ever reached a store, because for years companies could file applications for hypothetical products that didn't exist yet, and pay no fee to do it. Applications ran ten times higher than for over-the-counter drugs. Fewer than five percent of licence holders had even told Health Canada which factory made their products — the regulator did not know where most of the products it licensed were manufactured. Meanwhile it inspected about six percent of the licensed companies over three years — and the inspections that did happen found quality problems at every single site visited. Factories weren't inspected before products went to market; they signed an attestation. A promise. Drug factories get inspected first; vitamin factories sign a form. When Health Canada finally ran a pilot inspection program after the audit, it inspected thirty-six sites — and all thirty-six had compliance issues, with more than forty percent serious enough to threaten their licences. Between 2021 and 2023 alone there were a hundred voluntary recalls of licensed products, for problems including bacterial contamination and foreign matter such as fibreglass. And the audit's darkest exhibit: a product linked to a hospitalization, contaminated with pharmaceutical ingredients that could cause miscarriages and birth defects, was still for sale on the company's Canadian website three and a half years after the first report — after a seizure, a recall request, a border alert and a cancelled licence. Because until 2023 — this is the line to remember — Health Canada had no legal power to force a recall of any supplement, for any reason, even one it believed was hurting people. The maximum fine was five thousand dollars. In 2023, a budget bill finally extended Vanessa's Law to supplements: mandatory recall powers, and fines of up to five million dollars a day. The industry's answer was a campaign called Save Our Supplements, about a million postcards to MPs, and a bill to repeal those new powers — a bill that died when Parliament prorogued and was immediately reintroduced. It passed second reading in March 2026. It is sitting in the House health committee right now. And if you want to know what's still out there right now: when Health Canada pointed an artificial-intelligence tool at supplement marketing to look for cancer claims — claims no supplement is licensed to make — it flagged over three thousand eight hundred products, and sixty-three percent of them were carrying non-compliant cancer claims. So when someone tells you Canada regulates supplements more strictly than the United States — they're right, on paper. The FDA itself states it does not approve supplements before sale, and Canada does license them. But what we license on can be a photocopy from 1902, what we inspect is six percent, and the power to pull a dangerous product off the shelf is three years old and currently up for repeal. A stricter gate, with a near-empty guardhouse behind it.

So what's actually worth buying? Two purchases, and one honest sentence that beats both. First: single-ingredient basics in the right form, from the value end of the shelf. That's also why Canada legally fortifies milk with vitamin D, at levels that roughly doubled as of the end of 2025. Yes, that same Winnipeg factory had lot-specific recalls — check your lot number, which takes ten seconds — but the product itself is the right molecule at the right dose at a price nobody else touches. In the same category: Webber Naturals B12 as methylcobalamin — the already-active form, from a company that's genuinely Canadian-owned and manufacturing in BC since 1948, around fourteen dollars — and CanPrev's magnesium bisglycinate, an Ontario company selling the absorbable chelated form instead of the four-percent oxide. Notice the pattern: the winners are single ingredients, in the named superior form, where you can read the label in five seconds and there's nothing to hide. Second, for people who genuinely want a multivitamin: pick one carrying a real third-party mark — NSF certification, like Thorne's line, which ships to Canada — because "third-party tested," "lab tested" and "pharmaceutical grade" have no legal definition in this country. None. Anyone can print them. A named certification with a published standard is the only version of that promise you can check. While you're reading that label, two warnings are worth knowing, because they're mandatory in Canada and they tell you the risks are real: any package with more than 250 milligrams of total iron must carry an octagonal warning that reads "There is enough iron in this package to seriously harm a child" — that's regulation, not caution. And since Health Canada's safety review linked vitamin B6 to nerve damage at surprisingly ordinary doses, any product delivering ten milligrams or more of B6 per day must warn you to stop if you feel numbness or tingling. Check your B-complex tonight — a lot of them clear ten milligrams several times over. And the honest sentence that beats both purchases? It's from Canada's own dietary guidelines, and it's the sentence this entire industry is built on hoping you never read: "When eating a variety of nutritious foods, vitamin and mineral supplements are not generally required." The official Canadian exceptions are short: vitamin D for older adults. Folic acid, 400 micrograms, for anyone who could become pregnant. Specific cases your doctor identifies — a measured deficiency, a restricted diet. That's the list.
