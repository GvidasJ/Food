# SCRIPT — Bottled Water

TITLE: Canadians Must AVOID These 8 "Pure" Water Brands (Only 3 Are ACTUALLY Worth Buying)

ALTERNATES:
- Canadians Must AVOID These 8 "Spring" Water Brands (3 That Are ACTUALLY Real Springs)
- Your Bottled Water Is City Tap Water — We Checked Every Canadian Label

---

That bottle of water in your fridge has words like "pure" and "natural" printed on the front, mountains on the label, and a price that can run nine hundred times what the same litre costs from your kitchen tap. Here's what almost nobody knows: in Canada, only two words on a water bottle actually have legal teeth. "Spring" and "mineral." By federal regulation, water sold under those words must come from a real underground source — not from a city water system — and the label must print the actual location of that source. Every other word — pure, fresh, glacial, smart — is art direction. So I went through every big bottled water brand on Canadian shelves and read them the way the law reads them. What I found: two of the biggest sellers in the country are city tap water, re-filtered — and their own labels admit it, in words most people have never been taught to decode. The most famous water brand in Canada hasn't been owned by the company on the label since 2021. Quebec's proudest spring water belongs to a Wall Street bank. The trendy alkaline water in the carton went into receivership last September. And the raw water inside a two-dollar bottle cost the company that pumped it about one fortieth of a cent — I'll show you the government price list. One in five Canadian households drinks mainly bottled water, so this is not a small story. Eight brands where the label, the owner, or the price doesn't match the promise — in order, starting with the biggest seller in the country — then the three that are actually worth your money. And the best brand on this list ends up owning the exact wells the worst story on this list walked away from. You'll see. Let's get into it.

Number one — Pure Life. The green label. This is the biggest bottled water brand in Canada, the default case at every barbecue, and for twenty years it had one of the most trusted names in food printed above it: Nestlé. Flip the bottle: "100% Natural Spring Water." And to be completely fair — that claim holds. Pure Life in Canada is real spring water under the legal definition, from named springs, historically two of them: Aberfoyle, Ontario, and Hope, British Columbia. Here's what the front of the label no longer tells you. Nestlé is gone. In 2021, Nestlé sold its entire North American water business — Pure Life included — to two American private equity firms for four point three billion US dollars. The company became "BlueTriton," and in November 2024 it merged again into something called Primo Brands. Go to the Canadian Pure Life website today and scroll to the fine print: the copyright line reads "Triton Water Canada Holdings." The Swiss company you trusted left the building four years ago; the label kept the aura. Then, this January, it got stranger. The Aberfoyle plant — the most famous, most fought-over water bottling site in Canada — stopped bottling entirely on January 31st, 2025, with about two hundred jobs affected. The company's own announcement says the Hope, BC plant "will serve the Western provinces." Which leaves a question the website doesn't answer: if you're buying Pure Life in Ontario or Quebec today, where is that water actually coming from? The springs page says "carefully selected and monitored springs" — and no longer names them. A spring water brand whose legal advantage is a named source, no longer telling you the source's name. Hold onto Aberfoyle, because those wells didn't die — and where they ended up is the best story in this video, saved for the end. Next: the biggest brand that never claimed a spring at all.

Number two — Dasani. The blue bottle. Coca-Cola's water, everywhere from vending machines to hockey rinks, priced well above the store-brand case one shelf down. Now read the Canadian label — not the front, the legal name and the ingredient list, because Dasani has one of the few water labels in the country with an actual ingredient list. The product's name on Canadian retail listings: "Remineralized Water." The ingredients: "Filtered demineralized water, magnesium sulphate, potassium chloride, salt, ozone." Decode that, word by word. "Demineralized water" — under Health Canada's rules, that name means the water was treated to strip its dissolved minerals down to almost nothing. It is the legal opposite of spring water. Where does it start? Coca-Cola describes Dasani as sourced from local water supplies — municipal systems, treated by reverse osmosis. City water, filtered hard. Then "remineralized": after stripping everything out, they add minerals back — and look at which ones. Magnesium sulphate is what your pharmacy sells as Epsom salt. Potassium chloride is a salt substitute. And salt is salt. The label says these are added — and this is the phrase to remember — "for taste." Not for nutrition. Completely demineralized water tastes flat, so a pinch of mineral seasoning goes back in, like seasoning a soup. To be fair: all of this is legal, disclosed, and safe — Health Canada holds bottled water to the same safety expectations as tap. The issue is arithmetic. At a recent Superstore price, Dasani works out to around a dollar sixty a litre — roughly three hundred times the price of the Toronto tap water that costs about half a cent a litre — for water that began its life in a municipal pipe just like yours. The brand next to it made the same admission in writing — nineteen years ago.

Number three — Aquafina. Pepsi's water, the other half of the cola-water duopoly. Here I don't even need the ingredient list, because on Canadian grocery listings the product's legal name does all the work: "Aquafina Demineralized Treated Water." That is the actual name of the product. Demineralized — stripped-down treated water, not spring. And Aquafina's origin story is the most famous label correction in bottled water history. Back in 2007, Aquafina labels carried the cryptic letters "P.W.S." A consumer group — Corporate Accountability International — spent years pushing Pepsi to admit what the initials meant, and in July 2007 PepsiCo agreed to spell it out on the label: "Public Water Source." The company's own spokesperson said at the time, quote, "If this helps clarify the fact that the water originates from public sources, then it's a reasonable thing to do." And here's the Canadian detail from CBC's reporting: Canadian Aquafina labels disclosed sources like — quote — "water from the public distribution of Mississauga, Ontario," and a public source in Vancouver. The public distribution of Mississauga. That is the municipal system. The same network of pipes that runs under the houses of everyone watching this from Peel Region — bottled, branded blue, and sold back at hundreds of times the metered price. To be fair to both colas: they never lied. It's printed right there. The scandal was never the label — it's that the label was written in a language nobody taught us to read, underneath a picture of mountains that the water has never seen. Now you know the two magic words the law reserves for the real thing — and the confession words: demineralized, distilled, treated, remineralized. If this video has already changed how you'll read the water aisle, a quick like genuinely helps more Canadians learn the difference. Because the next brand IS real spring water — and the surprise is who owns it.

Number four — Eska. If you're in Quebec, this one might sting a little. Eska is Quebec's water — drawn from the Saint-Mathieu–Lac-Berry esker near Amos, in Abitibi. An esker is a glacial gravel ridge that filters water over decades, and Eska's water is genuinely exceptional: a real named source, printed on the label with its mineral content and fluoride level, exactly as the regulation demands, bottled right at the source. Every part of the natural story checks out. The corporate story is another matter. Eska is not owned by a Quebec family, or a Quebec company, or any Canadian company. Eska's parent has been owned since 2005 — when it was purchased for eighteen million dollars out of insolvency proceedings — by Morgan Stanley Strategic Investments. The Wall Street bank. Quebec's glacial esker water, the bottle that reads like terroir, sends its profits to an investment bank in New York. And one small asterisk for the record: in July 2020, Eska voluntarily recalled a batch of its four-litre bottles after pieces of plastic were found — a Class 3 recall, the lowest-risk category, handled through the normal CFIA process. The water is real. The label is honest. It's the ownership that's a plot twist — and it's about to become a theme, because the next bottle costs seven times more than Eska and won't even tell you where the water comes from.

Number five — Smartwater. The tall bottle. Coca-Cola bought the Glacéau brands in 2007, and Smartwater has become the default "premium" water at every checkout — at Superstore recently, three dollars and seventy-nine cents for a single litre. That is around seven hundred and eighty times the price of tap. So what does the label legally declare for that money? Read it: "Vapour distilled water, calcium chloride, magnesium chloride, potassium bicarbonate, ozone." Distilled water. Boiled into vapour, re-condensed — a process the marketing describes as "inspired by the clouds" — which by definition strips out everything, minerals included. Then, exactly like Dasani, minerals are added back, and the label uses the same two words: "for taste." Here's the question I couldn't answer, because the company doesn't say: distilled from what? Coca-Cola Canada's own product page describes the cloud-inspired process in loving detail and never discloses what water goes into the still. Spring water tells you its address by law. Smartwater's starting point is simply not on the label. To be fair, distillation is a genuine purification process and the product is perfectly safe. But you are paying spring-water prices — beyond spring-water prices — for treated water with a mineral seasoning packet stirred back in, from an undisclosed source. "Smart" is the brand. The label quietly tells you which side of the transaction the smart is on. And if you think that's a premium, wait until the water starts crossing oceans.

Number six — Evian. The French one. The pink-and-blue label, the water of spas and hotel minibars, and — credit where due — absolutely real natural spring water under Canadian law: the source is the Cachat spring system in Évian-les-Bains, and the marketing tells you the water spends, quote, a "15 year journey through the French Alps." Beautiful. Now do the geography. That fifteen-year journey through the Alps ends with a six-thousand-kilometre journey across the Atlantic Ocean, because every bottle of Evian on a Canadian shelf was bottled in France and shipped here. At Metro, a one-and-a-half-litre bottle runs about four dollars — roughly two dollars sixty-six a litre, call it five hundred and thirty times tap. And here is the fact that reframes the whole aisle: under the same Canadian regulation, Eska's esker water, Naya's Laurentian spring — even Costco's house-brand spring water — hold the exact same legal status as Evian. "Spring water" is "spring water." The Alps are not on the certificate. They're on the sticker. You're not paying for a purer category of water; you're paying for a postcard and a boat ride. And even Evian isn't the top of the price ladder. That title crosses the Pacific.

Number seven — Fiji. The square bottle. "Natural Artesian Water" from the Yaqara aquifer on the island of Viti Levu — owned not by Fijians but by The Wonderful Company, the Los Angeles conglomerate of the Resnick family. Again, to be fair: legally, it's the real thing — an actual underground artesian source, which is why the label can say so. But Fiji is where the price ladder reaches its top rung. Depending on the store, Fiji runs as high as four dollars and forty-nine cents a litre in Canada. Against Toronto tap at about half a cent, that is roughly nine hundred times the price — for water that travelled more than eleven thousand kilometres to reach your cart. Nine hundred times. Not because it's nine hundred times cleaner — Health Canada applies the same safety expectations to every bottle in the aisle, and to your tap. The premium buys distance, a square bottle, and a hibiscus flower. And now, the arithmetic that puts this whole aisle in perspective — what the companies themselves pay for water. In Ontario, a bottler pays the province five hundred three dollars and seventy-one cents per million litres. Work that out: the raw water in a half-litre bottle costs about one fortieth of a cent. In British Columbia it's more staggering: two dollars and twenty-five cents per million litres — a rate set in 2016 and never raised — which means about a dollar's worth of raw water fills nearly nine hundred thousand bottles. Everything above that fortieth of a cent is treatment, plastic, trucking, refrigeration, and story. One more avoid entry — the brand that took the story to its logical extreme, and then ran out of road.

Number eight — Flow. The carton one. Canadian, from an artesian spring in South Bruce County, Ontario — "naturally alkaline spring water, pH 8.1," in a plant-based Tetra Pak, at two dollars and fifty cents to three dollars a litre. Flow was the future of water: celebrity investors, a public listing, an Aurora, Ontario plant built to fill half a million cartons a day. About that word — "alkaline." Here is the entire Canadian regulatory position: a pH number may be printed on a label if it's factual. That's it. No Canadian authority — not Health Canada, not the CFIA — recognizes any health benefit from alkaline water. The CFIA's labelling guidance even gives an example of the kind of vague alkaline wordplay it considers misleading. The pH is real chemistry; the promises built on it are marketing. And the market delivered its own verdict. On September 4th, 2025, Flow was placed into receivership by the Ontario Superior Court, owing creditors roughly seventy-one million dollars after three failed attempts to sell the business. Weeks before I wrote this, one of its flavoured waters was recalled for mould — again, Class 3, low risk, properly handled. But the arc is the lesson: premium water at five hundred times tap, sold on a molecule story the regulator never endorsed, could not make the math work. Bankruptcy is what happens when the label's promise and the product's reality finally meet the customer's arithmetic. So who actually deserves your money? Three answers — counted down to the best story in the Canadian water aisle.

Best option number three — Kirkland Signature, and honestly, every store-brand case beside it. Here is the twist this entire video has been building toward, the exact mirror of what we found in the pasta aisle: the cheapest water in the store is the real thing. Costco's Kirkland Signature: forty bottles for around four dollars and twenty-nine cents — about eleven cents a bottle, twenty-one cents a litre. Flip it: "Natural Spring Water." The legal words. Underground source, protected name, mineral and fluoride content printed as the law requires. Walmart's Great Value: about twelve cents a bottle. "Spring Water." President's Choice, Compliments, Selection — spring water, spring water, spring water. Let that land: the two-dollar-a-litre designer bottles are distilled tap with seasoning, while the eleven-cent bottle in the forty-pack is legally protected spring water at forty-three times tap instead of nine hundred. One honest note: the store brands don't name their bottler on the listing, and I won't guess for them — but the "spring water" declaration itself is a legal commitment, not a slogan. If your household runs on cases of water, this shelf is where the math stops being embarrassing.

Best option number two — Naya. The Quebec story with the ending Eska didn't get. Naya is real spring water from Mirabel, at the foot of the Laurentians, bottled at the source — and the label carries two claims almost nobody else can print: "never treated with ozone" and "naturally sodium-free." Most spring waters, including the store brands, at least ozonate; Naya's process is water, filter, bottle. But here's why it takes this spot: the ownership arc. Naya spent two decades passing through foreign hands — Danone of France, then a US private equity firm. In March 2021 — the same year Nestlé sold Canada's biggest water brand to American investors — Naya went the other way: Champlain Financial Corporation and GefCo, Québec investors, bought it back. One hundred percent Québécois-owned, about eighty jobs at the Mirabel source, at forty-something cents a litre — a tenth of Smartwater's price for water that, under the law, out-credentials it. If the ownership line matters to you at the cheese counter and the pasta shelf, it's allowed to matter in the water aisle too.

Best option number one — Ice River. And here's the full-circle ending I promised. Ice River Springs is a family company — the Gott family — from Feversham, Ontario, population a few hundred, bottling real Grey County spring water since 1995. Two things put it at number one. First, the bottle itself: Ice River's green bottles are one hundred percent recycled plastic — and not bought-in recycled plastic. This family built North America's first beverage-company-owned recycling operation, a plant in Shelburne, Ontario that swallows about twenty-nine thousand tonnes of blue-box plastic a year and turns it back into bottles. No other major brand in the aisle closes its own loop. They're also Canada's biggest bottler of private-label water — so there's a decent chance you've been drinking their springs for years without knowing their name. And second — remember Aberfoyle? The famous wells that "Nestlé" walked away from in January 2025? The property sold, for a reported sixty-one million dollars according to land-registry reporting, to a company owned by James and Alexandra Gott — the Ice River family. In October 2025, Ontario renewed the water-taking permits in their name. After twenty-five years of the most bitter water fight in the country — a fight that gave host municipalities a veto over new bottling permits, the strongest local water power in Canada — the wells ended up with a Canadian family that makes its bottles out of your recycling bin. And one more ending worth knowing: the little Middlebrook well near Elora that the community fought hardest over? This April, the township bought it from the water company — for one point eight million dollars — to save it as future drinking water for Fergus and Elora. As one of the local campaigners put it, "Now our water is one hundred per cent safe." The town that spent a decade fighting for its water finally just bought it back. To drink it.

So here's the honest bottom line, because this video is not "bottled water bad." If you're over fifty, there's a good chance your bottled-water habit started in May 2000, when E. coli in the town water of Walkerton, Ontario killed seven people and made twenty-three hundred sick. Losing trust in the tap that year was rational. But know what that tragedy built: the inquiry that followed gave Ontario the Safe Drinking Water Act — licensed operators, mandatory testing, public reports — and big-city tap water is now tested around the clock; Toronto checks its supply every four to six hours. Bottled water plants, according to CBC's reporting, see a federal inspector on average once every three years. And yet — fairness demands this said plainly — Health Canada's own position is that quality standards for bottled and tap water are similar, both are safe, and bottled water in Canada has never caused a recorded disease outbreak. Less watched is not less safe. It's just more expensive. Also true: for some Canadians, this isn't a choice. As of this month, forty First Nations communities remain under long-term drinking water advisories — in parts of this country, the case of bottled water is the water system, and nothing in this video mocks that. And in Brantford, StatCan says forty-four percent of households drink mainly bottled water — the map of bottled water in Canada is a map of who still can't, or can't yet, trust their pipes. For everyone else, here are your four checks, thirty seconds in the aisle. One: find the legal words. "Spring water" or "mineral water" means a real underground source — the law reserves those words. "Demineralized," "distilled," "treated," "remineralized" means processed water, very often municipal. Two: real spring water must print its source's location, minerals, and fluoride content — read the address, that's the law working for you. Three: do the per-litre math — eleven cents a bottle and four dollars a litre are sitting in the same aisle, holding the same legal certificate. Four: ignore every promise attached to pH — printable chemistry, empty promises. And keep a case at home regardless — emergency guidance says two litres per person per day, and as Calgary's burst water main and Iqaluit's fuel scare proved, the day you need stored water, you really need it. Check your fridge, check the label, and send this to someone who's been paying Smartwater prices for city water — because now you both know how to read the two words that actually matter: where it's from, and what they had to call it.
